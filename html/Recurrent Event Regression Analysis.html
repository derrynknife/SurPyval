Recurrent Event Regression Analysis

In the same way that we want to understand the relationship between a single event outcome and a set of covariates, we may want to understand the relationship between a recurrent event process and a set of covariates. For example, we may want to understand the relationship between the number of times an item needs repairing based on the environment, the duty cycle, and the types of inputs it receives. For example, an electric motor in a hot and humid environment may need more repairs than one in a cool and dry environment. Additionally, a motor that is used more often may need more repairs than one that is used less often. Finally, a motor that is used in a factory that produces a lot of dust may need more repairs than one that is used in a cleaner environment. We can use recurrent event regression to understand the relationship between these covariates and the number of repairs that a motor needs.

This page builds on the counting-process ideas — the intensity \(\lambda(t)\), the cumulative intensity \(\Lambda(t)\) and the Poisson-process likelihood — explained on the Recurrent Event Analysis page. Runnable examples of everything here are on the Recurrent Event Regression Modelling with SurPyval page, and the API is documented under Recurrent Event Models.

From a rate to a rate that depends on covariates

We can start with the simplest version of recurrent event regression, a Homogeneous Poisson Process Regression. In this case we have a regular Homogeneous Poisson Process, whose expected number of events by time \(t\) is given by:

\[\Lambda(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[N(t)\right] = \lambda t\]

But we extend this to include the impact that additional factors have on the cumulative count. So the model becomes:

\[\Lambda(t \mid Z) = \phi\left( Z \right) \lambda t\]

Here \(Z\) is the row vector of an item’s covariates (for example \(Z = [\text{humid}, \text{duty cycle}]\)) and \(\phi\) is a positive function of them. In this case, just as was the case with single event proportional hazard models, there is a factor that relates the covariates to the counting function. In doing so we can now model jointly the cumulative event process with factors that are likely to impact the rate at which the events occur. Again, repeating the lessons from single event survival analysis, a logical choice for the phi function would be the exponential function. This is because it ensures there is never a negative number and so will always provide a valid rate even during optimisation. That is, our model will be:

\[\Lambda(t \mid Z) = e^{Z \beta} \lambda t\]

This is the proportional intensity HPP model. The log-linear (exponential) link function \(e^{Z\beta}\) is the standard choice because it guarantees a positive rate regardless of the sign of \(\beta\), mirrors the Cox model for single events, and gives regression coefficients a direct multiplicative interpretation on the rate. Here \(\beta\) is the column vector of regression coefficients, one per covariate, so \(Z\beta = \beta_0 z_0 + \beta_1 z_1 + \dots\). (SurPyval names each coefficient by its column of \(Z\) where the fit has the columns’ names – fit_from_df or a DataFrame Z – and coef_0, coef_1, … in the order of the columns otherwise.)

Time-varying baselines: the Duane example

A constant rate is often too simple: a system under development gets more reliable as its faults are found and fixed, and an ageing one fails more and more often. The Duane process, particularly relevant in reliability engineering, is the classic model of such a trend. It is a non-homogeneous Poisson process (NHPP) whose expected number of events is a power of time; the parameterisation SurPyval uses is

\[\Lambda(t) = b\, t^{\alpha}, \qquad \lambda(t) = \frac{d\Lambda(t)}{dt} = \alpha\, b\, t^{\alpha - 1} .\]

The exponent \(\alpha\) controls the trend — below one the rate of events falls over time (reliability growth, the case Duane studied), above one it rises (wear-out) — and \(b\) is the expected number of events by \(t = 1\). (Many textbooks swap the letters, writing \(\alpha t^{\beta}\); the parameters that SurPyval prints are alpha for the exponent and b for the scale.)

To add covariates we keep this trend as a baseline intensity \(\lambda_0(t)\) and scale it by the same factor as before, exactly as the proportional hazards model scales a baseline hazard:

\[\lambda(t \mid Z) = \lambda_0(t)\, e^{Z\beta} = \alpha\, b\, t^{\alpha - 1} e^{Z\beta} .\]

Every item shares the power-law trend; its covariates only make its events more or less frequent at every age.

The general proportional-intensity model

More generally, any of SurPyval’s counting-process baselines can play the role of \(\lambda_0(t)\). The proportional-intensity model multiplies that baseline by the covariate factor \(e^{Z\beta}\):

\[\lambda(t \mid Z) = \lambda_0(t)\, e^{Z\beta}, \qquad \Lambda(t \mid Z) = \Lambda_0(t)\, e^{Z\beta}.\]

With a constant baseline this is the proportional-intensity HPP; with a power-law (Duane / Crow-AMSAA) or log-linear (Cox-Lewis) baseline it is the proportional-intensity NHPP. Because the covariate factor scales the whole cumulative intensity, the ratio of expected event counts between two covariate settings is the constant \(e^{(Z_2 - Z_1)\beta}\) at every time — the recurrent-event analogue of a hazard ratio [Cook2007r], and the reason the coefficients \(\beta\) read directly as multiplicative effects on the event rate.

The baseline \(\lambda_0(t)\) is the intensity of an item whose covariates are all zero. The covariates change how many events an item has, never the shape of its intensity over time: every item shares the same trend, scaled up or down. Each item’s events still form a Poisson process — given its covariates, an item’s intensity does not depend on its own past events.

Estimation

The parameters are estimated jointly by maximum likelihood [Lawless1987], maximising the NHPP log-likelihood with the covariate-scaled intensity substituted in. For item \(i\) with covariates \(Z_i\), observed from \(s_i\) to \(\tau_i\) with events at \(t_{i1}, t_{i2}, \dots\),

\[\ell(\theta, \beta) = \sum_i \left\{ \sum_j \left[\ln \lambda_0(t_{ij}; \theta) + Z_i\beta\right] - e^{Z_i\beta}\left[\Lambda_0(\tau_i; \theta) - \Lambda_0(s_i; \theta)\right] \right\},\]

where \(\theta\) are the baseline parameters. Exactly as for the models without covariates, the window end \(\tau_i\) is the item’s right-censoring time (time-truncated) or its last event (failure-truncated), the start \(s_i\) is its left-truncation time or zero, and counts of events over inspection intervals enter as Poisson terms. The static, per-item covariates enter through \(e^{Z\beta}\), so an item in a harsher environment simply accumulates events faster. This proportional-intensity framing is standard in the reliability-growth literature [Rigdon2000r].

The baseline parameters and the coefficients are estimated together, and their joint covariance (the inverse observed information) gives standard errors for every parameter and delta-method confidence bounds on \(\Lambda(t \mid Z)\) at any covariate setting.

Covariates that change over time

So far a covariate has been a property of the item. Often it is a property of a period of the item’s life instead: a pump runs at high load in some months and at low load in others, a machine is overhauled part of the way through its life, a vehicle moves to a harsher route. Using one number per item for such a covariate (its first value, or its average) gives the wrong answer: it credits failures to conditions that were not in force when they happened, and it throws away the timing that carries most of the information about the covariate’s effect.

The proportional-intensity model extends to this case with one assumption: the value the covariate has now multiplies the intensity now. In words, while the pump is on high load its failure rate is the baseline rate for its present age times the high-load factor, and when it returns to low load the rate returns to the baseline. The model has no memory of earlier values; if accumulated exposure matters, it has to be supplied as a covariate of its own. With \(Z_i(t)\) the covariates of item \(i\) in force at time \(t\),

\[\lambda_i(t) = \lambda_0(t)\, e^{Z_i(t)\beta} .\]

SurPyval takes covariates that are piecewise constant: item \(i\)’s history is cut into intervals \((a_{ik}, b_{ik}]\), on each of which its covariates hold one value \(Z_{ik}\). Two facts then give the likelihood. The expected number of failures in an interval is the baseline’s expected number over that interval times the factor for the covariates holding then, \(e^{Z_{ik}\beta}\left[\Lambda_0(b_{ik}) - \Lambda_0(a_{ik})\right]\). And each failure contributes the intensity at its time, with the covariates holding then. The log-likelihood is the sum over failures of the log intensity minus the sum over intervals of the expected counts:

\[\ell(\theta, \beta) = \sum_i \left\{ \sum_j \left[\ln \lambda_0(t_{ij}; \theta) + Z_i(t_{ij})\beta\right] - \sum_k e^{Z_{ik}\beta}\left[\Lambda_0(b_{ik}; \theta) - \Lambda_0(a_{ik}; \theta)\right] \right\} .\]

When every item has a single covariate value this is exactly the likelihood of the previous section, so the two fits agree; and cutting an interval in two with the same covariates on both pieces changes nothing, because the two expected counts add up to the original one. An item need not be watched from time zero (its first interval can start later: delayed entry) or without a break (a gap between intervals is time it was not at risk). Failures that were only counted over an interval, \(n\) of them somewhere in \((a, b]\), enter as the Poisson probability of that count, whose mean is the interval’s expected count.

A prediction now depends on the whole schedule of covariates, not on one value. The expected number of failures by time \(t\) for an item that follows a schedule with segments \((a_k, b_k]\) and values \(z_k\) adds up the same pieces,

\[\Lambda\bigl(t \mid Z(\cdot)\bigr) = \sum_k e^{z_k\beta} \left[\Lambda_0(\min(t, b_k)) - \Lambda_0(a_k)\right] \quad \text{over the segments with } a_k < t,\]

and the intensity at \(t\) is \(\lambda_0(t)\, e^{Z(t)\beta}\). Its confidence bounds come from the same delta method, on the log scale, as those at a fixed covariate setting. The number of failures an item actually has over a period is Poisson distributed about the expected number, as before. The worked example is on the Recurrent Event Regression Modelling with SurPyval page.

Several event types, each with its own effects

When the events come in several mutually exclusive types (the failure modes of a machine), each type can be given its own baseline and its own coefficients. In words: the rate of type \(k\) events on an item is that type’s baseline rate at the item’s age, times that type’s own factor for the item’s covariates. A covariate can then raise one type’s rate and leave another’s alone. For \(K\) types,

\[\lambda_k(t \mid Z) = \lambda_{0k}(t; \theta_k)\, e^{Z\beta_k}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K .\]

If the types arrive as independent Poisson processes (an event of one type neither hastens nor delays an event of another), the log-likelihood is a sum of \(K\) terms, the \(k\)-th involving only \(\theta_k\) and \(\beta_k\):

\[\ell = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \ell_k(\theta_k, \beta_k),\]

where \(\ell_k\) is the proportional-intensity log-likelihood above for the events of type \(k\) alone, over the whole time each item was observed. So each type is estimated by fitting the ordinary model to its own events, with the other types’ events left out (not censored: the item stays under observation through them), and the estimates of different types are independent. \(e^{\beta_{kj}}\) is the rate ratio of covariate \(j\) for type \(k\).

The rate of events of any type is the sum of the types’ rates, and the expected number of events of any type is the sum of their expected numbers:

\[\lambda_\bullet(t \mid Z) = \sum_k \lambda_k(t \mid Z), \qquad \Lambda_\bullet(t \mid Z) = \sum_k \Lambda_k(t \mid Z) .\]

Because the types’ estimates are independent, the variance of an estimated total is the sum of the types’ delta-method variances, \(\operatorname{Var}\hat\Lambda_\bullet = \sum_k \operatorname{Var}\hat\Lambda_k\), and the confidence bounds are formed from it on the log scale, as each type’s are. Note that the total does not follow a proportional-intensity model of its own unless every type has the same coefficients: its rate ratio for a covariate is a mixture of the types’ ratios, weighted by their shares of the events, which is why one model fitted to all the events can report a small effect for a covariate that is strong for one type. The information criteria of the whole model use the summed log-likelihood, the parameters of every type and the events of every type. In SurPyval this is CauseSpecificProportionalIntensity; with covariates that change over time each \(\ell_k\) is the time-varying likelihood of the previous section. The worked example is on the Recurrent Event Regression Modelling with SurPyval page.

Interpreting the coefficients

  • \(e^{\beta_k}\) is the rate ratio for a one-unit increase in covariate \(k\), holding the others fixed: \(\beta_k = 0.7\) means about twice as many events (\(e^{0.7} \approx 2.0\)) at every age. Because the ratio is the same at every time, it is also the ratio of the expected number of events by any time \(t\).

  • A confidence interval for \(\beta_k\) becomes one for the rate ratio by exponentiating its end points.

  • The baseline describes \(Z = 0\). Coding a two-level factor as 0/1 makes the baseline one of the levels; centring a continuous covariate (subtracting its mean) makes the baseline a typical item and usually makes the optimisation better behaved too.

  • A factor with \(L\) levels needs \(L - 1\) indicator columns; a column of ones would duplicate the baseline’s scale parameter, and the model would not be identifiable.

Choosing the baseline

The choice of baseline is the choice of the shared trend over time, and the same reasoning applies as without covariates: use a constant baseline (the HPP) if there is no trend, and an NHPP baseline — a power law (Duane, the default, or CrowAMSAA) or the log-linear CoxLewis — if there is. Duane and Crow-AMSAA are the same power-law process in two parameterisations, so as baselines they describe the same model, and their fits should agree. (Likewise ProportionalIntensityNHPP with an HPP baseline is the same model as ProportionalIntensityHPP.) Compare candidate baselines with the information criteria and the diagnostics below.

A useful fact: when every item is observed over the same window, the coefficient estimates do not depend on the shape of the baseline at all — only the total expected count over the window enters the part of the likelihood that involves \(\beta\). The baseline then matters for prediction over time but not for the covariate effects. When the windows differ (items entered service at different times, say), the two are estimated jointly and a wrong baseline can bias the coefficients.

Assumptions and limitations

  • Static covariates in fit. There the covariates are properties of the item, constant over its observation. Supply them one row per event row (repeating the item’s values) or as a dictionary keyed by item. The residuals and the goodness-of-fit test take each item’s covariates from its first row.

  • Covariates that change over time (fit_tvc) must be piecewise constant, and act only through their current value. The residuals, the trend and goodness-of-fit tests, diagnose, plot and simulation along a schedule are not available for such a fit yet; each refuses, saying so.

  • Proportionality. Every item follows the same trend, scaled by its covariates. If harsh-environment motors wear out faster (a different shape), rather than simply failing more often, a single proportional model is the wrong tool; fit the groups separately and compare their shapes.

  • Poisson behaviour and heterogeneity. The model assumes the covariates explain all systematic differences between items. If items differ in ways the covariates do not capture (extra-Poisson variation), the coefficient estimates remain sensible but the model-based standard errors tend to be too small, and the goodness-of-fit test and martingale residuals are the place to look for it.

  • Scope. Covariates are available for the HPP and NHPP intensity models, and for the models with an unspecified baseline in the next section. The imperfect-repair (renewal) models and gapped (multi-window) observation of the parametric models do not take covariates.

Without a baseline shape: four ways to treat a repeat event

The models above give the baseline intensity a formula. When you only care how the covariates change the rate, and would rather not say what the baseline looks like, the baseline can be left unspecified, exactly as a Cox model leaves the baseline hazard of a single event unspecified. The coefficients are then estimated from the order in which the items had their events: at each event the model asks which of the items that could have had it did.

“Could have had it” is the whole question, because an item that has had an event is still there and can have another. There are four standard answers. They are four different statements about what a repeat event is, so they answer different questions, and choosing between them is a matter of the subject, not of fit.

Model

What it says about repeat events

The question it answers

Andersen-Gill [AndersenGill1982]

An event does not change the item. Its rate at any age depends on its covariates and its age, not on how many events it has had or when. One baseline for every event.

How do the covariates change the rate of events?

Prentice-Williams-Peterson, total time

An event changes the item. The rate of a second event is its own function of age, different from that of a first; an item cannot have a second before its first.

Among items that have had \(k - 1\) events, how do the covariates change the rate of the \(k\)-th, at a given age?

Prentice-Williams-Peterson, gap time

The same, but the clock restarts at each event: what matters is the time since the last event, not the age.

How do the covariates change the time between one event and the next?

Wei-Lin-Weissfeld

Nothing about how one event leads to the next. Each of “the time from the start to the first event”, “… to the second event”, and so on, is modelled on its own.

How do the covariates change the time, from the start, until the \(k\)-th event?

Two examples make the difference concrete.

A fleet of pumps. Pumps trip and are restarted. If a restart leaves a pump as it was, the number of earlier trips tells you nothing more than its age and duty do, and the Andersen-Gill model is the natural one: “an abrasive duty multiplies the trip rate by 1.8”. If instead each trip is followed by a repair that changes the pump, so that a pump on its third repair is not the machine it was when new, each repair number deserves a baseline of its own: the gap-time Prentice-Williams-Peterson model says “an abrasive duty shortens the time from one repair to the next”, with a different typical gap after the first, second and third repair.

A trial of a treatment for a relapsing illness. Patients are randomised at time 0 and may relapse several times. The Andersen-Gill model answers “does the treatment lower the relapse rate?”. The conditional models answer “among patients who have relapsed once, does the treatment delay the second relapse?”, which is a question about a selected group: patients who relapsed early despite treatment are not like those who relapsed on placebo. The Wei-Lin-Weissfeld model keeps the comparison the trial randomised: every patient counts towards “time from randomisation to the second relapse”, whether or not they have had the first.

In formulas, with \(Y_i(t)\) equal to 1 while item \(i\) is “at risk” and 0 otherwise, each model is

\[\lambda_{ik}(t \mid Z_i) = Y_{ik}(t)\, \lambda_{0k}(t)\, e^{\beta' Z_i}\]

and they differ in the baseline and in the risk indicator:

Each is fitted by the Cox partial likelihood on rows laid out to match: one row per interval at risk, in the stratum of its event number where the baseline depends on it [Therneau2000]. So the tie handling, the treatment of a coefficient the data cannot determine and the check that a maximum was reached are those of the Cox model.

Why the standard errors are “robust”

One item contributes several rows, and they are not independent: a pump that trips often does so for reasons the covariates do not record (its installation, its operator), and a patient who relapses early tends to relapse again. The partial likelihood’s own standard errors take every row as a separate piece of evidence, so when items differ in such unrecorded ways they are too small, often by a wide margin. The remedy keeps the estimates and replaces the standard errors: add up, item by item, how much each item pulls the estimate (its score residuals), and measure the spread of those pulls between items. Items are independent of each other even when an item’s events are not.

This is the sandwich estimator with each item a cluster [LinWei1989]. If \(A\) is the information of the partial likelihood and \(s_i\) is item \(i\)’s summed score residual,

\[V_{robust} = A^{-1} \Big( \sum_i s_i s_i^{\top} \Big) A^{-1},\]

where the model-based covariance is \(A^{-1}\) alone. With it the Andersen-Gill coefficients remain valid as effects on the mean number of events even when events are not Poisson [LinWeiYangYing2000], and it is what makes the Wei-Lin-Weissfeld model usable at all, since there every item appears in every stratum [WeiLinWeissfeld1989]. SurPyval reports the robust standard errors by default for all four models, and the model-based ones beside them; the two being close is itself a finding (the covariates explain the differences between items).

What these models do not give

  • A formula for the baseline. The cumulative baseline is a step function known only up to the last event, so it cannot be extrapolated. To forecast beyond the data, use the parametric proportional-intensity models above.

  • A simple expected count from the conditional and marginal models. The Andersen-Gill cumulative baseline, scaled by the covariates, is the expected number of events by an age. The others give the cumulative hazard of one event number, not a count.

  • Later events for free. A baseline per event number is estimated from the items that reached that number, which soon become few. It is usual to model the first few events only [PrenticeWilliamsPeterson1981].

Model checking

The fitted regression model carries the same diagnostics as the unconditional intensity models, applied per item with each item’s intensity scaled by its own covariate factor. The time-rescaling residuals pool the rescaled inter-arrival increments across items (i.i.d. Exp(1) under a well-specified model), the trend test checks whether a time-varying baseline was warranted at all, and the Cramér–von Mises test provides a bootstrapped goodness-of-fit p-value. Confidence bounds on the fitted cumulative intensity at a covariate setting come from the delta method via cif_cb.

A few points are specific to the regression setting:

  • The martingale residuals (observed minus expected events per item) are the most direct check of the covariate model. Plotted against a covariate, a trend in them suggests the covariate’s effect is not log-linear, and against a covariate left out of the model, a trend suggests it should be in.

  • The cumulative-hazard residuals only include the gaps that ended in an event; each item’s final, right-censored gap is left out. With only a few events per item that selection pulls their average below one even when the model is right, so judge their pattern rather than insisting on a mean of exactly one.

  • The trend test uses only the event times and observation windows, not the covariates. It answers “is there a common trend over time?”, which is the question that separates the HPP and NHPP baselines.

  • The Cramér–von Mises bootstrap resimulates every item from its own covariate-scaled intensity and refits the whole regression each time, so it checks the baseline shape and the covariate effects together.

These methods form a comprehensive toolkit for researchers and practitioners working with recurrent event data, enabling detailed analysis and prediction of event occurrences.

For worked examples — fitting the proportional-intensity HPP and NHPP models, reading off the event-count ratio between covariate settings, and plotting the covariate-conditional cumulative intensity with confidence bounds — see the Recurrent Event Regression Modelling with SurPyval page.

A terminal event that depends on the recurrences: the joint frailty model

Every model above ends an item’s history with a right-censored row, and takes that end to say nothing about the item’s events: the observation stopped because the study did. Often it stopped because of an event that ends the history for good – a machine scrapped after one repair too many, a patient who dies after repeated hospital admissions – and the items with many recurrences are the ones that reach it sooner. That end is then informative, and a model of the recurrences alone is biased: the items with the highest rates leave the data early, so the rate looks lower than it is.

The joint frailty model [LiuWolfeHuang2004] [Rondeau2007] models the two kinds of event together. Each item has its own unobserved frailty \(u\) – how prone it is to events, beyond what its covariates say – which multiplies its rate of recurrent events, and, raised to a power \(\alpha\), its hazard of the terminal event:

\[r_i(t \mid u) = u\, r_0(t)\, e^{\beta' Z_i}, \qquad \lambda_i(t \mid u) = u^{\alpha}\, \lambda_0(t)\, e^{\gamma' Z_i}.\]

In words:

  • \(\theta\), the variance of the frailty, says how much the items differ: 0 is no difference beyond the covariates, and the two kinds of event are then independent.

  • \(\alpha\) says how the terminal event depends on that difference: positive when the items with more recurrences reach it sooner, 0 when the terminal event does not depend on the recurrences (given the covariates), negative when the frail items last longer.

  • \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) are the covariates’ effects on each kind of event, as log rate ratios given the frailty.

The frailty is gamma with mean 1 (family="gamma", the default) or log-normal with \(\log u \sim N(0, \theta)\) (family="lognormal"). The baselines are Weibull: \(R_0(t) = (t / \eta_r)^{k_r}\), the power-law (Crow-AMSAA) intensity, and \(\Lambda_0(t) = (t / \eta_t)^{k_t}\). The recurrent intensity runs on the item’s age (time_scale="calendar", the Andersen-Gill form) or on the time since its last event (time_scale="gap": each event renews the baseline); the terminal hazard is always on the age. An item seen only from an age \(e\) (delayed entry, tl) is one that had not reached the terminal event by then, and the likelihood divides by the chance of that, which depends on the frailty.

The fit maximises the marginal likelihood, the frailty integrated out of each item’s likelihood,

\[L_i = \frac{\int_0^\infty \prod_j u\, r_0(t_{ij}) e^{\beta' Z_i} \, e^{-u R_i e^{\beta' Z_i}} \, \big(u^\alpha \lambda_0(X_i) e^{\gamma' Z_i}\big)^{\delta_i} e^{-u^\alpha \Lambda_0(X_i) e^{\gamma' Z_i}} \, dG_\theta(u)} {\int_0^\infty e^{-u^\alpha \Lambda_0(e_i) e^{\gamma' Z_i}} \, dG_\theta(u)},\]

with \(R_i\) the item’s cumulative baseline intensity over its observation and \(\delta_i\) 1 if the terminal event ended it. The integral has no closed form unless \(\alpha = 1\), and is computed in \(w = \log u\) over each item’s own range, from the integrand’s peak out to where it has fallen by \(e^{-36}\), by a trapezoid rule, which on an integrand this smooth is exact to rounding. The parameters’ covariance is the inverse of the exact (automatic-derivative) Hessian; param_cb gives Wald or profile-likelihood bounds.

What the model gives:

  • frailty_tests(): the likelihood-ratio test of \(\theta = 0\) (no frailty: the two parts fitted separately), whose p-value is that of an even mixture of \(\chi^2_1\) and \(\chi^2_2\): near the null the frailty acts through two variances on the edge of their range, \(\theta\) in the recurrent part and \(\alpha^2 \theta\) in the terminal part (the reference for one such variance, half a \(\chi^2_1\), rejected a true null 18% of the time at the 5% level in a simulation study; this mixture held 5.0% there and 4.6% in a second study of a different design); and of \(\alpha = 0\) (the terminal event independent of the recurrences), against \(\chi^2_1\).

  • frailties: each item’s predicted frailty, its mean given its own history.

  • cif and iif: the recurrent events’ cumulative intensity and intensity with no terminal event to stop them; mcf: the expected number of recurrences by an age, the terminal event stopping them, \(e^{\beta' Z} \int_0^t r_0(s)\, E[u\, S(s \mid u)]\, ds\).

  • sf, ff, hf, Hf, df: the terminal event’s functions. Each prediction is for the population of items with the covariates (frailty=None, averaged over the frailty) or for one item of known frailty (frailty=u).

  • random: items simulated from the model, as rows fit takes.

R’s frailtypack (frailtyPenal with terminal()) fits the same model; SurPyval’s agrees with its log-normal fits to three or four figures. With the gamma frailty the two integrate the frailty differently (a fixed Gauss-Laguerre rule there; here a rule checked against scipy’s adaptive quadrature to 1e-10), so their estimates agree to a few per cent. Not built: spline (penalised) baselines, covariates that change over time, several terminal events, and frailties shared by groups of items (a fleet, a hospital).
