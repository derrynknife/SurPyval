Relative Survival

When units are followed after some starting point – patients after a diagnosis, machines after an overhaul – many of them have the event for reasons that have nothing to do with what is being studied: a patient with a cancer dies of a heart attack, a vehicle is scrapped after an accident. Often the cause of each event is not recorded, or not reliably. Relative survival deals with this by comparing the units with the population they come from, whose own hazard is known from a table of rates.

In plain words

Two hazards add up. Each unit’s hazard is split into the hazard it would have anyway, as a member of its population (read from a rate table for its age, the calendar year and, say, its sex), and an excess hazard due to the condition studied:

\[\lambda_i(t) = \lambda_{P,i}(t) + \lambda_E(t).\]

The population part changes as each unit gets older and as the calendar moves on, so an 80-year-old’s background is far higher than a 50-year-old’s.

Net survival is the survival the units would have if the excess hazard were the only one: \(S_E(t) = \exp(-\Lambda_E(t))\). It is the standard measure for comparing cancer survival between countries and periods whose background mortality differs, and it needs no cause of death: an event of any cause counts, and the rate table takes out what the population would have had anyway.

Two estimators. RelativeSurvival.fit estimates net survival:

  • method="pohar-perme" (the default): Pohar Perme’s estimator weights each unit by the inverse of its expected survival, which puts back, in expectation, the units the background hazard took. It estimates net survival whatever the excess hazard depends on.

  • method="ederer2": Ederer II, the relative survival ratio of the units still followed. Where the excess hazard depends on age (it usually does), the units still followed late on are the younger ones, and Ederer II no longer estimates net survival.

A regression. ExcessHazard.fit models how covariates act on the excess hazard: \(\lambda_i(t) = \lambda_{P,i}(t) + \lambda_0(t) e^{\beta' Z_i}\). Its coefficients are logs of excess hazard ratios, and its baseline \(\lambda_0\) is constant within intervals of follow-up (one by default; breaks gives more).

Net survival is not a probability of a set of units. It is a difference of hazards. Where fewer units fail than the population rates expect it rises, and it can exceed 1. SurPyval reports it as it is.

The rate table

A RateTable holds the population’s hazard rates by age band, and optionally by calendar-year band and stratum. Its ages, years and the follow-up times must all be in one unit, and its rates per that unit: per person-year with times in years. National life tables give the probability \(q\) of dying within a year of age; the rate for that year is \(-\log(1 - q)\).

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import surpyval as surv

# A population whose death rates rise about 9% a year of age, the
# men's 70% above the women's (annual bands, 0 to 110+).
ages = np.arange(0, 111)
women = 4e-5 * np.exp(0.092 * ages)
table = surv.RateTable({"F": women, "M": 1.7 * women}, ages=ages)
print(table)
# The chance that a woman aged 70 lives another ten years
print(table.sf(10, age=70, stratum="F").round(3))

RateTable: 111 age band(s) (0 to 110+), 2 strata ('F', 'M')
0.675

A table by calendar year as well takes a 2-D array of rates per stratum, one column per year band, and years= the bands’ lower edges; each unit then also needs its calendar time at the start of follow-up (year=2011.5). RateTable.from_df reads a long table (one row per age, year and stratum) from a DataFrame.

Estimating net survival

The units below are simulated: 2,000 people aged 50 to 85, whose excess hazard is 0.1 a year at 65 and rises 5% a year of age, each also subject to the population rates above, followed for up to 10 years.

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
size = 2000
age = rng.uniform(50, 85, size)
sex = rng.choice(["F", "M"], size)
excess_rate = 0.1 * np.exp(0.05 * (age - 65))
excess = rng.exponential(1 / excess_rate)
# Each unit's time to death from the population's hazard: its
# expected cumulative hazard on a grid, inverted at an exponential draw
grid = np.linspace(0, 60, 1201)
H = table.Hf(grid[None, :], age[:, None], stratum=sex[:, None])
reached = (H < rng.exponential(size=size)[:, None]).sum(axis=1)
population = grid[np.minimum(reached, grid.size - 1)]
years = np.minimum(np.minimum(excess, population), 10.0)
censored = (years == 10.0).astype(int)

pohar_perme = surv.RelativeSurvival.fit(
    years, censored, rate_table=table, age=age, stratum=sex
)
ederer2 = surv.RelativeSurvival.fit(
    years, censored, rate_table=table, age=age, stratum=sex,
    method="ederer2",
)
truth = [np.mean(np.exp(-excess_rate * t)) for t in (5, 10)]
print("true net survival      ", np.round(truth, 3))
print("Pohar Perme            ", pohar_perme.sf([5, 10]).round(3))
print("Ederer II              ", ederer2.sf([5, 10]).round(3))
# 90% two-sided pointwise bounds on Pohar Perme's net survival at 10
print("Pohar Perme, 90% bounds", pohar_perme.cb(10, alpha_ci=0.1).round(3))

true net survival       [0.551 0.328]
Pohar Perme             [0.545 0.337]
Ederer II               [0.559 0.367]
Pohar Perme, 90% bounds [0.311 0.366]

Pohar Perme’s estimate at 10 years is close to the truth, inside its 90% bounds. Ederer II’s is higher than both: the older units, whose excess hazard is higher, have left the risk set faster than the younger, and the relative survival ratio of those still followed describes the younger ones.

model.table gives, at each follow-up time, the number at risk and of events, the increments of the observed and expected cumulative hazards, the cumulative excess hazard Hf, its standard error and net survival. cb gives pointwise bounds on sf, ff or Hf, on the log scale (bound_type="log", the default, as R’s relsurv) or the plain one ("normal"); neither is cut at 1.

Excess hazard regression

ExcessHazard fits the excess hazard as \(\lambda_0(t) e^{\beta' Z}\) beside each unit’s population hazard, by maximum likelihood (Estève et al., 1990). Here a treatment halves the excess hazard, which is otherwise constant at 0.15 a year:

treated = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, size)
excess = rng.exponential(1 / (0.15 * np.exp(-0.7 * treated)))
years = np.minimum(np.minimum(excess, population), 8.0)
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "years": years,
    "censored": (years == 8.0).astype(int),
    "treated": treated,
    "age": age,
    "sex": sex,
})
model = surv.ExcessHazard.fit_from_df(
    df, "years", ["treated"], "age", table, c_col="censored",
    stratum_col="sex", breaks=3,
)
print(model)
columns = ["coef", "exp(coef)", "coef lower 90%", "coef upper 90%", "p"]
print(model.summary(alpha_ci=0.1)[columns].round(3))

The treatment’s excess hazard ratio, exp(coef), is about a half, and its 90% interval holds the true coefficient, -0.7. breaks=3 gives the baseline three intervals with equal numbers of events (an array of times gives them directly); every interval needs events. A covariate that is constant, or a combination of the others, is aliased (its coefficient nan, with one warning), and a fit whose likelihood has no finite maximum – an interval with no more events than the population rates expect, or a group with no events at all – says so.

The model’s sf, ff, hf, Hf, df and qf are the net (excess-only) functions of a unit with covariates Z; cb bounds them by the delta method on the log of the cumulative excess hazard, and observed_sf is a unit’s all-cause survival, its expected survival from the rate table times its net survival:

print("net survival at 5, treated or not:",
      model.sf(5, [[1], [0]]).round(3))
print("all-cause survival at 5 of a treated woman aged 70:",
      model.observed_sf(5, [1], age=70, stratum="F").round(3))

net survival at 5, treated or not: [0.705 0.465]
all-cause survival at 5 of a treated woman aged 70: 0.606

The maths

With \(Y_i(t)\) unit \(i\)’s at-risk indicator, \(\Lambda_{P,i}\) its expected cumulative hazard along its path through the rate table and \(u_k\) the distinct follow-up times:

  • Ederer II: \(\hat\Lambda_E(t) = \sum_{u_k \le t} d_k / Y(u_k) - \int_0^t \sum_i Y_i(s)\lambda_{P,i}(s) / Y(s)\, ds\), with variance \(\sum_{u_k \le t} d_k / Y(u_k)^2\);

  • Pohar Perme: the same with each unit weighted by \(w_i(t) = 1/S_{P,i}(t)\), \(Y^w = \sum_i Y_i w_i\); the expected part over a stretch with the same units at risk is the increase of \(\log Y^w\) there (as \(dw_i/ds = w_i\lambda_{P,i}\)), and the variance \(\sum_{\text{events}} w_i^2 / Y^w(u_k)^2\).

The expected parts are integrated exactly between the follow-up times; between two of them a prediction takes the expected part as linear in time. The excess hazard model’s log-likelihood is

\[\ell = \sum_i n_i \Big[ \delta_i \log\big(\lambda_{P,i}(x_i) + \lambda_0(x_i) e^{\beta' Z_i}\big) - e^{\beta' Z_i}\Lambda_0(x_i) - \Lambda_{P,i}(x_i) \Big].\]

What is not built yet: delayed entry (left truncation) and interval censoring, which the fits refuse; a spline or other flexible baseline excess hazard; and covariate effects on the excess hazard that change with time.
