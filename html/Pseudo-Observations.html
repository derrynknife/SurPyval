Pseudo-Observations

Most survival regressions describe how a covariate changes the hazard (Cox, PH) or the time scale (AFT). Often the question is simpler and asked directly on a probability or a time: “how much more likely is a patient in group B to be alive at one year?”, “how many more days, on average, does a pump last in its first year if it is run cooler?”, “is the chance of failing from corrosion by 5 years higher at the coast?”. Pseudo-observations answer such questions without a model of the hazard.

The idea in plain words

If no time were censored, the questions above would be ordinary regressions: regress “alive at one year” (1 or 0), or “time lived in the first year”, min(x, 365), on the covariates. Censoring stops that, because a unit censored at 200 days has no known one-year outcome.

A pseudo-observation fills that gap. Take the quantity the whole sample estimates, say the Kaplan-Meier survival at one year. Recompute it with one unit left out. How much the estimate moves when that unit is left out says what the unit contributed: the unit’s pseudo-value is N times the full estimate minus N - 1 times the leave-one-out estimate (a jackknife). For a unit followed past the time it is close to its observed outcome (1 if it survived, 0 if it failed), and for a unit censored before the time it is the sample’s best guess (a value in between). The pseudo-values then go into an ordinary regression as if they were complete responses.

Three quantities are offered:

  • survival probability at one or more times (pseudo_survival(), R’s pseudo::pseudosurv);

  • restricted mean survival time (RMST) to a horizon tau, the average time lived in [0, tau], the area under the Kaplan-Meier curve (pseudo_rmst(), R’s pseudo::pseudomean);

  • cumulative incidence of one cause when there are competing causes of failure (pseudo_cif(), R’s pseudo::pseudoci).

The regression on them is pseudo_regression() (or pseudo_regression_from_df()). It fits a generalised linear model to the pseudo-values by estimating equations (GEE) with an independence working model, and reports robust (sandwich) standard errors, which allow for the pseudo-values not being independent of each other. This is what R’s geepack::geeglm(..., corstr = "independence") gives on R’s pseudo-values; SurPyval matches both to within 1e-8 (the pseudo-values) and 1e-6 (coefficients and standard errors).

The formulas

For an estimator \(\hat\theta\) of the quantity from all \(N\) units and \(\hat\theta_{(-i)}\) from all but unit \(i\),

\[\hat\theta_i = N \hat\theta - (N - 1) \hat\theta_{(-i)}.\]

With \(\hat\theta = \hat S(t)\) (Kaplan-Meier), \(\int_0^\tau \hat S(u)\,du\) (RMST) or \(\hat F_k(t)\) (Aalen-Johansen), and censoring independent of the covariates, \(E[\hat\theta_i \mid Z_i] \approx \theta(Z_i)\) (Andersen, Klein and Rosthøj 2003; Graw, Gerds and Schumacher 2009). The regression solves

\[\sum_i D_i^\top (y_i - \mu_i) = 0, \qquad g(\mu_{ij}) = a_j + s\,\beta^\top Z_i,\]

over each unit’s times \(j\), with \(D_i = \partial\mu_i / \partial(a, \beta)\), and the robust covariance \(A^{-1} B A^{-1}\), \(A = \sum_i D_i^\top D_i\) and \(B = \sum_i D_i^\top r_i r_i^\top D_i\). A unit with count n counts n times in both.

The sign of the coefficients

SurPyval reads every coefficient one way: a positive coefficient means a shorter life (as its PH and AFT models). So:

  • the survival regressions model the chance of the event by \(t\), \(F(t) = 1 - S(t)\). With the default complementary log-log link, \(\log(-\log S(t)) = a(t) + \beta^\top Z\), a proportional-hazards model at the chosen times: \(e^\beta\) is a ratio of cumulative hazards, close to a Cox hazard ratio. With link="logit", \(e^\beta\) is an odds ratio of failing by \(t\). (To compare with R, fit geeglm to 1 - pseudosurv.)

  • the cumulative incidence regressions model the incidence of the cause, so a positive coefficient means more failures from it, as in Fine-Gray.

  • the RMST regression is \(g(\text{RMST}) = a - \beta^\top Z\). With the identity link (the default) a covariate’s coefficient is minus its difference in RMST: \(\beta = 20\) means 20 time units less of the first \(\tau\). With link="log", \(e^{-\beta}\) is the ratio of RMSTs. R’s geeglm on pseudomean reports the same numbers with the other sign.

An example: lung cancer

import numpy as np
import surpyval as surv

lung = surv.datasets.load_lung()
lung["c"] = 1 - lung["status"]          # 1 = censored
lung["female"] = (lung["sex"] == 2).astype(int)

The average number of days lived in the first year, by sex and age:

rmst = surv.pseudo_regression_from_df(
    lung, "time", ["female", "age"], c_col="c", quantity="rmst", tau=365
)
rmst

Competing risks

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
z = rng.integers(0, 2, 300)
t1 = rng.exponential(10 * np.exp(-0.5 * z))   # cause "wear"
t2 = rng.exponential(15, 300)                  # cause "impact"
cens = rng.uniform(0, 25, 300)
x = np.minimum.reduce([t1, t2, cens])
e = np.where(x == cens, None, np.where(t1 < t2, "wear", "impact"))

cif = surv.pseudo_regression(
    x, z, quantity="cif", e=e, cause="wear", t=[5, 10], names=["z"]
)
cif.summary()[["coef", "exp(coef)", "se(coef)", "p"]]

What is not covered

  • Delayed entry (tl) is refused: leave-one-out estimates of a left-truncated sample do not give pseudo-values with the right expectation. Use CoxPH or a parametric model with tl.

  • Left and interval censoring are refused.

  • Times past the largest time in the data are refused (the estimate is not defined there), and so is a time with no failures before it.

  • Censoring must not depend on the covariates for the pseudo-values to be unbiased; where it does, the regression is biased (Binder, Gerds and Andersen 2014 give an inverse-probability weighted version).

Cost

Each leave-one-out estimate is a product over the distinct event times, computed once for each distinct row, in blocks: the work is proportional to (distinct rows) x (distinct event times). Pseudo-values at two times and an RMST for 5,000 units with distinct times take about half a second.
