Joint Models

A measurement read again and again on each item – a pump’s vibration, a gearbox’s oil debris, a tyre’s tread depth, a patient’s biomarker – often says how close the item is to failing. The item’s risk rises as the measurement does. But the measurement is noisy, it is read only at inspections, and it stops when the item fails. A joint model describes the measurement (the marker) and the lifetime together, so that each is read in the light of the other. In reliability terms it gives each asset’s remaining life given its readings so far, updated as new readings arrive.

SurPyval’s joint model (WeibullJointPH, #891) is the standard one (Rizopoulos, 2012):

  • the marker follows a degradation path model – a straight line in age for WeibullJointPH, or any of the degradation module’s paths (QuadraticPath, ExponentialPath, …) or one of your own – with parameters of the item’s own (drawn, across items, from a multivariate normal), read with normal noise;

  • the hazard is Weibull proportional hazards in the path’s current, noise-free value, and the item’s covariates, which also shift its path.

The hazard need not follow the marker’s level. It can follow how fast the marker is changing (its slope), or the exposure accumulated so far (the area under its path), or more than one of these: see What the hazard follows: the link. And the marker need not multiply the hazard at all: in WeibullJointAFT it makes the item age faster, the life used up at a high reading not given back when the reading falls (see How the reading acts: a higher failure rate, or faster ageing).

Why not the obvious approaches?

Two shortcuts are common, and both are biased:

  • The last reading as a time-varying covariate (a time-varying Cox or Weibull PH fit, the reading carried forward to the next). It takes a noisy reading as the truth, and between readings a value that is out of date. Both pull the marker’s estimated effect towards zero – regression dilution – however many items there are.

  • The marker alone (a degradation model). The readings stop when an item fails, and the items that fail are the ones whose marker was high: fitting the readings without the failures under-states how markers rise. And a threshold on the marker cannot describe failures that come at any level, with a risk that only grows with it.

The joint likelihood fixes both: each item’s line is integrated over what its readings leave uncertain, and its readings and its lifetime inform the same line. (A middle way, fitting each item’s line first and the lifetimes on those lines second, is quick but still biased: see A quick first look: the two-stage fit.) (A third approach, which models no path at all, is landmarking: a model of the units still at risk at a time, by their last readings then.)

The model

Item \(i\) has readings \(y_{ij}\) at ages \(t_{ij}\), covariates \(z_i\), and fails or is censored at \(T_i\). Its marker follows the path model \(m(t; \theta)\) (path(t, *theta); for WeibullJointPH the line \(a + b t\)) with parameters \(\theta_i\) of its own, read with noise:

\[y_{ij} = m(t_{ij}; \theta_i) + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad \theta_{iV} \sim N(\mu_V + \Delta_V z_i, D), \qquad \theta_{iC} = \mu_C + \Delta_C z_i,\]

the parameters that vary between items (\(V\), all of them by default) multivariate normal about means the covariates shift, the others (\(C\)) common to every item, and \(\varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\). By default every covariate shifts every path parameter’s mean and enters the hazard; a fit can put each covariate in one part or both (\(\Delta\) and \(\gamma\) are then 0 where a covariate is left out: see Covariates in each part). The hazard is

\[h_i(t) = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{t}{\alpha}\right)^{\beta-1} \exp\left(\gamma' z_i + \zeta\,\bigl(m(t; \theta_i) - r\bigr)\right).\]

\(\zeta\) (marker_effect) is the change in the log hazard per unit of the marker: exp(marker_effect) is the hazard ratio of one unit. The marker enters relative to \(r\) (marker_reference: the mean reading of the data, unless the fit is given another), so the Weibull \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the baseline of an item with its marker at \(r\) and its covariates at 0.

Given its path, an item’s readings and lifetime are independent, so its likelihood is the integral over its varying parameters

\[L_i = \int p(y_i \mid \theta)\, h_i(T_i \mid \theta)^{d_i}\, S_i(T_i \mid \theta)\, \phi(\theta_V; \mu_V + \Delta_V z_i, D)\, d\theta_V,\]

\(d_i\) 1 for a failure and 0 for a censoring. The fit maximises the sum of the \(\log L_i\), each integral by adaptive Gauss–Hermite quadrature: a product grid of 7 points a dimension (49 for two varying parameters, 343 for three; see how the integral is done for four and five, and for more, where there is no grid), centred at each item’s most likely parameters and scaled by how sure its readings make them (the Gauss–Newton curvature, from the path’s Jacobian: exact for a path linear in its parameters). The nodes are held fixed while the parameters are searched (with autograd’s gradient, in the parameters’ standard errors), and a step is taken only as far as the likelihood with the nodes placed again at the new point improves; this repeats until the log-likelihood stops moving. A common path parameter is searched through the path model itself (its Jacobian, and differences of it for the Hessian). The cumulative hazard along a path is by 15-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature. The fit starts from the path fitted to each item alone (the path model’s own fit; items with too few readings for it are left out of this step), the spread of those fits about the covariates’ means, and a survival fit with each item’s predicted path plugged in – a two-stage estimate, which is biased (A quick first look: the two-stage fit returns it as a model of its own). The covariance is the inverse of the Hessian of the negative log-likelihood, by autograd.

An item found failed at an inspection is known only to have failed between two ages, \(l_i < T_i \le u_i\) (interval_censored; left_censored is the interval from its entry, or 0): \(h_i(T_i)^{d_i} S_i(T_i)\) is then \(S_i(l_i \mid \theta) - S_i(u_i \mid \theta)\), the hazard integrated over the interval directly (so a narrow interval late in life loses no precision). An item that came under observation at age \(L_i > 0\) (left_truncation) is in the data because it reached \(L_i\), and one right truncated at \(R_i\) (right_truncation) because it failed by then: its likelihood is divided by the probability of that, \(\int (S(L_i \mid \theta) - S(R_i \mid \theta)) \phi(\theta_V)\, d\theta_V\), over the population’s paths. The likelihood of every kind of item agrees with an independent dense-grid integral over the random effects to within 1e-5 an item (tests/univariate/joint/test_joint_extensions.py).

How the integral is done

Each item’s likelihood is an average over the path parameters it could have (its own level, its own rate, …), and that average is a sum over a grid of 7 values of each, placed where that item’s readings and lifetime say its parameters are. The grid grows quickly with the number of varying parameters: 7 nodes for one, 49 for two, 343 for three, 2,401 for four and 16,807 for five, for every item at every step of the search. A sixth would make it 117,649, so beyond five there is no grid: the integral is Laplace’s approximation.

integration= on fit (and fit_from_df) chooses how:

  • "grid": the full grid. The default up to five varying parameters: the more accurate choice.

  • "pruned": the grid without the nodes whose weight is negligible – its far corners, where several parameters are extreme at once. The rule is fixed: the lowest-weight nodes are left out, as many as hold less than 1e-9 of the grid’s weight between them (nodes of equal weight together), whatever the data. That is none of the nodes for up to three parameters, 80 of 2,401 for four and 1,392 of 16,807 for five. An option, never the default: it trades a little accuracy for a little speed, and in practice saves little (below).

  • "laplace": Laplace’s approximation, with no grid. The default beyond five varying parameters, where a grid is refused, and an option below (below).

The model says which it used (model.integration, and the Integration line of its printout), keeps it when saved, and predicts on the same kind of grid (a Laplace fit predicts on the full grid up to five varying parameters, and by Laplace’s approximation beyond). A model saved before the choice existed reads as "grid".

from surpyval import JointPH, Weibull

fitter = JointPH(Weibull, cubic)            # a, b, c and d all vary
model = fitter.fit(data)                    # the full grid: 2,401 nodes an item
model.integration                           # 'grid'
pruned = fitter.fit(data, integration="pruned")

The pruned grid is a small saving that costs no accuracy: 3% fewer nodes with four varying parameters and 8% fewer with five (one evaluation of the likelihood and its gradient is 1% and 6% quicker). Where the integrand is exactly normal (no marker effect, a path linear in its parameters) both grids reproduce the closed form in four and five dimensions, the pruned one short by the weight it leaves out, 3e-10 and 8e-10 of an item’s likelihood. With a marker effect the two log-likelihoods differ by that much again on items read often, and by up to 7e-7 an item in the hardest case tried (five varying parameters, a reading every 0.7 years with noise five times the usual, a marker effect of 2); a fleet of 60 items fitted on each grid gave estimates within 1e-6 of a standard error of each other (tests/univariate/joint/test_joint_integration.py).

Two things to expect with four or five varying parameters. The fit takes minutes where two or three take seconds – the fleet of 60 items took two to five minutes – and most of that is the search over the ten or fifteen variances and correlations of the items’ spread, not the grid. And the 7 points themselves are less exact where items have few, noisy readings and the marker effect is strong: against 11 points a dimension the log-likelihood of such a fleet was off by up to 5e-5 an item (it was within 4e-10 an item on a fleet read often). That is the grid’s own error, the same on the full grid and the pruned one. Fewer correlations (next) make that search smaller.

Laplace’s approximation

A grid samples each item’s integrand at many points. Laplace’s approximation uses only where it peaks: the integrand’s height at the item’s most likely parameters \(\hat b_i\), and how sharply it falls away from them,

\[\log L_i \approx g_i(\hat b_i) + \frac{q}{2} \log 2\pi - \frac{1}{2} \log \det\bigl(-g_i''(\hat b_i)\bigr),\]

with \(g_i\) the log of the integrand and \(q\) the number of varying parameters. That is the integral of the normal curve that matches the integrand at its peak, so it is exact where the integrand is normal (a path linear in its parameters and no marker effect), and off by a term of the order of one over the item’s number of readings otherwise (Tierney and Kadane 1986; for joint models, Rizopoulos, Verbeke and Lesaffre 2009). The curvature is the integrand’s own, by differences on \(q^2 + q + 1\) points around the peak: 21 evaluations an item for four varying parameters where the grid takes 2,401, and 43 for six, where there is no grid.

model = JointPH(Weibull, cubic).fit(data, integration="laplace")
model.integration                           # 'laplace'

# six varying parameters: Laplace's approximation without asking
JointPH(Weibull, six_bumps).fit(data).integration    # 'laplace'

The fit maximises the approximated likelihood exactly: each item’s peak moves as the parameters change, and the gradient the search follows and the curvature the standard errors come from both account for that (the gradient through the implicit-function theorem, the curvature by differences of that gradient with every peak found again). A fit by Laplace’s approximation has aic(), bic() and neg_ll() like any other, of the approximated likelihood: compare models fitted the same way.

How close it is to the grid, measured (tests/univariate/joint/test_joint_laplace.py and scripts/studies/joint_recovery/laplace_few_readings.py):

  • With no marker effect it reproduces the closed form to 1e-8 an item in two, three, four and six dimensions.

  • With a marker effect, at the simulation’s truth, its log-likelihood was within about 1e-6 an item of an 11-point grid’s on fleets read every 0.1 years (up to 31 readings an item), and within 5e-4 an item read every 0.5 years with three times the noise (up to 7 readings).

  • Fitted to the recovery study’s pumps (300 a fleet, two varying parameters, read monthly: up to 25 readings), its estimates were within 0.02 standard errors of the full grid’s on each of five fleets, and its standard errors within 0.3%. A fleet of 60 items with four varying parameters gave estimates within 0.001 standard errors and standard errors within 0.3%, in 9 seconds where the full grid took 555.

  • A recovery study of 200 such fleets of 100 items (four varying parameters, all six correlations estimated, their truth 0; scripts/studies/joint_recovery/study.py cubic with INTEGRATION=laplace, summary_cubic_laplace.json) took about 45 seconds a fleet. 61 of the 200 fits had no standard errors: the estimated correlation matrix ran to the edge of where a correlation matrix can be (on one, its smallest eigenvalue was 2e-4), as six correlations among 100 items can; three of them, fitted again with random_effects="diagonal", reached a verified maximum with standard errors. On the 139 that had them, the 95% intervals covered the truth 89% to 98% of the time (the path’s coefficients 91% to 96%, the marker’s effect 94%), and the marker’s effect was 2.7% high (Monte Carlo error 1.2%), the Weibull’s scale 1.4% high (0.6%) and one correlation 0.07 low (0.03). These are of the fits that had standard errors, not of all 200.

  • It degrades as the readings thin out and are noisy. The same pumps read every 90 days (at most 9 readings; 6% to 9% of the pumps with three or fewer): within 0.08 standard errors. Every 180 days (at most 5; 36% to 42% with three or fewer): 0.10 to 0.18. Once a year (at most 3): 0.23 to 0.41, and one fit of five did not reach a verified maximum. The difference is largest in the marker’s effect. Where the readings are precise it holds up with few: fleets of a quadratic path with three varying parameters and less than a third of the pumps’ noise stayed within 0.005 standard errors with at most 4 readings an item.

A second run, its design and its rule for the warning fixed before it ran, read 20 more fleets of the pumps every 100 to 160 days (9% to 36% of the pumps with three readings or fewer): every fleet with up to 15% so read was within 0.074 standard errors, and from 16% fleets were off by up to 0.164 (five of the fourteen beyond 15% were over 0.1). So the fit warns, with the count, when more than 15% of the items have three readings or fewer, and says to use a grid (with up to five varying parameters) or to let fewer parameters vary. Two more things it does not do. It does not take truncated items (late entry, or in the data only because they failed by an age): the chance of an item being in the data is an integral with no readings in it, which the approximation is not accurate for, so the fit refuses and says to use a grid. And beyond five varying parameters its predictions are also by Laplace’s approximation (each integral at its own peak), so the survival of an item with few readings or none is approximate there; up to five they are on the full grid.

Beyond five varying parameters the fit starts differently too. The start from each item’s own fitted path fails where a path has many parameters and an item barely as many readings, so the items’ fits are summarised by their median and spread, and the marker’s own mixed model is settled first. A fleet of 120 items with six varying parameters (a marker that is a sum of six smooth bumps along the age, the height of each an item’s own, uncorrelated) was recovered within 3 standard errors in about three minutes.

Which path parameters are correlated

By default every pair of varying parameters is correlated between items: an item that starts high may rise faster, or slower, and the fit estimates by how much (corr_a_b). That is one correlation for two varying parameters, three for three, six for four and ten for five, and a fleet of tens of items cannot pin down ten. random_effects= on the fitter says which are correlated:

  • "full": every pair (the default; the model as it always was).

  • "diagonal": none. Each varying parameter varies between items independently of the others.

  • blocks of names, such as [("a", "b"), ("c",)]: those in a block are correlated with each other and with none outside it. Every varying parameter is in exactly one block; a name that is not a varying parameter, or one named twice or left out, is refused, saying which.

from surpyval import JointPH, Weibull
from surpyval.degradation import QuadraticPath

independent = JointPH(Weibull, random_effects="diagonal").fit(data)
independent.parameter_names[:4]       # ['a', 'b', 'sd_a', 'sd_b']
blocks = JointPH(Weibull, QuadraticPath, random_effects=[("a", "b"), ("c",)])

A correlation left out is 0, not estimated: it has no entry in params, in summary() or in the saved model, and aic() counts the parameters that are estimated. The structures nest – the full one holds every other with some correlations at 0 – so they compare on the same data by aic() or by the likelihood ratio. The model keeps the structure as random_effects_structure ("full", "diagonal", or the blocks as a tuple of tuples of names), and its printout and the summary’s heading of the spread say it. A model with a structure other than the full one saves as schema 5, which SurPyval 0.28 refuses rather than read its parameters as a full one’s; a model saved before the choice existed reads as "full".

The data: readings on the item records

The readings are the markers of each item’s ItemLifetime, {age: value}, taken while it was observed (from its left_truncation, else 0, to its failure or censoring). Here is a fleet of 300 pumps followed for two years and read monthly, simulated from the model: vibration starts near 3 mm/s and rises about 1 mm/s a year, each pump at its own level and rate, read with noise of 0.7 mm/s; each mm/s multiplies the hazard by \(e^{0.9} = 2.5\).

import numpy as np
from surpyval import ItemLifetime, SurpyvalData, WeibullJointPH

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)


def pump(k):
    start = rng.normal(3.0, 0.6)             # vibration when new, mm/s
    rate = rng.normal(1.0, 0.5) / 365        # its rise, mm/s a day
    # hazard: Weibull (800 days, shape 2) at 4 mm/s, x e^0.9 per mm/s
    t = np.linspace(0, 3000, 30001)
    h = (2 / 800) * (t / 800) * np.exp(0.9 * (start + rate * t - 4.0))
    H = np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum((h[1:] + h[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(t))])
    life = np.interp(rng.exponential(), H, t)
    end = min(life, 730.0)                    # followed for two years
    ages = np.arange(0.0, end, 30.0)          # read monthly
    readings = start + rate * ages + rng.normal(0, 0.7, ages.size)
    outcome = "observed" if life <= 730 else "right_censored"
    return ItemLifetime(
        f"P{k}",
        **{outcome: round(end, 1)},
        markers=dict(zip(ages, readings.round(2))),
    )


fleet = SurpyvalData.from_items([pump(k) for k in range(300)])
print(fleet.summary())

Covariates in each part

A record’s covariates enter both parts by default: each path parameter’s mean (a_<name>, b_<name>: a shift of the level, of the rate) and the hazard (<name>, a log hazard ratio at a given marker). Named covariates (covariates={"site": 1}) name the parameters; a covariate the data cannot determine (constant, or a combination of others) is aliased, its coefficients nan, with one warning.

A condition often acts on one part only. A heavy load can make a pump’s vibration rise faster without making it any more likely to fail at a given vibration: it acts through the marker, on the path. A different site – another maintenance crew, another duty – can change the risk at a given vibration without changing how vibration rises: it acts on the hazard. hazard_covariates=[...] names the covariates in the hazard and path_covariates= those that shift the path, a list (every path parameter) or {path parameter: [...]} (here only the rate b); each defaults to every covariate, [] is none, and a covariate must be in at least one. Putting a covariate where it does not act wastes the data on coefficients that are 0, and putting it only where it does not act biases the others. Late entry

Items that came under observation late – a fleet whose records start when monitoring was installed – are left_truncation-ed, and the fit accounts for having seen only the ones that reached that age; items in the data only because they failed by an age (a recall of failed units, say) are right_truncation-ed, likewise.

More than one way to fail: failure modes

Units fail in more than one way, and a reading usually speaks to only some of them: vibration warns of a bearing failure, not of a seal leak. Fitting every failure alike waters the reading’s effect down, and treating the other failures as “still running” overstates the life. Give each failure its mode (event_type on the record, e with arrays, e_col with a DataFrame) and the model has a hazard for each mode, with a Weibull baseline, coefficients and a marker effect of its own:

\[h_{ik}(t) = \frac{\beta_k}{\alpha_k}\left(\frac{t}{\alpha_k} \right)^{\beta_k - 1} \exp\left(\gamma_k' z_i + \zeta_k \left(m(t; \theta_i) - r\right)\right).\]

An item fails by whichever mode comes first, and is then no longer at risk of the others; the marker part is one for all the modes. Here some of the pumps’ failures are seal leaks, which come at random and have nothing to do with the vibration (made-up numbers):

What the hazard follows: the link

So far the risk has followed the marker’s level: a pump at 5 mm/s is at more risk than one at 3 mm/s, however each got there. That is one of three things a marker can say about risk, and JointPH(..., link=...) chooses between them:

  • link="value" (the default): risk follows the level. What matters is where the marker is now. A vibration level that is a symptom of the present damage; a biomarker that measures how ill the patient is today.

  • link="slope": risk follows how fast it is changing. What matters is the marker’s rate of rise, not where it stands. Bearings that settle at different vibration levels by their mounting, where a level that is climbing is the sign of trouble; a tumour marker whose doubling time, not its level, says how aggressive the disease is.

  • link="cumulative": risk follows the accumulated exposure. What matters is the total so far, the area under the marker’s path from age 0: every hour at a high reading adds to the damage, and it does not go away when the reading falls. A load, temperature or contamination reading where the wear done is the dose received; a patient’s cumulative exposure to a raised blood pressure or blood sugar.

A list gives the hazard more than one, each with an effect of its own: link=["value", "slope"] asks whether, at a given level, a marker that is rising is at more risk than one that is steady.

In formulas, with \(m_i(t) = m(t; \theta_i)\) the item’s noise-free path, the hazard is

\[h_i(t) = h_0(t) \exp\left(\gamma' z_i + \zeta_v \bigl(m_i(t) - r\bigr) + \zeta_s\, m_i'(t) + \zeta_c \int_0^t m_i(s)\, ds\right),\]

with only the terms of the links chosen: marker_effect (\(\zeta_v\)) per unit of the marker, slope_effect (\(\zeta_s\)) per unit of the marker a unit of time, and cumulative_effect (\(\zeta_c\)) per unit of the marker times a unit of time. The slope is the path model’s own derivative (slope; exact for the built-in paths, central differences for a path of one’s own without one), and the area is by 15-point Gauss–Legendre quadrature (exact for a line or a quadratic). Everything else – the data, the quadrature over each item’s path, the predictions, the bounds, saving – is as before.

Three things to know:

  • The baseline. alpha and beta are the Weibull of an item whose slope is 0 and whose accumulated area is 0 (the printout says so: alpha at slope = 0). marker_reference is the value’s reference only, and is refused where the hazard does not follow the value. The area is of the marker itself, from age 0: for the exposure above a level – the hours above a rated temperature, say – subtract that level from the readings first.

  • Paths whose slope starts infinite. The cumulative hazard runs from age 0, so the slope link needs a path whose slope is finite there. A logarithmic path (slope \(b / t\)) and a Lloyd-Lipow path (slope \(b / t^2\)) are refused with the slope link when the fitter is built, and a power path \(a t^b\) is refused by the fit when its average item’s power is below 1 (slope \(a b t^{b - 1}\)): near age 0 the failure rate would be infinite for every item whose slope has the sign of its effect. Each refusal names the links the path can take instead, and paths whose slope is finite at 0 (a line, a quadratic, an exponential).

  • The units. A slope’s and an area’s effects depend on the unit of age as well as the marker’s: a slope_effect per mm/s a day is 365 times one per mm/s a year, and a cumulative_effect per (mm/s)·day is 365 times one per (mm/s)·year. Ages in years (or a rate per year) keep them readable. Give both units – unit= for the ages and marker_unit= for the readings (a Measurements read with units= gives its own) – and the model says what each effect is per, in effect_units and on its printout’s Effect units line. They are labels only: nothing is converted.

  • What the data can tell apart. For a straight line the slope is the item’s rate b, constant in age: its effect is found from how lifetimes differ between items with different rates, so it needs rates that vary between items (b in varying, or shifted by a covariate). The links are compared by aic(), the models having the same data, or all at once by compare_links (see Comparing the links).

A fleet of gearboxes, their oil debris read monthly for two years (ages in years). Each starts near 3 ppm and rises about 1 ppm a year, at a level and rate of its own; what wears a gearbox out here is how fast the debris is rising, each ppm a year multiplying the hazard by \(e^{1.0} = 2.7\):

gen = np.random.default_rng(5)


def gearboxes(n, alpha, beta, slope=0.0, cumulative=0.0):
    spread = np.array([[0.36, 0.06], [0.06, 0.25]])  # sds 0.6, 0.5
    lines = gen.multivariate_normal([3.0, 1.0], spread, size=n)
    target = gen.exponential(size=n)
    t = np.linspace(0.0, 40.0, 400001)
    items = []
    for k, (level, rate) in enumerate(lines):
        log_ratio = slope * rate + cumulative * (level * t + rate * t**2 / 2)
        h = beta / alpha * (t / alpha) ** (beta - 1) * np.exp(log_ratio)
        H = np.concatenate([[0], np.cumsum((h[1:] + h[:-1]) / 2 * np.diff(t))])
        life = np.interp(target[k], H, t)
        end = min(life, 2.0)                      # followed for two years
        ages = np.arange(0.0, end + 1e-9, 1 / 12)  # read monthly
        readings = level + rate * ages + gen.normal(0, 0.7, ages.size)
        outcome = "observed" if life <= 2.0 else "right_censored"
        items.append(
            ItemLifetime(k, **{outcome: end}, markers=dict(zip(ages, readings)))
        )
    return SurpyvalData.from_items(items)

Each candidate is fitted as its own fit would fit it, to the same maximum. A reading that acts later: a lag

Every link so far acts at once: the hazard at age \(t\) follows the path at \(t\). Some mechanisms have a lead time – debris in the oil now says what the bearing will do some hundreds of hours on. lag= shifts every link by a fixed time, in the lifetimes’ unit: JointPH(Weibull, link="value", lag=200.0) makes the hazard at age \(t\) follow the path at \(\max(t - 200, 0)\). Until the lag has passed the path is held at its age-0 value: the value link follows the value at age 0, and the slope and the area are 0 there. The cumulative hazard is integrated on each side of the lag (where the lagged path has a kink, and the slope link a jump), so the likelihood keeps its accuracy. JointAFT takes lag= the same way. With lag=0 (the default) the model is the one without a lag, number for number. The fitted model keeps its lag, prints it, saves it and predicts with it.

The lag is fixed, not estimated: the likelihood has a kink in it at every item’s age, so it is not smooth in the lag. Choose it from what you know of the mechanism, or fit the lags you have in mind and compare them by AIC (the same data and the same number of parameters):

fits = {lag: JointPH(Weibull, lag=lag).fit(data) for lag in (0.0, 0.25, 0.5)}
{lag: model.aic() for lag, model in fits.items()}

Expect the data to say little about a lag when the marker is close to a straight line: the line L earlier is the line now less b L, which the marker’s effect and the baseline largely absorb. On 300 simulated items whose hazard followed the line half a year earlier, the AICs of lags 0, 0.25 and 0.5 were within 0.6 of each other. A path that is infinite at age 0 (logarithmic, Lloyd-Lipow) cannot lag its value, and is refused.

Exposure that fades: a weight on the accumulated exposure

The "cumulative" link follows the area under the path from age 0, so an hour of exposure ten years ago counts like one last week. Where damage heals, is repaired or washes out, that is wrong: recent exposure matters and old exposure does not. weight= says how much of the exposure still counts, by how long ago it was:

\[\int_0^t w(t - s)\, m(s)\, ds, \qquad w(u) = e^{-u / \mathrm{scale}} \quad \text{or} \quad w(u) = 1 \text{ for } u \le \mathrm{width},\ 0 \text{ beyond.}\]
# exposure fades: what was accumulated u ago counts exp(-u / 0.5)
faded = JointPH(Weibull, link="cumulative", weight=("exponential", 0.5))
# only the last year's exposure counts
recent = JointPH(Weibull, link="cumulative", weight=("window", 1.0))
plain = JointPH(Weibull, link="cumulative")
fits = {name: f.fit(data) for name, f in
        {"faded": faded, "recent": recent, "all": plain}.items()}
{name: model.aic() for name, model in fits.items()}

The scale and the width are in the lifetimes’ unit of time. Like the lag they are fixed, not estimated: fit the ones you have in mind and compare them by AIC (the same data, the same number of parameters). A weight needs the cumulative link (alone or with others; the weight is on the area only) and is refused without it. cumulative_effect is still per unit of the marker times a unit of time (effect_units): with an exponential weight, a marker held at a level \(m\) for much longer than the scale has a weighted area of \(m \times \mathrm{scale}\), so the weighted area is then the recent level in other units; with a window, the area is the mean level over the window times its width. A window wider than every age in the data is the plain cumulative link. With a lag the weighted exposure acts lag later. The fitted model keeps its weight, prints it, saves it and predicts with it; compare_links keeps it on the candidates that have the cumulative link.

The weighted area is by quadrature for every path: a window by the plain area’s 15-point rule over the window, an exponential weight over the last 30 scales in three pieces of 15 points (what is older counts less than \(10^{-13}\)); both agree with the closed forms for line, quadratic and exponential paths to \(10^{-9}\). With a window the cumulative hazard is also integrated on each side of the age at which the window stops reaching back to age 0, where the weighted area has a kink.

A path’s parameters can be held common: JointPH(Weibull, QuadraticPath, varying=("a", "b")) gives each item its own level and rate, and every item the same curvature c. Up to five varying parameters are integrated on a grid of 7 points in each, and more by Laplace’s approximation (see how the integral is done). A path model fitted to a curve it cannot follow biases the joint model, as it does a degradation model: choose it as you would there (see Degradation Analysis).

How the reading acts: a higher failure rate, or faster ageing

Every model so far is a proportional hazards model (JointPH): a high reading multiplies the failure rate for as long as it is high, and when the reading comes back down the rate comes down with it, as though the high stretch had never happened. JointAFT (WeibullJointAFT for a line) is the other way a reading can act: it makes the item age faster. A month at a high reading uses up more than a month of life, and the life used up is not given back when the reading falls. It is the cumulative-exposure model of step-stress accelerated life tests, with the measured reading as the stress, so its effects mean what they mean in an accelerated test of the same product. It suits a reading of a stress that consumes life – a load, a temperature, a speed – and a biomarker that hastens the course of a disease; an effect reads as “a life so many times shorter”, which many find easier than a hazard ratio. (It is a statistical model of the readings and the lifetimes, not a model of the physics of the damage.)

By age \(t\) the item has used up the effective age

\[A_i(t) = \int_0^t \exp\left(\gamma' z_i + \zeta_v \bigl(m_i(s) - r\bigr) + \zeta_s\, m_i'(s)\right) ds,\]

and its survival and hazard are the baseline Weibull’s at that age:

\[S_i(t) = \exp\left(-\left(\frac{A_i(t)}{\alpha}\right)^{\beta} \right), \qquad h_i(t) = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{A_i(t)}{\alpha} \right)^{\beta - 1} A_i'(t).\]

A worked example, with made-up numbers. Say each ppm of oil debris above 3 ppm makes a gearbox age \(e^{0.3}\) times as fast (marker_effect 0.3, marker_reference 3), and the baseline is a Weibull of scale 6 years and shape 2. At 3 ppm a gearbox ages a year a year; at 5 ppm it ages \(e^{0.3 \times 2} = 1.82\) years a year. One that ran two years at 3 ppm and then a year at 5 ppm has an effective age of \(2 + 1.82 = 3.82\) years at the age of three, and a survival of \(\exp(-(3.82 / 6)^2) = 0.67\), against 0.78 for one that stayed at 3 ppm. If its debris then falls back to 3 ppm, the proportional hazards model returns it to the failure rate of a three-year-old (0.17 a year); the accelerated model keeps it a 3.82-year-old (0.21 a year).

The fitter is built and used as JointPH’s is: the same data, path models, varying, hazard_covariates and path_covariates, marker_reference, fit, fit_from_df, predictions and saving, and the same parameter names. A fleet of 300 gearboxes whose debris is a line in years, each ageing \(e^{0.6}\) times as fast for each ppm above 4:

from surpyval import WeibullJointAFT

gen_aft = np.random.default_rng(29)


def loaded(n, alpha=2.5, beta=2.0, effect=0.6, centre=4.0):
    spread = np.array([[0.36, 0.06], [0.06, 0.25]])  # sds 0.6, 0.5
    lines = gen_aft.multivariate_normal([3.0, 1.0], spread, size=n)
    items = []
    for k, (level, rate) in enumerate(lines):
        used = alpha * gen_aft.weibull(beta)  # the effective age it reaches
        # a line's effective age: e^(effect (level - centre)) (e^(k t) - 1) / k
        start, speed = np.exp(effect * (level - centre)), effect * rate
        grow = speed * used / start
        life = np.log1p(grow) / speed if grow > -1 else np.inf
        end = min(life, 2.0)                       # followed for two years
        ages = np.arange(0.0, end + 1e-9, 1 / 12)  # read monthly
        readings = level + rate * ages + gen_aft.normal(0, 0.7, ages.size)
        outcome = "observed" if life <= 2.0 else "right_censored"
        items.append(
            ItemLifetime(k, **{outcome: end}, markers=dict(zip(ages, readings)))
        )
    return SurpyvalData.from_items(items)

Four things to know:

  • The links. link="value" (the default) and "slope" (or both) set the ageing rate; "cumulative" is refused, because the effective age already accumulates the reading over the item’s life.

  • When the two models agree. With a reading that never changes and a Weibull baseline the accelerated model is the proportional hazards one, each effect multiplied by beta (an ageing rate \(e^{\zeta}\) is a hazard ratio \(e^{\beta\zeta}\)): the two fits of such a fleet have the same likelihood and parameters to 1e-4 (tests/univariate/joint/test_joint_aft.py). Once the reading moves they differ.

  • The history counts. The effective age at given depends on the whole path from age 0, so ages are ages since new, and the readings should start early in the item’s life: before the first reading the item’s path is the path model’s, extended back from its readings.

  • Which model. By what the reading is: a symptom of the present damage (proportional hazards), or a stress that uses up life (accelerated); and by aic() where the readings move enough for the data to tell.

Prediction: survival given the readings so far

The model predicts for one item: model.sf(x, Z, markers=..., given=...) is its survival to each age x given its covariates Z, its readings so far markers ({age: value}) and that it was still running at age given (1 at or before it), over the paths its readings leave possible; ff is its failure probability, and cb bounds either from the parameters’ uncertainty (the delta method on \(\log(-\log S)\), with the parameters’ covariance). One item’s dynamic prediction

model.predict(item) (or predict(Z, markers=..., given=...)) holds one item’s prediction as an object, for the questions asked of one asset: its survival, failure probability and hazard from now on given its readings (sf, ff, hf, df, Hf), its remaining life (remaining_life(p), the life left that it outlives with probability 1 - p, and qf the age), a Wald band from the parameters’ uncertainty (cb, the same as model.cb), its marker smoothed through the readings and projected beyond them (marker and marker_interval, the noise-free path’s mean and interval given the readings and that it reached its age now), and draws of its failure age (random). How good are the predictions

A joint model gives each running pump a chance of failing in the next six months. Before acting on those chances, check how good they were on pumps whose outcome is known. Three questions, at an age s (standing at one year old, say) and over a window w (the next 180 days), among the pumps still running at s:

  • Do the pumps that failed get the higher chances? The dynamic AUC (dynamic_auc()): take one pump that failed in the window and one that did not; the AUC is how often the model gave the one that failed the higher chance. 0.5 is a coin toss, 1 is perfect.

  • How far are the chances from what happened? The dynamic Brier score (dynamic_brier()): the mean squared difference between each pump’s chance and 1 if it failed in the window, 0 if not. Lower is better.

  • Does “30%” mean about 30%? Calibration (dynamic_calibration()): group the pumps by their chance, and set each group’s mean chance beside the share that failed.

Each pump’s chance is made from what was known at s: its readings up to age s, never those after. And each question is worth asking against a simpler model, to see whether the readings earn their cost: here a Weibull fitted to the ages alone, which gives every pump of the same age the same chance. A pump censored inside the window (still running when last seen) is not known to have failed or not. It is accounted for, as in Rizopoulos (2011) and Blanche et al. A quick first look: the two-stage fit

There is a quicker way to put the readings and the lifetimes together, in two steps. First, fit each pump’s line to its readings alone, pulling a pump with few readings towards the fleet’s average line (the less its own readings say, the further it is pulled; statisticians call the result a best linear unbiased predictor). Then fit the lifetimes by Weibull proportional hazards with each pump’s line as a time-varying covariate, as if the lines were known. It takes a fraction of a second, against seconds or minutes for the joint fit, and it is the method most published analyses used before joint models. It is fit(..., how="two-stage"), and it is exactly where the joint fit starts its search:

In formulas, step one fits \(y_{ij} = m(t_{ij}; \theta_i) + \varepsilon_{ij}\), \(\theta_i \sim N(\mu + \Delta z_i, D)\), to the readings alone (the path fitted to each item, then a moment estimate of the means and of \(D\)), and takes each item’s most likely path given its readings, \(\hat\theta_i\). Step two maximises the likelihood of the lifetimes with the hazard \(h_0(t) \exp(\gamma' z_i + \zeta\, m(t; \hat\theta_i))\) (or the slope or the area: the link), the path taken as a known covariate. JointAFT fitters take it too, with the accelerated survival part in step two.

The result is a joint model like any other – the same parameters, predictions (sf, cb, predict), accuracy measures and saving – and it says what it is (quick.how is "two-stage"). It is approximate in three ways, which the printout and summary() state:

  • Its estimates are biased. Step two forgets that each line is an estimate, and that a pump’s readings stop when it fails, so the pumps that failed early have the least certain lines.

  • Its standard errors are too small. Those of the survival part take each line as known, so they and the bands of its predictions (cb) are too narrow; the path part (step one’s moment estimates) has none (nan).

  • It has no likelihood. The two steps fit different things, so there is no likelihood of the whole model: neg_ll(), aic(), aic_c(), bic() and compare_links refuse it.

What it does and does not do

The joint model (#891) is the standard one. It has:

  • one marker, following any degradation path model (a straight line by default), with any number of its parameters varying between items (on a grid up to five, by Laplace’s approximation beyond) (multivariate normal on their natural scale, or on a log scale with a LinkedPathModel; every pair correlated, none, or blocks of them, random_effects=) and the rest common;

  • a Weibull proportional hazards survival part (JointPH(Weibull, path_model); WeibullJointPH is the line; other baselines are refused) in the path’s current value, its slope, the area under it, or more than one of them (link=);

  • or an accelerated (AFT) survival part on the Weibull (JointAFT(Weibull, path_model); WeibullJointAFT is the line), the path’s value, its slope or both setting how fast the item ages;

  • covariates in both parts (every path parameter’s mean, and the hazard) by default, or each in the parts named by hazard_covariates and path_covariates;

  • failures, right, left and interval censoring, with left and right truncation;

  • competing failure modes with the proportional hazards part (the failures’ event types): a baseline, coefficients and effects for each mode, any of them shared (shared=), see failure modes;

  • predictions for one item given its readings, with Wald (delta method) bounds, as arrays (sf, ff, cb) or as one item’s dynamic prediction (predict: the hazard, remaining life, the smoothed and projected marker, and draws);

  • the accuracy of those predictions at an age over a window (dynamic AUC, Brier score and calibration in surpyval.metrics, with cross-validation by unit);

  • the quick, approximate two-stage fit (how="two-stage") for a first look and for comparison.

Not yet (see #891’s roadmap): spline paths; other baselines (a log-normal or log-logistic one for the accelerated part, whose risk can rise and then fall, and spline baselines); several markers. The accelerated part has not yet been compared with R’s JM (method = "weibull-AFT-aGH", the same model) on reference data, nor had its prediction band put through a calibration study. The two-stage fit has not yet been compared with R’s two-step equivalent (nlme::lme, then survival with the fitted paths) on reference data, and its path part has no standard errors.

Parameters:

  • distribution (ParametricFitter) – surpyval.Weibull.

  • path_model (PathModel, optional) – The marker’s path. Default LinearPath.

  • varying (sequence of str, optional) – The path parameters that vary between items (their random effects, multivariate normal); the others are common to all items. Default all of them. Up to five, each item’s integral is on a grid by default; beyond five it is Laplace’s approximation (integration of JointPHFitter.fit()).

  • link (str or sequence of str, optional) – What of the marker’s path the hazard follows: "value" (the default: its current value, so risk follows the level), "slope" (its rate of change: risk follows how fast it is changing) or "cumulative" (the area under it from age 0: risk follows the accumulated exposure), or a list of them, each with an effect of its own (marker_effect, slope_effect, cumulative_effect).

  • lag (float, optional) – How long after the marker the hazard follows it, in the lifetimes’ unit of time (default 0: at once). With lag=200 the hazard at age t follows the path at t - 200, so a reading today says what the risk will be 200 hours on. Before the lag has passed the hazard follows the path held at its age-0 value: the value at age 0, a slope of 0 and an area of 0 (every link at max(t - lag, 0), the slope 0 before the lag). The lag is fixed, not estimated: the likelihood is not smooth in it. To choose one, fit each lag you have in mind and compare them by aic() (the same data, the same number of parameters).

  • weight (tuple, optional) – How much of the accumulated exposure (the "cumulative" link, which it needs) still counts, by how long ago it was. The default, None, counts all of it equally: an hour of exposure ten years ago like one last week. ("exponential", scale) makes exposure fade: what was accumulated u ago counts exp(-u / scale) of what is accumulated now (damage that heals or is repaired), so the hazard follows \(\int_0^t e^{-(t - s)/\mathrm{scale}} m(s) ds\). ("window", width) counts only the last width of it, the area under the path over (max(t - width, 0), t). The scale or width is in the lifetimes’ unit of time and, like the lag, is fixed, not estimated: fit each one you have in mind and compare them by aic().   • random_effects (str or sequence, optional) – Which of the varying path parameters are correlated between items. "full" (the default): every pair, a correlation each (corr_a_b, …; three for three varying parameters, ten for five); "diagonal": none, each varying independently of the others; or blocks of names, such as [("a", "b"), ("c",)]: those in a block are correlated with each other and with none outside it, every varying parameter in exactly one block. A correlation left out is 0, not estimated, and has no entry in params, summary() or the saved model, so aic() counts the parameters estimated. With many varying parameters and a fleet too small to tell their correlations apart, fewer can be what makes the fit identifiable; nested structures compare by aic() or the likelihood ratio of the same data.

compare_links(*args: Any, links: Any = None, metric: str = 'aic', **kwargs: Any) → LinkComparison

Fit the joint model with each of several links to the same data and compare them: does the risk follow the marker’s level, how fast it is changing, the exposure accumulated so far, or more than one?

Each candidate has this fitter’s baseline, path model and varying path parameters (its own link is not used) and is fitted as fit() fits, to the data given here, so the log-likelihoods and criteria compare directly. The result ranks the links by metric (lower is better), with each one’s difference from the best and its weight (table), and tests each nested pair, such as "value" against "value + slope", by the likelihood ratio (likelihood_ratio_tests()): see LinkComparison. Links less than 2 apart are no real preference, and the simpler of them is the best (fewer parameters, then the level before the slope before the area). The table says each row in words (reads_as) and verdict the whole comparison, such as “Risk follows how fast the marker is changing. Adding the level does not improve the fit. …”; to_dict() gives both as strict JSON. Which link a marker’s risk follows is a question for each dataset, and this is the way to ask it.

The value link is fitted first and the others start from its estimates of the path, which saves about a third of the time; a fit from that start that does not reach a verified maximum is made again from its own start, so each candidate is the maximum its own fit() finds.

A fit that is not a verified maximum is shown but neither ranked nor tested, with one warning naming it; a link that cannot be fitted is listed in failed with the reason, with one warning. A warning about the data that every fit gives is given once.

Parameters:

  • *args – The data and the options, as fit() takes them: a SurpyvalData of items with markers, or arrays with markers=; Z, path_covariates, hazard_covariates, unit, … A marker_reference is given to the links that follow the value only (it moves their alpha and nothing else).

  • **kwargs – The data and the options, as fit() takes them: a SurpyvalData of items with markers, or arrays with markers=; Z, path_covariates, hazard_covariates, unit, … A marker_reference is given to the links that follow the value only (it moves their alpha and nothing else).

  • links (list, optional) – The links to compare, two or more, each a link ("value", "slope" or "cumulative") or a list of them, each set once. Default the five: "value", "slope", "cumulative", ["value", "slope"] and ["value", "cumulative"], less those the path cannot take, with a warning: a logarithmic path, whose slope is unbounded at age 0, leaves out the two with the slope. A Lloyd-Lipow path, which also has no finite area from age 0, can take only the value, and is refused.

  • metric (str, optional) – The criterion the links are ranked by: "aic" (default), "aic_c" or "bic".

Returns:

The fitted candidates (models, by the link’s name, such as "value + slope"), the ranking (table), the best fit (best, best_link), likelihood_ratio_tests(), the verdict in words and to_dict().

Fit the joint model of the marker and the lifetime by maximum likelihood (or, with how="two-stage", by the quick and approximate two-stage estimate).

Each item’s marker follows the fitter’s path model with parameters of its own – those that vary between items multivariate normal about means that its covariates shift, the rest common – read with normal noise; its hazard is Weibull proportional hazards in the path’s current, noise-free value (or its slope or the area under it: the fitter’s link) and its covariates. A JointAFT() fitter’s (WeibullJointAFT) has the accelerated survival part in their place: the path and the covariates speed up the item’s ageing, and its survival is the Weibull’s at the age it has used up. The varying parameters are integrated out of each item’s likelihood by adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature (7 points a dimension, the nodes placed again at the new parameters until the log-likelihood stops moving; integration says on which nodes), and the likelihood is maximised with autograd’s gradient; the covariance is the inverse of its Hessian. The fit starts from the path fitted to each item alone, the spread of those fits, and a survival fit with each item’s predicted path.

That start is also a fit of its own, how="two-stage": first each item’s path from its readings alone, pulled towards the fleet’s average where it has few readings, then a Weibull proportional hazards (or accelerated) fit of the lifetimes with each item’s predicted path taken as a known covariate. It takes a fraction of a second, and it is biased: it forgets that each path is an estimate and that the readings stop when an item fails (in a simulation study, the marker’s effect was 8% low with readings every six months, where the joint fit’s was within 2.4%), and its standard errors (of the survival part only) are too small. It is for a first look and for comparison with the joint fit; the model says it is approximate (how), and has no likelihood to compare (aic(), bic() and neg_ll() are refused).

The data are usually a SurpyvalData of ItemLifetime records with markers: WeibullJointPH.fit(SurpyvalData.from_items(items)). Items may be failed (observed), still running (right_censored), found failed at an inspection (left_censored: failed by that age) or between two (interval_censored), and may have come under observation late (left_truncation) or be in the data only because they failed by an age (right_truncation): the likelihood is then that of an item that reached its entry age and failed by its right truncation, over the population’s paths. A covariate the data cannot determine (a constant, or a combination of others) is aliased: its coefficients are nan, with one warning.

Every covariate shifts every path parameter’s mean and enters the hazard, unless path_covariates or hazard_covariates say otherwise: a condition may change how fast the marker rises without changing the risk at a given reading (path only), or the risk at a given reading without changing the marker (hazard only).

compare_links(*args: Any, links: Any = None, metric: str = 'aic', **kwargs: Any) → LinkComparison

Fit the joint model with each of several links to the same data and compare them: does the risk follow the marker’s level, how fast it is changing, the exposure accumulated so far, or more than one?

Each candidate has this fitter’s baseline, path model and varying path parameters (its own link is not used) and is fitted as fit() fits, to the data given here, so the log-likelihoods and criteria compare directly. The result ranks the links by metric (lower is better), with each one’s difference from the best and its weight (table), and tests each nested pair, such as "value" against "value + slope", by the likelihood ratio (likelihood_ratio_tests()): see LinkComparison. Links less than 2 apart are no real preference, and the simpler of them is the best (fewer parameters, then the level before the slope before the area). The table says each row in words (reads_as) and verdict the whole comparison, such as “Risk follows how fast the marker is changing. Adding the level does not improve the fit. …”; to_dict() gives both as strict JSON. Which link a marker’s risk follows is a question for each dataset, and this is the way to ask it.

The value link is fitted first and the others start from its estimates of the path, which saves about a third of the time; a fit from that start that does not reach a verified maximum is made again from its own start, so each candidate is the maximum its own fit() finds.

A fit that is not a verified maximum is shown but neither ranked nor tested, with one warning naming it; a link that cannot be fitted is listed in failed with the reason, with one warning. A warning about the data that every fit gives is given once.

  • *args – The data and the options, as fit() takes them: a SurpyvalData of items with markers, or arrays with markers=; Z, path_covariates, hazard_covariates, unit, … A marker_reference is given to the links that follow the value only (it moves their alpha and nothing else).

Fit the joint model of the marker and the lifetime by maximum likelihood (or, with how="two-stage", by the quick and approximate two-stage estimate).

Each item’s marker follows the fitter’s path model with parameters of its own – those that vary between items multivariate normal about means that its covariates shift, the rest common – read with normal noise; its hazard is Weibull proportional hazards in the path’s current, noise-free value (or its slope or the area under it: the fitter’s link) and its covariates. A JointAFT() fitter’s (WeibullJointAFT) has the accelerated survival part in their place: the path and the covariates speed up the item’s ageing, and its survival is the Weibull’s at the age it has used up. The varying parameters are integrated out of each item’s likelihood by adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature (7 points a dimension, the nodes placed again at the new parameters until the log-likelihood stops moving; integration says on which nodes), and the likelihood is maximised with autograd’s gradient; the covariance is the inverse of its Hessian. The fit starts from the path fitted to each item alone, the spread of those fits, and a survival fit with each item’s predicted path.

That start is also a fit of its own, how="two-stage": first each item’s path from its readings alone, pulled towards the fleet’s average where it has few readings, then a Weibull proportional hazards (or accelerated) fit of the lifetimes with each item’s predicted path taken as a known covariate. It takes a fraction of a second, and it is biased: it forgets that each path is an estimate and that the readings stop when an item fails (in a simulation study, the marker’s effect was 8% low with readings every six months, where the joint fit’s was within 2.4%), and its standard errors (of the survival part only) are too small. It is for a first look and for comparison with the joint fit; the model says it is approximate (how), and has no likelihood to compare (aic(), bic() and neg_ll() are refused).

The data are usually a SurpyvalData of ItemLifetime records with markers: WeibullJointPH.fit(SurpyvalData.from_items(items)). Items may be failed (observed), still running (right_censored), found failed at an inspection (left_censored: failed by that age) or between two (interval_censored), and may have come under observation late (left_truncation) or be in the data only because they failed by an age (right_truncation): the likelihood is then that of an item that reached its entry age and failed by its right truncation, over the population’s paths. A covariate the data cannot determine (a constant, or a combination of others) is aliased: its coefficients are nan, with one warning.

Every covariate shifts every path parameter’s mean and enters the hazard, unless path_covariates or hazard_covariates say otherwise: a condition may change how fast the marker rises without changing the risk at a given reading (path only), or the risk at a given reading without changing the marker (hazard only).

class surpyval.univariate.joint.joint_model.JointModel

Bases: InformationCriteriaMixin, SerialisableMixin

A fitted joint model of a marker and a lifetime, returned by the JointPH() fitters, such as WeibullJointPH, and the JointAFT() fitters, such as WeibullJointAFT (survival says which).

Each item’s marker follows the degradation path model (LinearPath for WeibullJointPH; any PathModel) with parameters of its own: those that vary between items (varying) are multivariate normal about a mean that the covariates shift, the others are common to all items. Each reading is the path plus normal noise; the hazard is Weibull proportional hazards in the path’s current, noise-free value and the covariates:

\[y_{ij} = m(t_{ij}; \theta_i) + \varepsilon_{ij}, \qquad h_i(t) = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\left(\frac{t}{\alpha} \right)^{\beta - 1} \exp\left(\gamma' z_i + \zeta \left(m(t; \theta_i) - r\right)\right),\]

so alpha and beta are the baseline of an item with its marker at marker_reference (\(r\); the mean reading of the data unless the fit was given one) and its covariates at 0. With another link the hazard follows the path’s slope (slope_effect times \(m'(t)\)) or the area under it from age 0 (cumulative_effect times \(\int_0^t m(s) ds\)), in place of the value or beside it; the baseline is then at a slope and an area of 0. With a lag above 0 the hazard at age \(t\) follows each of these at \(\max(t - \mathrm{lag}, 0)\) (the path held at its age-0 value until the lag has passed, so the slope is 0 there): a reading acts lag later. With a weight the area counts older exposure for less, \(\int_0^t w(t - s) m(s) ds\) with \(w(u) = e^{-u/\mathrm{scale}}\) (("exponential", scale)) or \(w(u) = 1\) for \(u\) up to the width and 0 beyond (("window", width)).

A model fitted to failures with their modes (event types; causes lists them) has a hazard of this form for each failure mode \(k\), with its own \(\alpha_k\), \(\beta_k\), \(\gamma_k\) and \(\zeta_k\) (named "<cause>: alpha" and so on; those in shared are common to the modes), and one marker part. An item fails by the first mode to occur, so its survival, which sf, cb and predict give, is that of every mode together, \(\exp(-\sum_k H_{ik}(t))\).

In an accelerated model (survival == "AFT") the marker speeds up the item’s ageing in place of multiplying its hazard: by age \(t\) the item has used up the effective age

\[A_i(t) = \int_0^t \exp\left(\gamma' z_i + \zeta \left(m(s; \theta_i) - r\right)\right) ds, \qquad S_i(t) = \exp\left(-\left(A_i(t) / \alpha\right)^\beta \right),\]

so time at a high reading uses up life faster, and that life is not given back when the reading falls. The parameters have the same names; an effect is then the log of how many times faster the item ages per unit of the marker or the covariate (with the slope link, slope_effect times \(m'(s)\) in the exponent), and with a marker that never changes the model is the proportional hazards one with every effect divided by beta.

params holds, in the order of parameter_names: each path parameter’s mean (by the path model’s own names: a, b, …) followed by its covariates’ shifts (a_<cov>, …); the spread of the varying ones between items (sd_a, sd_b, …, corr_a_b, … of each pair correlated: every pair unless the fitter was given random_effects); sd_noise; the Weibull alpha and beta; the hazard’s covariate coefficients (<cov>); and marker_effect (\(\zeta\), the change in the log hazard per unit of the marker; slope_effect and cumulative_effect for those links). An aliased covariate’s entries are nan (aliased). neg_ll() is the negative log-likelihood of the readings and the lifetimes together (so aic() and bic() compare joint models of the same data).

sf(x, Z, markers=..., given=...) predicts for one item: its covariates Z, its readings so far and the age it was last seen running; sf(x, item) takes them from an ItemLifetime.

A model fitted with how="two-stage" (how) has the same parameters and predictions, from the approximate two-stage estimate: the paths fitted to the readings alone, then the survival part with each item’s predicted path taken as known. Its estimates are biased (most where the readings are few or the path curves); its survival part’s standard errors (and so the bounds of its predictions) are too small, and its marker part has none (nan; the bounds hold it at its estimates). It has no likelihood of the whole model, so neg_ll(), aic(), aic_c() and bic() are refused. The printout and the summary say so.

class surpyval.univariate.joint.dynamic.DynamicPrediction(model: JointModel, Z: Any, markers: Any, given: Any)

Bases: object

One item’s failure-age distribution given its covariates, its marker readings so far and that it was still running at age age: the dynamic prediction of a joint model, which JointModel.predict returns.

The item’s path parameters are averaged over their distribution given its readings and its survival to age (adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature at the mode), so sf(x) is P(T > x | T > age, readings): 1 up to age, then falling. The functions take ages (since new, as the model’s), not times from now; the remaining life is remaining_life(). The estimates are at the fitted parameters; cb() adds their uncertainty.

Examples

>>> import numpy as np
>>> from surpyval import ItemLifetime, SurpyvalData, WeibullJointPH
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
>>> items = []
>>> for k in range(60):
...     start, rate = rng.normal(2.0, 0.4), rng.normal(0.02, 0.005)
...     life = 100 * rng.weibull(2.0) * np.exp(-0.9 * (start - 2))
...     end = min(life, 120.0)
...     ages = np.arange(0.0, end, 10.0)
...     y = start + rate * ages + rng.normal(0, 0.2, ages.size)
...     outcome = "observed" if life < 120 else "right_censored"
...     items.append(
...         ItemLifetime(k, **{outcome: end},
...                      markers=dict(zip(ages, y)))
...     )
>>> model = WeibullJointPH.fit(SurpyvalData.from_items(items))
>>> pred = model.predict(markers={0: 2.1, 20: 2.5, 40: 3.0}, given=40)
>>> float(pred.sf(40))
1.0
>>> life = pred.remaining_life([0.1, 0.5, 0.9])
>>> bool(0 < life[0] < life[1] < life[2])
True

The cumulative hazard from age to x, -log sf(x).

cb(x: ArrayLike, on: str = 'sf', alpha_ci: float = 0.05, bound: str = 'two-sided') → NDArray

Wald confidence bounds on the item’s conditional sf, ff or Hf at ages x, from the parameters’ covariance: the delta method on log(-log sf), as JointModel.cb, which it is (Hf is -log of the bounds on sf). Up to age the bounds are the estimate.

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – The ages.

  • on ({'sf', 'ff', 'Hf'}, optional) – The function bounded ('R' and 'F' are 'sf' and 'ff'). Default 'sf'.

  • alpha_ci (float, optional) – The total tail probability. Default 0.05.

  • bound ({'two-sided', 'lower', 'upper'}, optional) – Default 'two-sided', [lower, upper] on a last axis.

df(x: ArrayLike) → NDArray

The density of the failure age at x given the readings and survival to age: hf(x) sf(x) (0 up to age).

ff(x: ArrayLike) → NDArray

The probability the item has failed by age x, given it was running at age: 1 - sf(x) (0 up to age).

hf(x: ArrayLike) → NDArray

The item’s hazard at age x given its readings and that it reached x: the hazard at x averaged over its path parameters’ distribution given the readings and survival to x (0 up to age, where the item is known to be running).

marker(x: ArrayLike) → Any

The mean of the item’s true (noise-free) marker at ages x, given its readings and survival to age: its smoothed path up to age, and beyond it the path projected (not conditioned on surviving to x).

marker_interval(x: ArrayLike, alpha_ci: float = 0.05) → NDArray

An equal-tailed 1 - alpha_ci interval for the item’s true marker at ages x, mean plus or minus a normal quantile of its standard deviation, at the fitted parameters: [lower, upper] on a last axis. It is the spread of the item’s own path, not of a new reading (which adds the noise, sd_noise), and for a path not linear in its parameters a normal approximation.

qf(p: ArrayLike) → NDArray

The age by which the item has failed with probability p, given it was running at age: age for p = 0, inf for 1 and where the survival never falls to 1 - p (a path that falls away can leave a chance of never failing), nan with a warning outside [0, 1].

random(size: int, random_state: int | Generator | None = None) → NDArray

Failure ages drawn from the prediction (each after age; inf for a draw beyond where the survival stops falling), by the quantile function of uniform draws.

Parameters:

  • size (int) – The number of draws.

  • random_state (int or numpy.random.Generator, optional) – None (the default) draws from numpy’s global generator, so np.random.seed reproduces it; an int or a generator gives a stream of its own.

remaining_life(p: ArrayLike = 0.5) → Any

The remaining life from age that the item outlives with probability 1 - p: qf(p) - age. The default is the median remaining life; remaining_life([0.05, 0.5, 0.95]) gives it with a 90% prediction interval (of the item’s life, at the fitted parameters: cb() bounds the survival for the parameters’ uncertainty).

sf(x: ArrayLike) → NDArray

P(T > x | T > age, readings): the probability the item is still running at age x (1 up to age).

to_dict(x: ArrayLike | None = None, alpha_ci: float = 0.05) → dict

The prediction as a dict of native Python values, strict JSON, for a report or a chart: an infinite or missing number is None, and recorded under "non_finite" (as every SurPyval to_dict does).

Parameters:

  • x (array_like, optional) – The ages to evaluate the functions at. The default is 101 ages from age to about the 0.99 quantile.

  • alpha_ci (float, optional) – The total tail probability of the two-sided bounds on sf and of the marker’s interval. Default 0.05.

Returns:

age; covariates, {name: value}; markers, the readings as [age, value] pairs; alpha_ci; x, the ages; sf, ff and hf at them; sf_lower and sf_upper, the Wald bounds of cb() (None where the model has no covariance); remaining_life, {"p": [0.1, 0.5, 0.9], "life": [...]}; and marker, the item’s path from its first reading (or age) to the last age, {"x", "mean", "lower", "upper"}; and how, how the model was fitted ("two-stage" for the approximate estimate, whose bounds are too narrow).
