Interval-Censored Cox Regression

Often a failure is not seen when it happens, only found at the next look:

  • a crack found at an inspection was not there at the one before;

  • a patient’s test is negative at one visit and positive at the next;

  • a buried pipe is found leaking at a survey, years after it was laid.

The time of the event is then known only to lie between two inspections. These are interval-censored data (c = 2, with x given as [left, right] pairs; see Conventions). An event before the first look is left censored (c = -1), and a unit still fine at its last look is right censored (c = 1).

The Cox model (Cox Proportional Hazards) cannot use such rows: its partial likelihood compares, at each event time, the unit that failed with the units still at risk, and with intervals nobody knows when the event happened or who was at risk then. IntervalCoxPH keeps what makes the Cox model attractive – the covariates multiply the hazard, and the shape of the baseline is left to the data – and fits the full likelihood instead.

In plain words

  • What it estimates. One hazard ratio per covariate, as in Cox regression: exp(coef) = 2 means the event comes at twice the rate, at every age.

  • The baseline. The survival curve of a unit with all covariates at 0 is estimated without a formula, the way the Turnbull estimator estimates one group’s curve from intervals: it can only drop inside the Turnbull intervals, the stretches of time where the data say failures happened. Where exactly inside such an interval the drop happens, the data cannot say; the curve is shown dropping at the interval’s end.

  • How sure it is. The standard errors come from the profile likelihood: how fast the fit’s likelihood falls as a coefficient moves away from its estimate, with the baseline re-fitted at each value. param_cb gives Wald intervals from them, profile likelihood-ratio intervals (method="lr") or bootstrap intervals (method="bootstrap").

The model

\[S(x \mid Z) = S_0(x)^{\exp(\beta' Z)}\]

Row \(i\) adds the log of the probability of what was seen: for an interval \((l_i, r_i]\), \(S(l_i \mid Z_i) - S(r_i \mid Z_i)\); for a left-censored row \(1 - S(r_i \mid Z_i)\); for a right-censored row \(S(l_i \mid Z_i)\); for an exact time the drop of the curve there. With an entry time \(t_{l,i}\) (left truncation) the probability is divided by \(S(t_{l,i} \mid Z_i)\). \(S_0\) puts mass only on the Turnbull intervals of all the rows pooled, and is maximised together with \(\beta\) (Finkelstein 1986; Huang 1996). It is the model of R’s icenReg::ic_sp(model = "ph"), and SurPyval’s fits agree with it.

The standard errors are the profile likelihood’s (Murphy and van der Vaart 2000): the curvature of \(p\ell(\beta) = \max_{S_0} \ell(\beta, S_0)\) by second differences, with a step of \(1/\sqrt{N}\) standard deviations of each covariate. With interval censoring the baseline is estimated more slowly than the coefficients, so the curvature of the likelihood in the baseline’s masses is not a valid variance; the profile likelihood’s is.

An example: a condition found at check-ups

Two hundred units, half of them treated (treated), with an age at the start (age, centred), are checked every one to three months; each check says only whether a condition has appeared yet. The data are simulated, so the truth is known: treatment halves the hazard (\(\beta = \log 0.5 \approx -0.69\)) and each year of age raises it by 5% (\(\beta \approx 0.049\)).

import numpy as np
import surpyval as sp

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n = 200
treated = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)
age = rng.normal(0, 10, n)
life = 12 * (rng.exponential(size=n)
             / np.exp(np.log(0.5) * treated + 0.049 * age)) ** (1 / 1.3)
x, c = np.empty((n, 2)), np.empty(n, dtype=int)
for i in range(n):
    visits = np.cumsum(np.r_[rng.uniform(0, 2), rng.uniform(1, 3, 40)])
    visits = visits[visits <= 36]
    if life[i] <= visits[0]:
        x[i], c[i] = visits[0], -1        # before the first check
    elif life[i] > visits[-1]:
        x[i], c[i] = visits[-1], 1        # still fine at the last
    else:
        k = np.searchsorted(visits, life[i]) - 1
        x[i], c[i] = (visits[k], visits[k + 1]), 2
Z = np.column_stack([treated, age])
print(np.bincount(c + 1))  # left, (exact), right, interval

[  7   0  18 175]

model = sp.IntervalCoxPH.fit(x, Z, c=c)
model

A parametric PH model of the same data assumes a Weibull baseline. Where it fits, its coefficients are close, and it has bounds on the curves and diagnose(); the semi-parametric model makes no assumption about the baseline’s shape.

weibull = sp.WeibullPH.fit(x, Z, c=c)
print(weibull.params[-2:].round(3), model.beta.round(3))

[-0.551  0.05 ] [-0.563  0.051]

Exact and right-censored data

The model takes exact times too (c = 0). With only exact and right-censored rows, it is the grouped-data proportional hazards model (Prentice and Gloeckler 1978): each event time is a drop of a discrete survival curve, where CoxPH’s partial likelihood treats the baseline as a continuous hazard. The coefficients are close and draw together as the data grow, but are not equal; for such data CoxPH is the usual choice, with its residuals, strata, time-varying covariates and diagnostics.

What it does not do

  • Bounds on the curves. cb refuses: no variance of the interval-censored baseline is offered. Bound the coefficients with param_cb, or use a parametric PH model (WeibullPH), whose cb bounds the curves.

  • Right truncation. Refused; a parametric PH model takes t=[tl, tr].

  • Proportional odds. The semi-parametric proportional odds model (ProportionalOdds) takes exact and right-censored data only; for interval-censored data use a parametric PO model (PO(LogLogistic), WeibullPO).

  • Diagnostics. diagnose() refuses and says what to check instead: a parametric PH fit’s diagnose(), and each group’s predicted curve against its Turnbull estimate.
