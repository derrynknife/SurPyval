Model Diagnostics

A fitted model is only as good as its assumptions. Every regression model in SurPyval has one method that checks them, diagnose(): it runs the checks the model’s assumptions call for and returns a report saying what holds, what does not, and what to do about it.

d = model.diagnose(alpha=0.05)   # columns=[...] checks a subset
d.table          # one row per check: test, statistic, p, verdict, covariates
d.advice         # plain-English remedies, each naming the SurPyval feature
d.plot()         # the visual checks (matplotlib is imported only here)
d.ok             # True if nothing failed
d.tables         # a check's own table, by check name (Cox: R's cox.zph table)
d.raise_if_failed()   # for pipelines
d.to_dict()      # machine-readable, strict JSON

The name is diagnose, not check_assumptions: besides the assumptions it looks at the fit, the functional form and the rows that drive the estimates. It is the one way to check a model’s assumptions: the Cox model’s check_ph(), deprecated in v0.26 and removed in v0.28, was replaced by diagnose()’s proportional-hazards test (see The proportional-hazards table).

The families, each with its section below: Cox proportional hazards (#868); parametric proportional hazards and accelerated failure time models (#869); proportional odds and additive hazards (#870); accelerated life models (#871); shared frailty (#872); recurrent events (#873); competing risks (#874); univariate distributions (#875); distributional regressions; and Aalen’s additive hazards model. The Cox and parametric proportional hazards and accelerated failure time reports also check the calibration of the predicted survival (#881). A few models have no checks yet, and their diagnose() refuses, saying what to check the model with meanwhile: Buckley-James (diagnose an AFT fit of the same data). Bayesian fits have theirs: see Bayesian fits.

Verdicts

Each row of d.table is a test or a screen, with a verdict:

  • fail: a test whose p-value is below alpha: evidence, at that level, against the assumption;

  • warn: a test whose p-value is borderline, at least alpha but below 2 * alpha, or that could not be computed on these data (its p is nan and its detail says why); or a screen (influence, outliers) that found something worth a look;

  • ok: otherwise.

A screen is not a test and never fails. Some screens carry a test’s p-value but choose between models rather than check an assumption, so a rejection is good news for the model fitted: the trend tests of a model whose rate changes, the tests of perfect and minimal repair of an imperfect-repair model, and the test of no frailty. Such a row is ok when its test rejects the simpler model and warns when it does not (the simpler model may do as well); it never fails. The calibration rows, the AIC comparisons and the extrapolation screen are screens too. d.ok is True when no row failed, so warnings do not stop a pipeline. Every row is judged at alpha on its own: with a dozen rows some fail by chance about alpha of the time each, even when every assumption holds, so read a lone failure with a p-value near alpha with that in mind.

Two covariates fail the proportional-hazards test: age on the Kaplan-Meier scale of time and wexp (work experience) on both. The advice depends on the kind of covariate: wexp is binary, so it can be stratified on; age is continuous, so its effect can be allowed to change with time instead.

for advice in d.advice:
    print('-', advice, '\n')

- The effect of wexp changes with time (it fails the proportional-hazards test). Stratify on wexp: fit with CoxPH.fit(..., strata=<the wexp column>) and leave wexp out of Z (from a DataFrame, CoxPH.fit_from_df(..., strata_col="wexp")). Each level then has its own baseline hazard, and the model no longer estimates an effect for wexp. 

- The effect of age changes with time (it fails the proportional-hazards test). Give it a time-varying coefficient, beta + gamma log(t): refit with CoxPH.fit(..., time_interaction={'age': "log"}) (or "t" for beta + gamma t; see where its scaled Schoenfeld residuals in d.plot('proportional_hazards') bend), then read the interaction's row in summary() and draw the coefficient over time with time_varying_effect('age').plot(). For an effect that changes at one or more times instead, split each unit's follow-up there and fit CoxPH.fit_tvc on the pieces, with one column per period holding age in that period and 0 elsewhere. Or bin age (with pandas.cut, say) and stratify on the bins (CoxPH.fit(..., strata=...)). Or fit a parametric accelerated failure time model, such as WeibullAFT.fit or LogNormalAFT.fit, which does not assume proportional hazards.

The proportional-hazards table

d.tables['proportional_hazards'] is the proportional-hazards test as R’s cox.zph prints it: a 1-d.f. test per covariate (every covariate, whatever columns chose), named as summary() names it, and the joint GLOBAL test on the last row, with columns statistic, df and p. It is on the scale of time diagnose(transform=...) names: "km" (the default, one minus the left-continuous Kaplan-Meier estimate), "rank", "identity" or "log", as R defines them; the table’s attrs['transform'] says which. The table’s rows judge the test on km and rank either way, and on the transform too when it is "identity" or "log".

The checks

proportional_hazards

For each covariate, whether its coefficient changes with time: the score test of adding \(Z_j g(t)\) to the model, on two scales of time \(g\), km (one minus the left-continuous Kaplan-Meier estimate) and rank (the rank of each time); and the joint GLOBAL test on each. A covariate fails if it fails on either scale. These are the tests of R’s survival::cox.zph (version 3.0 and later) and agree with it to the precision of the fit, under Efron and Breslow ties, delayed entry and time-varying covariates; d.tables['proportional_hazards'] is the test on one scale, the transform given to diagnose(), as cox.zph prints it, and with transform='identity' or 'log' the rows test on that scale too. A penalised fit (CoxPH.fit_penalised) refuses: it has no p-values. (lifelines, and R before version 3.0, compute Grambsch and Therneau’s approximation of it, whose statistics differ a little in small samples.)

functional_form

For each covariate with three or more distinct values, the score test of adding its square: whether it acts linearly on the log hazard (a two-valued covariate’s effect is linear by construction). R reports the same statistic as coxph(..., init = c(coef(fit), 0), iter.max = 0)$score.

influence

For each covariate, the largest |dfbeta| / se: how many standard errors the coefficient moves when one row is dropped (the one-step approximation). It warns when one row moves a coefficient by more than one standard error.

outliers

The deviance residuals. A row is an outlier when its |deviance residual| exceeds the two-sided Bonferroni normal cutoff for the number of observations \(N\), \(z_{1 - \alpha / (2N)}\) (3.66 for 200 observations at \(\alpha = 0.05\)), so that a model that fits flags a row about \(\alpha\) of the time whatever \(N\). It warns when any row is flagged.

overall_fit

A plot only: the Nelson-Aalen cumulative hazard of the Cox-Snell residuals against the line \(y = x\) of the unit exponential. For a Cox model the residuals sum to the number of events by construction, so there is no useful test of them.

A covariate with two distinct values is binary, one with three to five categorical, one with more continuous. A binary or categorical covariate that fails proportional hazards is advised stratification (strata=, or strata_col= from a DataFrame; for a formula’s coded level, stratification on the variable it codes); a continuous one a time-varying effect through CoxPH.fit_tvc, binning and stratifying, or a parametric accelerated failure time model (WeibullAFT, LogNormalAFT). A covariate that fails its functional form is advised a transform or bins in a formula. When the rows are grouped by subject (a fit_tvc model) the report offers the cluster-robust standard errors of summary(robust=True); a model without clusters says it has none.

What the Cox checks support

Every tie method (Efron’s and Breslow’s exactly; with "exact" and "kalbfleisch-prentice" the residuals use Breslow’s risk sets, as R’s cox.zph does, and the report notes it), delayed entry (tl), counts (n) and time-varying covariates (fit_tvc). diagnose() refuses, saying what to do: a model restored with from_dict (it has no data to check), one with an aliased coefficient and one whose fit has no finite maximum.

Adding a family

Each family is one module in surpyval/diagnostics that lists its checks and the advice rules for their failures; the report, the verdicts, the advice engine, columns, alpha and the plot layout are shared, and the models already have the method. The step-by-step guide is the docstring of surpyval.diagnostics, and surpyval/diagnostics/cox.py is the family to copy.

API

The report model.diagnose() returns: Diagnosis (#867).

One report for every model family. It holds the table of checks, the advice and the notes the family’s checks produced, and draws the family’s plots on request; it knows nothing about any one family. See surpyval.diagnostics for the verdicts and for how a family plugs in.

The verdict of a test with p-value p at level alpha, the one rule every family’s tests share:

  • "fail": p < alpha, evidence against the assumption at the chosen level;

surpyval.univariate.regression.proportional_hazards.diagnostics.zph_score_test(model: SemiParametricRegressionModel, transform: str = 'km') → dict

Test the proportional-hazards assumption (Grambsch-Therneau), as R’s survival::cox.zph does since version 3.0.

The test model.diagnose() reports (its proportional_hazards rows, and the table d.tables["proportional_hazards"]); this is the function it is computed by, for code that needs the raw numbers.

For each covariate, the score test of adding Z_j * g(t) to the model, at the fitted coefficients and a zero coefficient for the added term: a non-zero coefficient of Z_j * g(t) is an effect that changes with time, a violation of proportional hazards. The GLOBAL test is the score test of adding all of them. Each is the efficient score test, with the full information matrix of the enlarged model at every event time.

Parameters:

transform ({"km", "rank", "identity", "log"}) – The function g of time, as R defines it: "km" (the default, the scale-free choice) is 1 - S(t-), one minus the left-continuous Kaplan-Meier estimate from all the data; "rank" the rank of each row’s time among every row, censored ones too; "identity" the time and "log" its log (positive event times only). See _zph_times().

Returns:

"global", {"statistic", "df", "p_value"}; "per_covariate", a list of such dicts; and "transform", the transform used. Per-covariate tests have 1 degree of freedom; the global test is the joint p-d.f. test. A small p_value is evidence against proportional hazards. When the model was fit from a DataFrame, per-covariate entries carry the feature name.

Return type:

dict

Notes

The statistics are R’s cox.zph (survival 3.0 and later) to the precision of the fit, under Efron and Breslow ties, delayed entry and time-varying covariates (surpyval/tests/reference). Under Efron ties the variances are those of the Efron pseudo-risk sets; under any other tie method ("breslow", "exact", "kalbfleisch-prentice") they are Breslow’s, as R uses for every method but Efron’s. g is centred on its mean over the events, as R centres it, which matters only where the fit’s score is not exactly 0 (the exact methods). Counts n are frequency weights: a row with n = 3 is three tied observations, as in the data written out row by row. Where the transformed event times have no spread (every event at one time) the test is undefined and every statistic is nan.

Until v0.24 this was the approximation of R before version 3.0 and of lifelines, which puts the average covariate variance in place of the variance at each event time; the two agree closely in large samples and can differ in small ones.

The checks of a proportional hazards model

proportional_hazards

For each covariate, the likelihood-ratio test of a step in its coefficient at the median failure time \(\tau\): the model with hazard \(h_0(t) \exp(\beta'Z + [t > \tau](g_0 + \gamma Z_j))\) against the one with \(\gamma = 0\) (1 df); and the joint GLOBAL test of a step in every coefficient (one df per covariate). Both models step the level of the baseline at \(\tau\) too (\(g_0\)), so the test does not depend on where a covariate’s origin is. Drawn as each covariate’s coefficient in each quarter of the failure times, with its Wald interval. For a Weibull or exponential PH model the statistics agree with R’s to 1e-6 (a Poisson model of the follow-up split at \(\tau\), whose likelihood is the same), with delayed entry too.

functional_form

For each covariate with three or more distinct values, the likelihood-ratio test of adding its square (R’s survreg(... + I(z^2)) against survreg(...), to 1e-6), and the martingale residuals against the covariate.

influence and outliers

The screens of the Cox family: the largest one-step |dfbeta| / se of each coefficient (the observed information times each unit’s score, R’s residuals(survreg(...), type = 'dfbeta')) warns above 1, and a deviance residual beyond the Bonferroni normal cutoff warns.

baseline_distribution

Two screens. The AIC of the model against that of each other built-in baseline (ExponentialPH, NormalPH, WeibullPH, GumbelPH, LogisticPH, LogNormalPH, GammaPH) refitted to the same data, as fit_best compares distributions; and against the accelerated failure time model of the same baseline (not for the Weibull and the exponential, whose PH and AFT models are one model). Each warns when the other’s AIC is more than 2 lower; the statistic is the fitted model’s AIC less the other’s. Drawn as the Cox-Snell residuals on exponential probability paper, and the AIC of each candidate.

The checks of an accelerated failure time model

error_distribution

The time on the baseline’s scale, \(e^{\beta'Z} x\), follows the baseline distribution: drawn on its probability paper with the fitted line (for the Weibull and the log-normal, the standardised residuals of R’s survreg). The same two AIC screens: the other built-in error distributions (WeibullAFT, LogNormalAFT, …), and the proportional hazards model of the same baseline.

common_shape

For each categorical covariate (two to five distinct values, or the dummy columns a formula codes one factor with), the likelihood-ratio test of one shape for every level against a shape per level (the Weibull’s beta, the log-normal’s sigma, the gamma’s alpha; L - 1 df for L levels), which is R’s survreg(... + strata(g)) against survreg(...) for the Weibull and the log-normal (to 1e-6, with interval censoring too); drawn as each level’s standardised residuals on the baseline’s paper. The exponential has no shape to test, and a level with fewer than two distinct failure times has too few to estimate one (the test is then not computed, and warns).

functional_form

As for the proportional hazards model, of log time.

The advice: a time-varying effect (fit_tvc), an AFT model or, for a binary or categorical covariate, a stratified Cox model for a covariate that breaks proportional hazards; each level fitted on its own, or a Cox model stratified on it, for a shape that varies by level; a transform or bins in a formula for a covariate that is not linear; the better baseline, or the PH or AFT model, that the AIC screens found; and a look at an influential or badly predicted row.

What these checks support

Every kind of censoring, delayed entry and right truncation, counts (n), center=True, formulas, and any baseline with the exp(beta'Z) link (PH(dist), AFT(dist); the AIC comparison then sets the fitted baseline against the built-in ones). The martingale and deviance residuals are of the exact and right-censored rows that are not right truncated (the report says how many it left out); the tests use every row. A fit to time-varying covariates (fit_tvc) is checked too (below). diagnose() refuses, saying what to do: a fit_tvc model fitted with weights (w=), a model restored with from_dict, one with an aliased coefficient, and one whose fit is not a verified maximum (the tests compare it with larger models). A custom covariate link (any other than exp(beta'Z), such as a phi of your own given to ProportionalHazardsFitter) is refused for good, since every test is built on that link: refit with PH(dist) or AFT(dist), diagnose the refit and compare the two fits by aic(). The accelerated life models (#871) and the proportional odds and additive hazards models (#870) have their own families.

Time-varying covariates (fit_tvc)

A model fitted with fit_tvc (or fit_tvc_timeline and their _from_df forms) gets the same checks. A unit there has several rows, one for each stretch of time its covariates held still, so each residual is the unit’s, added up over its rows: how much hazard the model says the unit went through over the time it was watched, compared with whether it failed. The tests are the same likelihood-ratio tests, on the fit’s own likelihood. Influence and outliers are screened unit by unit and name the unit by the identifier fit_tvc was given. The functional form plot is of each row against the covariate value on that row, since a unit’s covariate can change. A fit with constant covariates, one row per unit or a unit split into rows with the same values, gets the residuals, statistics and verdicts of the ordinary fit.

How each check carries over:

  • The residuals. For a proportional hazards model a unit’s Cox-Snell residual is the sum over its rows of \(H(\text{stop} \mid Z_{row}) - H(\text{start} \mid Z_{row})\), and its martingale residual is its event indicator less that. For an accelerated failure time model the unit’s standardised residual is its accumulated age \(\psi = \sum_{rows} e^{\beta' Z_{row}} (\text{stop} - \text{start})\), the time on the baseline’s scale, as the fit’s likelihood accumulates it; a row’s share of the unit’s cumulative hazard is \(H_0(\psi(\text{stop})) - H_0(\psi(\text{start}))\). Against R’s flexsurvreg(Surv(start, stop, event) ~ ..., dist = "weibullPH") on the heart data the per-unit Cox-Snell and martingale residuals agree to 1e-5 (the two fits’ maximisations) and the step and quadratic tests to 1e-6.

  • proportional_hazards, functional_form and common_shape are the same likelihood-ratio tests, of the time-varying fit’s likelihood. The square in functional_form is of the covariate’s value on each row. common_shape gives each unit one shape (an accelerated failure time model evaluates the baseline once, at the unit’s accumulated age), so it tests a categorical covariate that keeps one level through each unit, and a note names any that changes within a unit.

  • functional_form’s plot is of each row’s martingale residual (its event indicator less the row’s share of the hazard) against the row’s covariate value, as Therneau and Grambsch (2000, section 5.6) draw it for start-stop data.

  • influence is each unit’s one-step change of the coefficients, its score the sum of its rows’; outliers is each unit’s deviance residual, with the Bonferroni cutoff over the units.

  • The AIC screens refit each other model with its own fit_tvc to the same rows. An accelerated failure time model refuses delayed entry and gaps between a unit’s rows, and is then left out with a note.

  • calibration is not made: a unit’s predicted survival depends on its covariate path, not on one row (as for CoxPH.fit_tvc).

The checks

distribution

The Cox-Snell residuals \(H(x_i \mid z_i)\), each unit’s fitted cumulative hazard at its own parameters, drawn on exponential probability paper (they agree with flexsurv’s coxsnell_flexsurvreg to 1e-4, with delayed entry too; their transform \(\Phi^{-1}(1 - e^{-H})\) is the normalised quantile residual of gamlss’s plot(), matched to 1e-5). As for the PH family they have no test of their own. The screen, which warns and never fails: the AIC of the model against each other built-in distribution fitted as a distributional regression to the same data, each with the model’s life-parameter columns on its own scale, location or rate and the model’s other columns on its other parameters; it warns when one is more than 2 lower.

functional_form

For each covariate with three or more distinct values, on each parameter whose predictor holds it, the likelihood-ratio test of adding its square to that predictor (flexsurv’s flexsurvreg(..., anc = ...) with and without I(z^2), to 1e-6). Drawn as each unit’s score residual \(\partial \log L_i / \partial \eta_{ik}\) against the covariate with a lowess trend: for a scale it is the martingale residual times \(-\beta\), and every row has one, whatever its censoring.

influence

For each coefficient, the largest one-step |dfbeta| / se (the observed information times each unit’s score); it warns above 1.

The advice: the better distribution the AIC screen found (LogNormalDistributional.fit, say), a transform of a covariate on the one parameter where it is not linear (fit_from_df(..., formulas={"beta": "np.log(load) + ..."})), and a look at an influential row. In a simulation with n = 200 and a quarter of the units censored, the quadratic-term test rejects a true model at close to its 5% level (5.3% on the scale and 4.4% on the shape, in 1000 replicates), and found a shape that bends with the load, and the AIC screen log-normal data fitted with a Weibull, in all of 200 (tests/calibration/test_size_distributional_diagnostics.py).

Every kind of censoring, delayed entry and right truncation, counts, center=True, fixed= (the AIC comparison is then left out, and a fixed coefficient has no influence row) and a parameter with only some of the columns are supported. diagnose() refuses, saying what to do instead: a fit with weights w (its likelihood-ratio statistics are not chi-squared; diagnose the unweighted fit), a penalised fit, a model restored with from_dict, one with an aliased coefficient and one whose fit is not a verified maximum. Whether a covariate left off a parameter belongs on it is not checked: refit with it there and use likelihood_ratio_test(parameter=...).

The checks

proportional_odds (proportional odds models), constant_effects (additive hazards models)

Follow-up is split into four periods at the quartiles of the event times, and \(g(t)\) is the period a time is in (0 to 3; the notes give the cuts). For each covariate the model is refitted with the added term \(\theta\, u(Z)\, g(t)\), where \(u(Z)\) is the covariate less its mean or, for a covariate with three to five values, the indicator of each level after the first, less its mean; the test is of \(\theta = 0\), the same coefficient in every period. For a binary or categorical covariate of a proportional odds model that is whether the log-odds of its groups stay parallel. The GLOBAL row adds every covariate’s term at once.

The added covariate changes at the cut times, which every one of these estimators fits exactly by splitting each row into one row per period, entering at the period’s start, so the refit is the model’s own estimator. For the proportional odds models and the parametric additive ones the test is the likelihood-ratio test of the two fits (for ProportionalOdds, of its profile likelihood). For Lin and Ying’s model, which has no likelihood, it is the Wald test of \(\theta\) from the closed-form estimating equations with Lin and Ying’s sandwich variance; R’s timereg::aalen, with const() terms on the split rows, gives the same statistic. It is the test that cox.zph makes for a Cox model (there the score test of \(Z\, g(t)\), which is a trend test of the Schoenfeld residuals), with the period as the clock: a step clock makes the extended model an exact fit of the family’s own estimator, and cuts at the event-time quartiles make the test the same whatever the unit of time.

\(u(Z)\) is centred because a proportional odds or parametric additive model cannot absorb a function of time alone into its baseline: centred, the added term leaves an average unit as the model has it. For a parametric model the test also reacts to a baseline distribution that does not fit the data, which shows as an effect that drifts.

A covariate that fails in a proportional odds model is advised a model that does not assume proportional odds: CoxPH.fit, WeibullAFT.fit or LogNormalAFT.fit for ProportionalOdds; for a parametric one the same baseline as proportional hazards or accelerated failure time (WeibullPH.fit or WeibullAFT.fit for WeibullPO; PH(dist) or AFT(dist) for a baseline with no pre-built fitter). One that fails in an additive hazards model is advised a time-varying effect, one column per period, through WeibullAH.fit_tvc (AdditiveHazards has no time-varying fit), or the parametric model’s own fit_tvc.

What these checks support

Counts (n), delayed entry (tl), fit_from_df and formulas, center=True, fixed parameters and, for the parametric models, time-varying covariates (fit_tvc). diagnose() refuses, saying what to do: a model restored with from_dict (it has no data), one with an aliased coefficient, one whose fit has no finite maximum and a parametric model fitted to left- or interval-censored or right-truncated data, whose rows cannot be split at the cut times. That last refusal is permanent: fit the PH and AFT models with the same baseline to the same data (WeibullPH, WeibullAFT, …), whose diagnose() takes every kind of censoring and truncation, compare them with the model by aic(), and test whether the covariates have any effect with likelihood_ratio_test(). The functional form, influence and outliers are not checked for these models yet. A parametric additive hazards fit often ends on the boundary of its support (as its fit warns); a refit that does not reach a verified maximum is noted, and its test is then approximate. The tests refit the model once per covariate and once for the GLOBAL row, which for a parametric additive hazards model can take some seconds.

Aalen’s additive hazards model

Aalen’s model (AalenAdditive) lets each covariate add to the hazard an amount that may change freely with time, so it has no constant effect to test. Its report asks two other questions. First, does any effect in fact change? If none does, the simpler Lin and Ying model (AdditiveHazards, one number per covariate) fits as well. Second, does the fit give any observed unit a negative hazard? An additive model can: a covariate that lowers the hazard, over a wide enough range, takes some units below 0, and their estimated cumulative hazard then falls, which a cumulative hazard cannot do. The checks of an Aalen model

constant_effects

For each covariate, the model’s own constant_effects_test(): the largest distance of \(B_j(t)\) from the straight line to its last value (Kolmogorov-Smirnov), with a p-value from n_boot resampled processes (1000 by default; random_state seeds them). The Cramér-von Mises p-value is in the detail, and the whole table, with the baseline, is d.tables['constant_effects']. The rows never fail: a small p-value says the effect changes, which the model allows. With every effect constant the advice is AdditiveHazards; with one that changes, how to read it.

The statistics are those of R’s timereg::aalen test for time-invariant effects, with two differences of detail: the straight line runs to the last time \(B\) is estimated (timereg runs it to the last event time, holding \(B\) where it cannot estimate it), and the supremum includes the left limits of the steps. The p-values differ by design: timereg resamples each unit’s martingale residuals, and in simulations with constant effects it rejected 47% of the time at the 5% level with 100 units and 17% with 300; SurPyval resamples each event’s contribution, and rejected 3-3.5% with 300. The price is power: an effect of 0.5 that falls to 0.1 at a third of follow-up was found a third of the time with 500 units.

non_negative_hazard

The largest fall of any observed covariate row’s estimated cumulative hazard below its running maximum, in standard errors, against the falls of the same resampled processes. Those are the falls a zero hazard would show by chance, the most a non-negative hazard can, so the test is conservative: in simulations it rejected none of 200 data sets with positive hazards, and 90% of those with a hazard convex in the covariate, which the additive line takes below 0. The detail gives the share of units whose own fall is beyond chance. A failure is advised a multiplicative model, CoxPH.fit, whose hazards cannot be negative.

Accelerated life (life-stress) models

An accelerated life model, AcceleratedLife(dist, life_model).fit(x, Z), assumes two things about how failure times change with stress: the distribution keeps one shape at every stress level (only its life moves), and its life follows the life model \(L(Z)\) (Arrhenius, Eyring, the inverse power law, …) from one level to the next. A test run at a few stress levels can check both, because the distribution can also be fitted at each level on its own. Neither can be checked at the use stress, so the report also looks at what the test can say about the extrapolation there.

The checks

A stress level is a distinct row of Z. A level enters the two tests when it has at least two failures (and, for the shape test, when the distribution fitted to it alone reaches a maximum); a note names any left out.

common_shape

The likelihood-ratio test of each level fitted on its own (its own shape and life) against one shape shared by every level, each keeping its own life: \(2(\sum_i \ell_i - \ell_0)\) on \((k - 1)q\) degrees of freedom, for \(k\) levels and \(q\) free shape parameters (a parameter the fit held with fixed stays held). The life model plays no part in it. A distribution with no shape (the Exponential) has no row. With few failures a level (ten or so), the test rejects a common shape somewhat more often than alpha: the maximum likelihood estimate of a shape is biased in small samples, so read a failure near alpha with that in mind.

life_stress

The likelihood-ratio test of the life model against a separate life at each level (with the common shape): \(2(\ell_0 - \ell)\) on \(k - m\) degrees of freedom, \(m\) the life model’s free parameters – the lack-of-fit test of the relationship. With no more levels than parameters (two levels and a two-parameter life model) the model passes through every level’s life and there is nothing to test: the row warns and says so.

life_model_comparison

A screen: each built-in life model for the model’s number of stresses (one stress: Power, Exponential – the Arrhenius model –, Eyring, InverseEyring, Linear, GeneralLogLinear; two: DualPower, DualExponential, PowerExponential, GeneralLogLinear), refitted with the same distribution to the same data, with its AIC. A model with an AIC lower than the fitted one’s by more than 2 warns. InversePower and InverseExponential are Power and Exponential under another parametrisation, with the same likelihood, so only one of each pair is compared. A life model is a physical claim as well as a curve: prefer a better AIC only where its physics suits the stress.

extrapolation

A screen: for each stress column, the number of levels tested. Two or fewer warn: a two-parameter life model then passes through each level’s life, so nothing in the data checks its form, and the life at the use stress rests on the form alone. With use_stress, also the use stress’s distance outside the tested range, in widths of the range, on the scale the life model’s log-life is linear in (log for a power term, 1 / stress for an Arrhenius or Eyring one, the stress itself for Linear and GeneralLogLinear); more than 1 warns. The comparison rows then give each life model’s life at the use stress.

With a continuous stress (each unit at its own stress) no level has two failures: the two tests cannot be computed, their rows warn and say so, and the comparison of life models, which needs no levels, still runs. Group the stresses into levels to test them.

The advice: for a shape that differs between levels, to look for a different failure mode at the extreme levels and refit without them, and to fit each level on its own (Weibull.fit, say) to see the shapes; for a life model that does not follow the levels, the best alternative by AIC (AcceleratedLife(dist, life_models.<name>)), or, if none is better, a life model of your own (a LifeModel subclass) or a second stress; for a better alternative alone, to refit with it and compare the two models’ lives at the use stress; for a use stress far outside the tested range, caution (its bounds, the other life models’ lives there, testing nearer it); and for two levels, to test at three or more. The advice on the next test names surpyval.test_planning: accelerated_test_plan gives the optimum or compromise plan (the stress levels and the units at each) for a quantile at the use stress, and accelerated_test_precision how precisely a plan of your own would estimate it, with the fitted model’s life model, parameters and shape as the planning values (see Test Planning with SurPyval).

diagnose() refuses a model restored with from_dict (it has no data) and one whose fit has no finite maximum. Every kind of censoring, truncation, counts and a fixed parameter are supported.

Recurrent events

A repairable system fails, is repaired and fails again. Its fitted model is one of three kinds, and each has its own family of checks:

  • a Poisson process (HPP, CrowAMSAA, Duane, CoxLewis): minimal repair, every item with the one intensity;

  • an imperfect-repair model (GeneralizedRenewal, GeneralizedOneRenewal, ARA, ARI), whose restoration parameter places the repairs between as good as new and as bad as old;

  • a proportional-intensity model (ProportionalIntensityHPP, ProportionalIntensityNHPP): a Poisson process whose intensity each item’s covariates scale.

Eight pumps are each watched for 100 days, and their failures come ever faster. A constant rate of failures, an HPP, is the simplest model:

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
x, i, c = [], [], []
for pump in range(8):
    # a power law: the expected number of failures by day t is (t / 25)^2
    times = np.sort(100 * rng.random(rng.poisson(16)) ** 0.5)
    x += [*times, 100]
    i += [pump] * (len(times) + 1)
    c += [0] * len(times) + [1]   # the last row ends the observation
x, i, c = np.array(x), np.array(i), np.array(c)

hpp = surv.HPP.fit(x, i, c)
d = hpp.diagnose(random_state=0)
print(d)

The recurrent checks

trend

The Laplace and MIL-HDBK-189C tests, whose null is a homogeneous Poisson process (a constant rate), and the Lewis-Robinson test, whose null is any renewal process: the Laplace statistic divided by the coefficient of variation of the times between failures. The first two are the model’s own trend_test; each item is tested on its own window. For Lewis-Robinson each item runs from its first renewal (its start if it was observed from new, else its first failure) to its last failure, so that it is centred under any renewal process. For a model with a constant rate (HPP, ProportionalIntensityHPP) these are tests of it; for the others a screen, whose row warns when its test does not reject its simpler null. A trend against a constant rate that Lewis-Robinson does not confirm points to a renewal process whose times between failures are not exponential (advised GeneralizedRenewal.fit); no trend at all, to a constant rate (HPP.fit, ProportionalIntensityHPP.fit).

fit

The model’s cramer_von_mises test, with diagnose(n_boot=...) bootstrap refits (99 by default) drawn with its random_state, the package’s one seed rule: give a seed for a report that is the same each time it runs. A failure is advised another intensity, another repair model or another baseline, as the model calls for.

between_items

(Poisson processes.) The Pearson dispersion test of the items’ event counts against those the model expects over their windows, with \(m - 1 - p\) degrees of freedom for \(m\) items and \(p\) covariate coefficients. Items that differ by more than chance fail it, and are advised a proportional-intensity model with what tells them apart as covariates (or, for one, more covariates). An item observed up to its last failure has a fixed count and is left out.

repair

(Imperfect repair.) The model’s repair_test: likelihood-ratio tests of perfect repair and of minimal repair (ARI: maximal repair, rho = 1; G1 has no minimal repair), with the 50:50 mixture of chi-squared(0) and chi-squared(1) for a value on the edge of the parameter’s range. A row warns when its repair is not rejected.

diagnose() refuses, saying what to do, a model built from its parameters or restored with from_dict (it has no data), a least-squares (how='MSE') fit, one with no finite maximum, and data with interval- or left-censored rows or right truncation. Every recurrent check is about the model as a whole: columns= is accepted for a proportional-intensity model and changes nothing.

Each cause’s goodness of fit is the univariate distributions’ test (below) on the cause’s own fit, pcr.models[cause]. It tests the cause’s Cox-Snell residuals r = H_k(x) - H_k(t_l), the cause’s cumulative hazard over each unit’s time at risk (from its entry, for delayed entry), which are a censored sample of the unit exponential when the cause’s distribution is right; the test compares u = 1 - exp(-r) with the uniform, with parametric bootstrap p-values. The alternatives rows are the univariate AIC screen, cause by cause. The incidence test is the largest gap between the two incidences up to the last time observed, D_k = sup_t |CIF_k(t) - AJ_k(t)|, with a p-value from a parametric bootstrap: each resample draws every unit’s latent time from each cause’s model, keeps the earliest, censors it as the data were, refits every cause and recomputes D_k. A cause’s incidence depends on every cause’s hazard, CIF_k(t) = int_0^t h_k(u) S(u) du, so a wrong distribution for one cause can move another’s incidence; the goodness-of-fit rows say which cause is wrong. With delayed entry (tl) the Aalen-Johansen estimate counts each unit at risk only from its entry, and when every unit entered late it is conditional on surviving to the first entry s; the model’s incidence is then conditioned the same way, (CIF_k(t) - CIF_k(s)) / S(s), and each resample draws a unit’s latent times beyond its own entry. A model fitted to left- or interval-censored rows (its causes fitted jointly) is not checked yet: diagnose() refuses it, saying to compare distributions by AIC and the all-cause survival with the Turnbull estimate. A model restored with from_dict, or assembled with from_fitted from fits of different data or from models that are not fitted distributions, refuses, saying what to do.

The checks

goodness_of_fit

The Anderson-Darling, Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Cramer-von Mises statistics. Each unit’s time is put through the fitted CDF, conditioned on its truncation window, \(u = (F(x) - F(t_l)) / (F(t_r) - F(t_l))\), which is uniform when the model is right (and right-censored for a censored unit); the statistics compare the Kaplan-Meier estimate \(F_n\) of the \(u\) with the uniform up to the largest \(u\): \(\sup |F_n(u) - u|\), \(N \int (F_n(u) - u)^2 du\) and \(N \int (F_n(u) - u)^2 / (u (1 - u)) du\). With complete, untruncated data they are the classical statistics, and agree with R’s fitdistrplus::gofstat to 1e-9.

The parameters are estimated, and the data may be censored, so the textbook tables do not apply: the p-values come from a parametric bootstrap. Each of n_boot resamples (200 by default) draws a time for every unit from the fitted model within its truncation window, censors it as the data were censored – a censored unit at its own censoring time, a failed one at a time drawn from the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the censoring distribution beyond its failure, Davison and Hinkley’s conditional bootstrap, which reproduces fixed-time and random censoring – refits the same model (distribution, how, offset, lfp, zi, fixed parameters) and computes its statistics. The p-value is the share of refits at least as extreme, \((1 + k) / (1 + B)\). For complete data from a location-scale family, whose null distribution does not depend on the parameters, the bootstrap reproduces the tables: its 5% points are D’Agostino and Stephens’s, within Monte Carlo error, and the test is exact. With censoring it is somewhat conservative in small samples: of 400 randomly censored exponential samples of 40, 5-9% had a p-value of at most 0.1 (two runs). Left- and interval-censored data have no such test (a resample would need the inspection times, which the data do not record): those rows warn and say so.

probability_plot

A plot only: model.plot(), the failures’ plotting positions against the fitted line on the distribution’s linearising axes.

alternatives

An AIC screen: the best other distribution of fit_best; a mixture of two of the model’s distribution (for a model without options, with 20 failures or more); the model with an offset; with a limited failure population (when some units are right-censored); and a zero-inflated model (when some units failed at time 0). A row warns when the alternative’s AIC is at least 2 below the model’s, and never fails; its statistic is the model’s AIC less the alternative’s. Each warning is advice naming the fit: fit_best(..., return_table=True), MixtureModel.fit(..., dist=..., m=2), Weibull.fit(..., offset=True), lfp=True or zi=True. A failed test that no alternative explains is advised a look at the probability plot, more mixture components or a non-parametric estimate (KaplanMeier.fit, Turnbull.fit).

Seeds and run time

diagnose(n_boot=..., random_state=...) sets the bootstrap’s size and seed (None draws from numpy’s global generator, as everywhere in SurPyval); the same seed gives the same report. A refit of a 2-parameter distribution to 100 units takes a few hundredths of a second, so the default 200 refits take a few seconds; an offset, lfp or zi model’s refits are slower, and fewer refits (n_boot=100) are still a valid test, with p-values in steps of 1/101.

diagnose() refuses, saying what to do: a model built with from_params or restored without its data, a fit with no finite maximum, and the event-probability models (Bernoulli, FixedEventProbability) and ExactEventTime, which have no distribution of times to test. Two refusals are permanent, and each names what to use instead. A discrete distribution (Poisson, NegativeBinomial, DiscreteWeibull, …) is refused because the tests and plot are for continuous times, not counts: compare discrete distributions fitted to the same counts by aic(), and the model’s sf with KaplanMeier.fit of the same data. A distribution whose fitter lacks the shared optimiser is refused because the bootstrap refits each resample with it: plot the model’s sf beside KaplanMeier.fit of the data it is meant to describe and, for a model fitted to data, compare it with other distributions by aic(). A MixtureModel is a model of its own, without diagnose() yet.

The checks

posterior_predictive

In plain words: draw a set of parameters from the posterior, simulate a data set like the one observed from them, and see whether the real data look unusual among the simulated ones; do it n_boot times (1000 by default). Each replicate keeps the observed design: every unit is drawn within its own truncation window and censored as the data were, a censored unit at its own observed censoring time and a failed one at a time drawn from the Kaplan-Meier estimate of the censoring distribution beyond its failure (fixed-time censoring is reproduced exactly; random censoring from the censoring times observed). Two discrepancies: the Anderson-Darling statistic of the data’s \(u = F(x \mid \theta)\) against the uniform, computed for the data and the replicate at the same draw \(\theta\) (a realised discrepancy, Gelman, Meng and Stern 1996); and Pearson’s \(\sum (O - E)^2 / E\) of the failures in up to ten time bins (one per five failures, at the observed failure times’ quantiles; with fewer than ten failures, the number of failures), \(E\) the replicates’ mean. The p-value is the share of draws whose replicate is at least as far off as the data, \((1 + k) / (1 + B)\).

A posterior predictive p-value is conservative: under a correct model it gathers near 0.5 instead of being uniform (Robins, van der Vaart and Ventura 2000), so a small one is strong evidence against the model and a moderate one is no evidence for it. In 300 randomly censored Weibull samples of 40 fitted with the right model, the Anderson-Darling check rejected at 5% in none and the bins check in 3 (1%) (tests/calibration/test_size_bayes_diagnostics.py). The price is power: against 100 log-normal lifetimes fitted with a Weibull, the two checks rejected in 12% and 25% of 100 samples, where the maximum-likelihood fit’s Anderson-Darling test below rejected in 90% (scripts/studies/bayes_diagnose_power.py). Left- and interval-censored data have no such check (a replicate would need the inspection times): the rows warn and say so.

goodness_of_fit

The univariate family’s Anderson-Darling, Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Cramer-von Mises tests (see Univariate distributions), run on the same distribution fitted to the same data by maximum likelihood, model.mle: their bootstrap’s null distribution is that of a maximum-likelihood refit, so they test that fit, not the posterior’s centre. They check the distribution, which the prior does not change, and have more power than the posterior predictive checks. Left out, with a note, where the data alone have no maximum-likelihood fit.

prior_data_conflict

In plain words: how many standard deviations apart are what the prior says and what the data alone say? On each parameter’s log scale (logit for a proportion), the prior’s mean and variance, and the maximum-likelihood estimate with its variance; if the prior is right, the estimate is a draw from the prior plus estimation error, so their gap \(\delta\) has variance \(V_{prior} + V_{data}\) and \(z = \delta / \sqrt{V_{prior} + V_{data}}\) is standard normal. This is Box’s (1980) prior predictive check in its normal form (Evans and Moshonov 2006). One row per parameter with a proper prior, and a joint chi-square, \(\delta' (V_{prior} + V_{data})^{-1} \delta\), when more than one has one. Drawing the truth from the prior itself, the rows rejected at 5% in 4.7%, 3.7% and 3.0% (joint) of 300 samples of 40. A flat parameter, a power prior’s old data and data with no maximum-likelihood fit are not checked (the last rows warn).

grid_accuracy

A grid posterior is computed again on a finer grid, and the largest change in any parameter’s 2.5%, 50% and 97.5% points is reported as a fraction of the 95% interval’s width: it warns at 1% and fails at 5%. A screen beside it warns when more than \(10^{-6}\) of the posterior sits in the grid’s outermost cells. A Laplace approximation is compared with the grid posterior the same way. A closed form is exact, and another sampler’s draws have that sampler’s diagnostics: neither has rows.

The advice: for a distribution that does not reproduce the data, the distributions fit_best ranks; for a posterior that fails while the distribution fits, or a conflict, model.compare() and a wider prior; for an inaccurate grid, fit_bayes(points=) or method='grid'. diagnose() refuses, saying what to do, a model without its data and a Bernoulli fit (whose posterior fits the proportion of events).

Calibration of predicted survival

The Cox, parametric proportional hazards and accelerated failure time families share one more check, calibration (#881): whether the survival the model predicts is the survival the units had. At a time \(t_0\), each unit’s predicted risk of failure by then, \(p = 1 - S(t_0 \mid Z)\), is set against the observed risk of the units with that prediction, estimated as Austin, Harrell and van Klaveren (2020) recommend: a Cox model of the observed times on a restricted cubic spline (three knots) of \(\log(-\log(1 - p))\). Right-censored units are at risk until they are censored, never counted as survivors, and a unit that entered late only after its entry. Plotted against the prediction, that observed risk is the calibration curve, on the diagonal for a calibrated model; its summaries are the mean (ICI, the integrated calibration index), median (E50) and 90th percentile (E90) of \(|\text{observed} - p|\) over the units.

The rule

calibration is a screen and never fails: on the data a model was fitted to, a test of calibration has no known null distribution. A row warns when the curve is both far from the diagonal and clearly so: the ICI is above 0.02 (the predictions are more than two percentage points off on average) and the predicted risk is outside the curve’s pointwise 1 - alpha band for more than 10% of the units. In simulations (two normal covariates, Weibull times, uniform censoring, 200 runs each), a correctly specified Cox model warned in 4% of reports at 50 units and in none from 300, a Weibull PH model in 12% at 50 units, 7% at 100 and 2% at 1000; a model with its coefficients halved warned in 79% at 100 units and every run from 300, one fitted to data with a 1.5 times longer scale in 94% at 100 units, an exponential model of Weibull (shape 2) times in 99% at 50, and a Cox model missing a quadratic term in 35% at 100 units, 87% at 300 and every run at 1000.

What it sees on the fitted data is misspecification, not overfitting. A Cox model’s baseline is estimated from the data and the calibration model is itself a proportional hazards model of the predictions, so a Cox model’s curve bends only where its log hazard is not linear in its linear predictor (a covariate with the wrong functional form; an effect that changes with time is the proportional_hazards check’s to find). For a parametric model the curve also shows a baseline that does not fit. The advice names the remedies: a transform or an interaction in a formula; and for a parametric model another baseline (the AIC rows), the other kind of model (PH or AFT), or a Cox model.

Shared frailty

A shared-frailty model (WeibullFrailty, …, Frailty(dist, family=...), and CoxFrailty) is a proportional hazards model given the frailty: \(h(t \mid Z, u) = u\, h_0(t) e^{\beta'Z}\), with \(u\) shared by the rows of a group. Its checks are of the frailty and of that conditional model. They are not those of the same model refitted without the frailty: integrating a frailty out makes the marginal hazards non-proportional (the hazard ratio fades as the frailer units fail), so WeibullPH or CoxPH diagnostics on clustered data would flag a model that holds.

The checks of a frailty model

frailty_variance (both)

The likelihood-ratio test of no frailty, theta = 0: twice the frailty fit’s log-likelihood less that of the same model without the frailty fitted to the same rows – <Dist>PH.fit(x, Z, c, n) for a parametric baseline (<Dist>.fit without covariates), and for CoxFrailty the Cox partial likelihood of CoxPH.fit, against R’s integrated likelihood. theta = 0 is on the boundary, so the p-value is that of the 50:50 mixture of \(\chi^2_0\) and \(\chi^2_1\) (Self and Liang 1987): half the \(\chi^2_1\) p-value, and 1 for a fit with theta at 0. A screen: it warns when the frailty is not significant (p >= alpha), and the advice names the model without it. The statistics agree with R’s (coxph(... + frailty(id, dist = 'gamma')), lme4’s glmer for the log-normal frailty, survreg without a frailty) to 1e-9. On data simulated without a frailty the test rejected 5.1% of 1000 Weibull data sets at alpha = 0.05 (4.75% and 3% of 400 for the log-normal and Cox frailties); more than half of the fits put theta on its boundary, which makes the test conservative at larger levels.

frailty_family (parametric)

A screen: the AIC against the other frailty family (family="gamma" or "lognormal") refitted to the same rows; it warns when the other’s is more than 2 lower. CoxFrailty has the gamma frailty only.

proportional_hazards (parametric)

For each covariate, the likelihood-ratio test of a step in its coefficient, given the frailty, at the median failure time (1 df), with a step in the baseline under both models, as for WeibullPH; and a GLOBAL test of a step in every coefficient. Both models are maximised over the marginal likelihood (the frailty integrated out, theta re-estimated). R’s values (a Poisson GLMM of the split follow-up for the log-normal frailty, the closed-form gamma-frailty likelihood for the gamma) agree to 1e-6.

functional_form (parametric)

For each covariate with three or more distinct values, the likelihood-ratio test of adding its square, given the frailty, and the martingale residuals given each group’s posterior frailty.

baseline_distribution (parametric)

A screen: the AIC against each other built-in baseline with the same frailty, refitted to the same rows; it warns when the best is more than 2 lower.

overall_fit (both)

A plot: the marginal Cox-Snell residuals, the frailty integrated out, against the unit exponential.

The advice: the model without the frailty when it is not significant; Frailty(dist, family=...) for the other family or a better baseline; a transform in a formula for a covariate that is not linear; and, as the frailty fitters have no strata or time-varying effects, a separate frailty fit per level, or a stratified or time-varying Cox model of the marginal hazard with standard errors clustered by group, for an effect that changes with time.

What these checks support

Both frailty families (gamma and log-normal), any baseline, counts (n), formulas and fits without covariates; for CoxFrailty both tie methods. A CoxFrailty fit with theta= given has no variance to test, and the report says so. A CoxFrailtyModel does not keep which group each row is in, so its conditional model is not checked (only the frailty variance and the Cox-Snell plot), and the report says why. Influence and outliers are not screened: a frailty model’s residuals depend on each group’s shrunken frailty estimate, and the rows of a group are not independent. diagnose() refuses, saying what to do: a model restored with from_dict, one with an aliased coefficient, and one whose fit is not a verified maximum.
