Intervention Analysis with SurPyval

A failure mode keeps coming back, so something is changed: a redesigned seal, a new lubricant, a revised procedure, a corrective action. Months later the question is whether it worked:

  • Did the failure rate fall? And by how much, with what uncertainty?

  • Is the evidence enough to say? If no change is seen, was there enough operating time since the change to have seen one? If not, how much more is needed?

surpyval.recurrent.intervention_test() answers both from the failure records of one repairable system or a fleet, each with its own observation window and its own date of change. It compares the rate of failures after the intervention with the rate before it, and reports the ratio of the rates (after over before: 0.5 means the rate halved), its confidence interval, the p-value of no change, and the evidence: the power the exposure had to detect the change hoped for, the smallest change it could detect, and the exposure that would be needed. The functions behind the evidence are in surpyval.test_planning, for planning before the data are in. The models of the rate (homogeneous and power-law Poisson processes) are explained in Recurrent Event Analysis.

One system

A pump failed eight times in its first 50 weeks of service. Its seals were changed at week 50, and it failed three times in the next 50 weeks. The failure times are the pump’s age at each failure, as for every recurrent model in SurPyval; T is the end of its observation (today), and intervention the time of the change:

import numpy as np
from surpyval.recurrent import intervention_test

x = [3, 8, 15, 19, 26, 30, 37, 44, 52, 77, 95]
result = intervention_test(x, T=100, intervention=50)
print(result)

Intervention test (HPP, per-system)
===================================
Systems          : 1 (1 compared)
Before           : 8 failures in 50
After            : 3 failures in 50 (5.5 expected with no change)
Rate ratio       : 0.375, 95% CI [0.06408, 1.562]
Test             : exact (two-sided), p-value 0.2266
Power            : 0.134 to detect a ratio of 0.5 (0.8 required)
Detectable ratio : 0.004185
Verdict          : inconclusive
  - Not enough evidence yet: the test had only 13% power to detect a ratio of 0.5 with this exposure.
  - No exposure after the intervention can give 80% power: the 8 failures before it are too few to pin down the earlier rate. Only more history before the intervention, or a larger change, would.

The rate after the change is estimated at 0.375 times the rate before it (3 failures in 50 weeks against 8 in 50), a 62% reduction. But the 95% confidence interval of the ratio runs from 0.064 to 1.56: the data are as consistent with no change as with a large one, and the p-value of no change is 0.23. The verdict is not “no change”: it is inconclusive, because the test only had a 13% chance of detecting even a halving of the rate. The notes say why, and it is worth knowing: with only eight failures before the change, the earlier rate itself is too uncertain for any amount of operating time after it to show a halving reliably. Only a longer history before the change, or pooling with other pumps, would.

The verdict is one of:

  • "decrease" or "increase": the test found a change (p_value < alpha_ci);

  • "no change detected": it found none, and it had at least power_required (80%) power to detect the change target_ratio (0.5, a halving) – so the change most likely did not achieve that;

  • "inconclusive": it found none, but the evidence was too little to tell;

  • "untestable": the data cannot answer (no exposure after the change yet, no failures at all, …), with a note saying why.

A test of “did it work?” is one-sided: alternative="decrease" asks only whether the rate fell, and its interval is a one-sided upper bound on the ratio, “the rate is now at most this fraction of what it was”:

one_sided = intervention_test(x, T=100, intervention=50,
                              alternative="decrease")
print(round(one_sided.p_value, 3), one_sided.ratio_cb.round(3))

0.113 [0.    1.296]

How the test works

With model="hpp" (the default) the rate is taken as constant before the change and constant after it. Let \(E_b\) and \(E_a\) be the operating time (the exposure) before and after, \(N_b\) and \(N_a\) the failures, and \(\rho = \lambda_a / \lambda_b\) the ratio of the rates. Given the \(N = N_b + N_a\) failures in all, the number after the change is binomial,

\[N_a \mid N \sim \text{Bin}\!\left(N,\ \pi(\rho) = \frac{\rho E_a}{E_b + \rho E_a}\right),\]

whatever the rates are (Cox and Lewis 1966, section 9.4). So no change, \(\rho = 1\), is tested by the binomial tail at \(\pi(1) = E_a / (E_b + E_a)\), which is exact for any number of failures, however few. The confidence interval inverts the test: it is the Clopper-Pearson interval of \(\pi\), mapped to \(\rho = (\pi / (1 - \pi)) (E_b / E_a)\). Here \(N = 11\) and \(\pi(1) = 0.5\):

from scipy import stats

lower_tail = stats.binom.cdf(3, 11, 0.5)
print(round(2 * lower_tail, 4), round(one_sided.p_value, 4))

0.2266 0.1133

An exact test of a count is conservative: its real level is below the 5% asked for (2.9% for one system expecting 10 failures before the change and 10 after), and its interval covers more often than 95%. method="mid-p" gives the mid-p test and interval (Lancaster 1961), which count half the probability of the observed count: their level and coverage are close to 5% and 95% on average, at the price of no longer being guaranteed. The calibration studies in SurPyval’s test suite check both, by simulation against the exact sums.

Is the evidence enough?

The evidence is judged against a change worth detecting, target_ratio (a halving unless given), with the power wanted, power (80% unless given). The result carries:

  • power: the chance that a test on this exposure would detect target_ratio, taking the rate before the change as the rate of its failures;

  • detectable_ratio: the ratio nearest 1 that this exposure detects with 80% power (the minimum detectable improvement is one minus it);

  • exposure_needed: the exposure after the change, in all, that would detect target_ratio with 80% power, additional_exposure how much more that is, and events_needed the failures to expect by then at the improved rate;

  • zero_failure_exposure: the exposure after the change at which, with no further failure at all, a decrease would be shown.

A fleet of four pumps had the new seals fitted at different times, and the records were pulled 800 hours after each change:

failures = {
    "A": [682, 1112, 1330, 1351, 1664, 1932, 2468, 2780],
    "B": [184, 648, 817, 1368, 1489, 1556, 1960, 2869, 3128, 3184,
          3269, 3751, 4064, 4077, 4835, 5532],
    "C": [721, 969, 2012, 2081, 3300],
    "D": [412, 503, 1696, 2035, 2406, 2754, 3705, 4330, 5085],
}
x = np.concatenate([failures[q] for q in failures])
i = np.concatenate([[q] * len(failures[q]) for q in failures])
change = {"A": 3000, "B": 4200, "C": 2500, "D": 3600}

early = {q: change[q] + 800 for q in change}
seen = np.array([t <= early[q] for t, q in zip(x, i)])
first_look = intervention_test(x[seen], i[seen], T=early,
                               intervention=change)
print(first_look)

Intervention test (HPP, per-system)
===================================
Systems          : 4 (4 compared)
Before           : 32 failures in 13300
After            : 4 failures in 3200 (6.751 expected with no change)
Rate ratio       : 0.54, 95% CI [0.1385, 1.527]
Test             : exact (two-sided), p-value 0.3359
Power            : 0.186 to detect a ratio of 0.5 (0.8 required)
Detectable ratio : 0.1198
Verdict          : inconclusive
  - Not enough evidence yet: the test had only 19% power to detect a ratio of 0.5 with this exposure.
  - 41,395 exposure after the intervention in all (38,195 more), with about 49.8 failures expected at that rate, would give 80% power.

Four failures in the 3200 pump-hours since the change, against 32 in the 13300 before it: the rate looks lower, but 3200 hours could only have detected a halving 19% of the time. About 41000 hours after the change would give 80% power. The full records, to the end of the study:

end = {"A": 7000, "B": 7000, "C": 6500, "D": 7000}
final = intervention_test(x, i, T=end, intervention=change)
print(final)

Intervention test (HPP, per-system)
===================================
Systems          : 4 (4 compared)
Before           : 32 failures in 13300
After            : 6 failures in 14200 (18.42 expected with no change)
Rate ratio       : 0.1919, 95% CI [0.06513, 0.4696]
Test             : exact (two-sided), p-value 4.24e-05
Power            : 0.607 to detect a ratio of 0.5 (0.8 required)
Detectable ratio : 0.4041
Verdict          : decrease
  - The failure rate fell to 0.192 times its earlier rate, a reduction of 81% (95% confidence interval of the ratio 0.0651 to 0.47).

The rate fell to about a fifth of its earlier value. The interval of the reduction, final.reduction_cb (53% to 93%), says the seals removed at least half of the failures. final.power (61%) is below 80%: the exposure was still short of what detecting a mere halving needs, but the reduction was larger than a halving, and the test found it. The note about power matters most when nothing is found.

The same calculations plan a study before the data are in. rate_ratio_design() takes the rate before the change and the exposures, and solves for whichever of the ratio, the exposure after and the power is left None. With 30 failures in 10000 hours before a change, 5000 hours after it would detect a halving about a third of the time, and 41000 hours would be needed for 80%:

from surpyval.test_planning import rate_ratio_design

rate = 30 / 10000
before = dict(rate=rate, exposure_before=10000)
power = rate_ratio_design(0.5, exposure_after=5000, **before).power
smallest = rate_ratio_design(exposure_after=5000, power=0.8, **before).value
needed = rate_ratio_design(0.5, power=0.8, **before).value
print(round(power, 3), round(smallest, 3), round(needed))
print(rate_ratio_design(0.5, rate=12 / 10000, exposure_before=10000,
                 power=0.8).value)

0.356 0.263 41188
inf

The last line is the limit seen for the single pump: however long the pumps run after the change, the rate after it becomes known exactly, but the rate before it stays as uncertain as its 12 failures make it. Twelve failures cannot show a halving with 80% power; the exact test needs about 24 (the normal approximation’s 16 is far too few). The power is the exact sum over the possible counts of failures, not a normal approximation, which is poor for the handful of failures a corrective action is usually judged on.

Fleets

Systems differ: some fail far more often than others. With baseline="per-system" (the default) each system keeps its own rate and only the ratio is shared, and the test is the exact stratified test: the failures after the change, summed over the systems, whose exact distribution given each system’s total is the sum of the systems’ binomials (Breslow and Day 1987, chapter 3). baseline="common" pools the exposures and the failures of every system, as if they shared one rate.

Pooling can mislead badly when systems with different rates have different shares of their exposure after the change. Here system a fails often and was changed late, so almost all its exposure, and its failures, are before the change; system b fails rarely and was changed early. Neither rate changed at all:

a = np.linspace(0, 100, 42)[1:-1]            # 40 failures to 100
b = np.array([40.0, 60.0, 100.0, 130.0, 160.0, 190.0])
x2 = np.concatenate([a, b])
i2 = ["a"] * a.size + ["b"] * b.size
ends = {"a": 100.0, "b": 200.0}
changed = {"a": 80.0, "b": 20.0}

pooled = intervention_test(x2, i2, T=ends, intervention=changed,
                           baseline="common")
per_system = intervention_test(x2, i2, T=ends, intervention=changed)
print("pooled    :", pooled.verdict, round(pooled.ratio, 3))
print("per system:", per_system.verdict, round(per_system.ratio, 3))

pooled    : decrease 0.219
per system: inconclusive 1.088

Pooled, the period before the change is mostly the busy system’s and the period after mostly the quiet one’s, so the rate seems to fall by nearly 80%; each system compared with itself shows no change. In the calibration study of a fleet like this, the pooled test finds a “decrease” 73% of the time when nothing changed, and the per-system test 4% of the time. Use "common" only when the systems are known to share one rate (identical units in the same duty); it is then more powerful, and it uses systems seen on one side of the change only, which a per-system comparison cannot.

Each system’s change time is given in intervention as a dict (or an array in the sorted order of the system ids); inf marks a system never changed, and a time at or before a system’s start one that had the change from new. A dict T may name systems with no failure at all, so that their exposure counts. The observation can also be given in the fitters’ form, a c = 1 row ending each system (c=), with a later start of observation (tl=, delayed entry), or as separate windows of observation (windows=). The time since a system’s last failure is evidence about the change, so the end of observation is always required.

When the rate trends

A constant rate on each side of the change is often not true: a system wearing out fails more often as it ages, and a comparison of average rates then takes the ageing for an effect of the change (a worsening after it, or a hidden improvement). The HPP result checks for this: trend_p_value is the Laplace test of a trend within the periods before and after the change, and a note recommends the power law when it is small.

model="power-law" lets the rate trend with age as the Crow-AMSAA power law, \(u(t) = \lambda \beta t^{\beta - 1}\), and the change multiplies it by \(\rho\), keeping its shape. The ratio is tested by the likelihood ratio, with the scale (each system’s, per system) and the shape profiled out, and its interval is the profile-likelihood interval. These pumps wear out (shape 3), and nothing changed at week 50:

rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
wearing = np.sort(100 * rng.uniform(size=60) ** (1 / 3))
hpp = intervention_test(wearing, T=100, intervention=50)
power_law = intervention_test(wearing, T=100, intervention=50,
                              model="power-law")
print("HPP      :", hpp.verdict, round(hpp.ratio, 2),
      "- trend p-value", round(hpp.trend_p_value, 4))
print("power law:", power_law.verdict, round(power_law.ratio, 2),
      "- shape", round(power_law.shape, 2))

HPP      : increase 7.57 - trend p-value 0.0005
power law: inconclusive 1.02 - shape 3.07

The HPP comparison finds a large “increase”, and flags the trend; the power law finds the shape (about 3) and no change at the intervention. In the calibration study of five such systems, the HPP comparison finds a change 54% of the time when there is none, and the likelihood-ratio test 5%. The power law’s evidence is judged in the scale of its cumulative intensity, where the process has a constant rate, taking the shape as known; it is therefore a little optimistic, and its exposure_needed (in that scale, not in hours) is left out: events_needed gives the failures to expect after the change instead.

Saving the result

to_dict gives the result as strict JSON (an infinite bound or a missing value is written as null and recorded under "non_finite", as for every SurPyval model), and InterventionTestResult.from_dict restores it:

import json
from surpyval.recurrent import InterventionTestResult

text = json.dumps(final.to_dict())
again = InterventionTestResult.from_dict(json.loads(text))
print(again.verdict, again.ratio_cb.round(3))

decrease [0.065 0.47 ]

What the test cannot tell

The comparison is of the periods before and after the change, so anything else that changed between them is attributed to the intervention:

  • Regression to the mean. A change is usually made because of a run of failures, and a run of bad luck tends to be followed by fewer failures whether or not anything is done. Count the run that prompted the change as “before”, fix the intervention time from the records of the work, not from the failure data, and judge the period after it on its own exposure.

  • Other changes. A new duty cycle, a season, a different operator or a change in how failures are recorded at the same time is indistinguishable from the intervention. Systems never changed (inf) observed over the same calendar time show whether the rate moved for everyone; they enter the per-system comparison only through the systems that were changed, so compare them separately.

  • The failure mode. Test the failures the change was meant to remove: a change that halves one mode among several can be lost among the others.

  • Exposure. The exposure is in the units of x. Operating hours are better than calendar time when the systems’ use varies.
