Distributional Regression

What it does

A regression model in SurPyval usually lets the covariates change one thing about the life distribution. In an accelerated failure time (AFT) model a heavier load makes every unit wear out sooner by the same factor; in a proportional hazards (PH) model it multiplies the hazard by the same factor at every age. Either way the shape of the distribution is the same for every unit: if failures are wear-out at light load, they are wear-out, in the same way, at heavy load.

A distributional regression lets the covariates change every parameter of the distribution, each through its own link. For a Weibull that is the scale alpha (how long units typically last) and the shape beta (whether failures come early, at random or from wear-out). It answers a question the AFT and PH models assume away: does the way units fail change with the conditions they run in? It is the model of R’s gamlss (“generalised additive models for location, scale and shape”) and of flexsurvreg’s anc argument, on SurPyval’s survival data: right, left and interval censoring, truncation and counts work as in every other fit.

When to use it:

  • the shape may depend on a stress (a shape near 1 at light load, random failures, and well above 1 at heavy load, wear-out), which changes the maintenance strategy, not only the interval;

  • the spread of lives differs between groups (sites, suppliers, treatment arms) while the typical life may or may not;

  • a plot of the data by group shows lines that are not parallel on a Weibull (or log-normal) probability plot: the AFT and PH models make them parallel.

A worked example: does the shape depend on load?

Bearings were run at four loads (1 to 4 kN) until they failed or the test stopped at 2000 hours. (The data are simulated, with a shape that grows with load, so we can see whether the fits find it.)

rng = np.random.default_rng(2026)
load = rng.choice([1.0, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0], size=240)
life = 3000 * np.exp(-0.4 * load) * rng.weibull(np.exp(0.25 * load))
bearings = pd.DataFrame({
    "hours": np.minimum(life, 2000.0),
    "censored": (life > 2000.0).astype(int),
    "load": load,
})

First the usual model, a Weibull AFT: load shortens life, one shape for all. Then the distributional model, with load on both parameters (with no parameter named, every covariate goes on every parameter):

aft = surv.WeibullAFT.fit_from_df(
    bearings, x_col="hours", c_col="censored", Z_cols=["load"]
)
model = surv.WeibullDistributional.fit_from_df(
    bearings, x_col="hours", c_col="censored", Z_cols=["load"]
)
model

Read the coefficients through their links, with SurPyval’s sign (that of its AFT models: a positive coefficient means a shorter life). alpha_load = 0.40: each kN divides the scale by \(e^{0.40} = 1.49\), the same coefficient the AFT model reports. beta_load = 0.27: each kN multiplies the shape by \(e^{0.27} = 1.31\) (a shape has no direction of its own, so its coefficient keeps the link’s sign), and its interval is well clear of 0. So the shape does change with load. The shape at each load is one call away:

The likelihood-ratio test of “the shape does not depend on load” compares the two fits (the AFT is the distributional model with beta_load = 0), and the AIC agrees:

from scipy.stats import chi2

lr = 2 * (aft.neg_ll() - model.neg_ll())
print(f"LR statistic {lr:.1f}, p = {chi2.sf(lr, 1):.1e}")
print(f"AIC: AFT {aft.aic():.1f}, distributional {model.aic():.1f}")

LR statistic 22.3, p = 2.3e-06
AIC: AFT 3115.6, distributional 3095.2

It matters for what the models predict. The B10 life (the age by which 10% have failed) at each load, from each model:

print("distributional:", model.qf(0.1, loads).round(0))
print("AFT:           ", aft.qf(0.1, loads).round(0))
print(model.quantile_cb(0.1, loads).round(0))

distributional: [350. 359. 334. 287.]
AFT:            [649. 430. 285. 189.]
[[226. 541.]
 [289. 447.]
 [287. 388.]

The AFT model, forced to one shape for all loads, puts the B10 at 4 kN at 189 hours; the distributional model, which lets the heavy-load failures bunch up as wear-out does, at 287 hours (95% bounds 237 to 347). At 1 kN it is the other way round: 649 hours from the AFT, 350 from the distributional model, because random-looking failures at light load start early.

Choosing each parameter’s covariates

Each parameter takes its own covariates. With columns, covariates names them per parameter; a parameter not named has none (its intercept only):

shape_only = surv.WeibullDistributional.fit_from_df(
    bearings, x_col="hours", c_col="censored", Z_cols=["load"],
    covariates={"beta": ["load"]},
)
shape_only.parameter_names

['alpha', 'beta', 'beta_load']

With a formula per parameter, formulas does the same, with everything a formula can say (categorical columns, interactions, transforms such as np.log(load)):

by_formula = surv.WeibullDistributional.fit_from_df(
    bearings, x_col="hours", c_col="censored",
    formulas={"alpha": "load", "beta": "np.log(load)"},
)
by_formula.parameter_names

['alpha', 'beta', 'alpha_load', 'beta_np.log(load)']

With arrays, fit(x, Z, c=..., covariates={"beta": [0]}) picks columns of Z by number. A fitted model predicts from a DataFrame with the same columns, or from an array of every column it was fitted to, one row per unit, as every regression does.

The model, in formulas

For a distribution with parameters \(\theta_1, \dots, \theta_K\), each parameter has a linear predictor and a link \(g_k\):

\[g_k\bigl(\theta_k(z)\bigr) = g_k(\theta_k) + s_k\, x_k(z)' b_k, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K,\]

where \(\theta_k\) is the parameter at \(z = 0\), \(x_k(z)\) the parameter’s columns and \(s_k = \pm 1\) its sign (below). A unit with covariates \(z\) has the distribution’s own functions at its parameters, so for a Weibull

\[S(t \mid z) = \exp\left[-\left(\frac{t}{\alpha(z)}\right)^{\beta(z)}\right], \qquad \alpha(z) = \alpha\, e^{-x_\alpha(z)' b_\alpha}, \quad \beta(z) = \beta\, e^{x_\beta(z)' b_\beta}.\]

The likelihood is that of every SurPyval regression, each row at its own parameters: \(\log f\) for an observed time, \(\log S\) for a right censored one, \(\log F\) for a left censored one, \(\log(F(r) - F(l))\) for an interval, less the log of each row’s probability of falling inside its truncation window, all weighted by the counts. It is maximised directly, with the exact gradient and Hessian.

Links. The default link keeps each parameter in its range: log for a positive parameter (a scale, a shape, a rate), identity for one on the whole line (a location). links={"mu": "log"} puts a log link on a location; an identity link on a positive parameter is refused, because it could take the parameter below 0, where the likelihood is not defined.

Parameters. params are the distribution’s parameters at \(z = 0\) (at the covariate means with center=True), then each parameter’s coefficients on its link’s scale, named <parameter>_<column>.

The sign. The coefficients follow SurPyval’s sign convention, that of its AFT and PH models: a positive coefficient means a shorter life. Each parameter’s sign \(s_k\) (model.coefficient_signs) says how its coefficients enter, one rule for each kind of parameter:

  • A scale (a Weibull’s, log-logistic’s or ExpoWeibull’s alpha, a Rayleigh’s sigma): \(s = -1\), so \(\alpha(z) = \alpha e^{-b'z}\) and a positive coefficient shrinks the scale, exactly as the AFT model’s coefficient does.

  • A rate (an Exponential’s failure_rate, a Gamma’s beta): \(s = +1\), so \(\lambda(z) = \lambda e^{b'z}\) and a positive coefficient raises the rate, again the AFT model’s coefficient.

  • A location (mu of a LogNormal, Normal, Gumbel, GumbelLEV or Logistic): \(s = -1\), so \(\mu(z) = \mu - b'z\) (or \(\mu e^{-b'z}\) with a log link) and a positive coefficient moves failures earlier; for the LogNormal it is the AFT model’s coefficient.

  • A shape that orders lives, one whose increase makes every quantile later (a Gamma’s shape alpha, an ExpoWeibull’s mu, a Beta’s alpha): \(s = -1\), so a positive coefficient lowers it and shortens lives; a Beta’s beta, whose increase makes values earlier, has \(s = +1\).

  • A shape or spread with no direction (a Weibull’s or log-logistic’s shape beta, every sigma of a location-scale distribution): it makes some quantiles earlier and others later, so “shorter life” does not say which way it should go. It keeps the link’s sign, \(s = +1\): a positive coefficient raises the shape or the spread.

Beside the other regressions:

What a positive coefficient means

Model

A positive coefficient

PH (WeibullPH, CoxPH, RoystonParmarPH, …) and AFT (WeibullAFT, BuckleyJames, …)

a shorter life (a higher hazard, or faster ageing)

PO (PO(dist), ProportionalOdds, RoystonParmarPO)

higher survival odds: a longer life

Distributional regression (WeibullDistributional, …)

on a scale or a location, a shorter life, as AFT; on each parameter as model.coefficient_signs says

PO differs because it models the odds of surviving: a positive coefficient multiplies those odds, so it lengthens life.

R’s gamlss and flexsurvreg (and lifelines and survreg) give every coefficient the link’s sign, \(s = +1\), so their coefficients on a scale or a location are SurPyval’s with the sign changed, and their coefficients on a shape or spread are SurPyval’s as they are. SurPyval 0.26 did the same; see the changelog for 0.27. A model saved by 0.26 (to_dict or pickle) is converted on loading and predicts as it did.

A Weibull PH model with covariates is also one, covariates on alpha with b_PH = beta * alpha_z; and the accelerated life models with a log-linear life (Power: alpha on np.log(V)) are too. The tests check each of these gives the same fit.

Inference. Wald bounds come from the exact observed information; method="lr" gives likelihood-ratio bounds and method="bootstrap" parametric bootstrap bounds, for the parameters (param_cb), the functions (cb), the quantiles (quantile_cb) and each row’s parameters (parameter_cb, computed on the link’s scale, so a shape’s bound stays above 0). A calibration study (in the test suite) checks their coverage. parameters_at(Z) gives each row’s parameters and distribution_at(row) the univariate distribution of one unit, with every univariate function.

Tests of the covariates. likelihood_ratio_test() asks whether the covariates, on all the parameters together, explain anything: the null model is the distribution fitted alone to the same data, and twice the gain in log-likelihood is compared with a chi-squared distribution with one degree of freedom per estimated coefficient. With a parameter named, it tests that parameter’s covariates alone: parameter="beta" asks whether the shape depends on the covariates, against the fit with the shape’s coefficients at 0 and the scale’s kept (refitted), on one degree of freedom per coefficient of the shape. Aliased coefficients and those held at 0 are not counted; a coefficient held at another value refuses the test, since the null model is then not inside the fitted one.

model.likelihood_ratio_test()                  # every coefficient
model.likelihood_ratio_test(parameter="beta")  # the shape's alone

The result is a dictionary, {"statistic", "df", "p_value", "log_likelihood", "log_likelihood_null"}, as for the other regressions.

Checks of the fit. Each parameter’s columns are checked on their own: a column that is constant, or a combination of that parameter’s other columns, is aliased (reported as nan, with one warning). A search that does not reach a verified maximum says so, and data with no finite maximum (for example a group whose failures all fall at one time, whose shape then grows without bound) give a “No finite maximum” warning.

Smooth terms

A spline basis of fixed size in the formula, bs(load, df=3) or cr(load, df=3, constraints="center"), lets a parameter follow a smooth curve in a covariate rather than a straight line. The basis is fixed before the fit, so the fit and its bounds are ordinary maximum likelihood; compare a few sizes by AIC:

curved = surv.WeibullDistributional.fit_from_df(
    bearings, x_col="hours", c_col="censored",
    formulas={"alpha": "load", "beta": "bs(load, df=3)"},
)
print(f"AIC: straight line {model.aic():.1f}, spline {curved.aic():.1f}")

AIC: straight line 3095.2, spline 3099.0

Here the straight line on the log scale is enough (the simulation used one), and the AIC says so. cr() without constraints="center" includes the intercept’s direction, so one of its columns is aliased (the fit says so); bs() does not. A fitted spline predicts, saves and loads through its formula. Penalised splines, whose flexibility is chosen from the data, are not available yet.

Limits

  • The distributions are the continuous ones whose support does not depend on their parameters: Exponential, Rayleigh, Weibull, Gamma, LogNormal, LogLogistic, Normal, Gumbel, GumbelLEV, Logistic, Beta and ExpoWeibull. Prebuilt fitters are WeibullDistributional, ExponentialDistributional, GammaDistributional, LogNormalDistributional, LogLogisticDistributional, NormalDistributional, GumbelDistributional and LogisticDistributional; Distributional(dist) builds the others.

  • A covariate that changes over a unit’s life is not supported: sf_tvc and the other path functions refuse, because with the shape moving there is no accumulated age to carry from one value to the next. Use an AFT or PH model for a covariate path.

  • More parameters need more data: covariates on the shape typically need several failures at each covariate level.

The fits are checked against R’s flexsurvreg(anc = ...) (with left truncation) and gamlss with gamlss.cens (observed, right, left and interval censored data together) for the Weibull, Gamma, LogNormal, Normal, Logistic and largest-extreme Gumbel.

References: Rigby and Stasinopoulos (2005), Generalized additive models for location, scale and shape, Applied Statistics 54(3); Jackson (2016), flexsurv: a platform for parametric survival modeling in R, Journal of Statistical Software 70(8); Meeker and Escobar (1998), Statistical Methods for Reliability Data, on accelerated tests whose shape depends on stress.
