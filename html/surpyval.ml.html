Machine Learning

Tree-based survival models: a survival tree, and a random survival forest built from them. Both accept the full surpyval data model – arbitrary censoring and truncation – and return a fitted model at each leaf (parametric, or a Nelson-Aalen or Turnbull estimate for kind="non-parametric"), so a prediction is a distribution rather than a point. Import them from surpyval.ml.

The tree kind couples the split with the leaf model, and every kind takes every kind of censoring and truncation:

kind

Observed and right-censored data, with or without left truncation

Left- or interval-censored data, or right truncation (any truncation)

"non-parametric"

Risk-set log-rank split; Nelson-Aalen leaves

Turnbull-score split (log-rank scores of the pooled Turnbull estimate, each less the score of its truncation window); Turnbull leaves

"exponential"

Exponential deviance split; Exponential leaves

The same, on the full likelihood

"weibull" (default)

Weibull deviance split; Weibull leaves

The same, on the full likelihood

For "non-parametric" the column is chosen at each node (and leaf) from the rows that reach it. Either selection ("greedy" or "ctree") works with every kind and data type.

Note

Before v0.27 these lived in the beta tier as surpyval.beta.ml. They are now part of the stable surface, with the same stability promise and deprecation cycle as the rest of the package. The old name still imports them, with a FutureWarning, until the first release on or after 12 April 2027 (see Machine Learning (beta)).

For a narrative introduction see the survival-forest section of Regression Modelling with SurPyval.

A few things worth knowing:

  • Fitted from a DataFrame (fit_from_df with Z_cols or a formula, or fit with a DataFrame Z), a tree or forest keeps the covariate names as feature_names; print(tree) shows its splits by name (temp <= 42), a forest’s feature_importances() method returns a pandas.Series keyed by name, and predictions read a DataFrame by those names. Fitted from arrays the covariates are shown as Z0, Z1, …

  • A "weibull" or "exponential" tree grows until min_leaf_samples or min_leaf_failures stops it, which suits a forest. For a tree used on its own, set min_split_gain="aic" (or "bic", or a log-likelihood gain), or use selection="ctree".

  • On observed and right-censored data the likelihood splits are found directly (no optimiser per candidate), at a cost of the same order as the log-rank split’s; with left or interval censoring or truncation each candidate needs an optimiser and growing a forest takes much longer. A parametric leaf is fitted when the tree first predicts, so the first prediction of a "weibull" forest can take longer than growing it.

  • sf(x, Z) (and ff, df, hf, Hf) pair row i of a covariate matrix with the time x[i], as the regression models do: one row is used at every time and one time for every row, and other counts are refused. grid=True gives every time for every row, shape (len(Z),) + x.shape, which was the default before 0.24.

  • A forest grows its trees one after another by default. n_jobs (as in joblib and scikit-learn; -1 for every core) grows them in worker processes; given a random_state the forest is the same whatever n_jobs is.

Competing-risks trees

With several causes of failure, a tree grown on “any failure” splits on whatever changes the overall risk, and its leaves cannot say which cause a unit is at risk of. Give SurvivalTree.fit the cause of each failure as e (a missing value for a right-censored row; fit_from_df takes it as e_col) and the tree becomes a competing-risks tree:

  • Splits that tell the causes apart (split). The default, "cause-specific", scores a cut by the sum over the causes of the squared cause-specific log-rank statistics (each cause’s failures as the events, everything else censored): the chi-squared statistic of “this cut changes no cause’s hazard”, so a cut that raises one cause and lowers another counts, rather than cancelling. "gray" scores a cut by Gray’s two-sample statistic for one nominated cause’s cumulative incidence (event=); it targets the incidence itself, but costs a full Gray test per candidate cut, so it is much slower on a large node, and it refuses delayed entry, as gray_test does. With one cause the cause-specific rule is the plain log-rank split.

  • A competing-risks model in each leaf. For kind="non-parametric" the Aalen-Johansen estimate (CompetingRisks, with the Kaplan-Meier all-cause survival, so sf is one less the sum of the incidences); for "weibull" or "exponential" one distribution per cause, each fitted to the cause-specific view of the leaf as a plain leaf of that kind is, assembled as ParametricCompetingRisks. A cause with no failure in a leaf has zero incidence there, and print(tree) says so. min_leaf_failures counts every failure; for a parametric kind each child must also keep that many failures of each cause the node has at least twice that many of.

  • Predictions per cause. cif(x, Z, event) is the cumulative incidence of a cause where the covariates put a unit, total_cif(x, Z) the sum over the causes, and sf, ff, Hf, hf and df the all-cause functions; the shapes are those of sf.

A tree with causes takes exact and right-censored times, with delayed entry (tl) for the cause-specific rule. Left- and interval-censored rows and right truncation are refused for now (fit CompetingRisks or ParametricCompetingRisks to such data), as are selection="ctree" and a non-zero min_split_gain, whose tests and likelihoods are of a single event. Without e a tree is exactly as before.

Competing-risks forests

RandomSurvivalForest.fit takes e (and split, event) the same way, and grows every tree as a competing-risks tree for every cause in the data, even where a tree’s bootstrap sample misses a rare cause (that tree then gives it zero incidence).

  • cif(x, Z, event) is the average over the trees of their leaf incidences of the cause, total_cif the sum over the causes, and sf the average all-cause survival.

  • oob_log_likelihood() scores each failure by the ensemble’s sub-density of its own cause, the slope of that cause’s cumulative incidence at its time, and each censored row by the all-cause survival: the competing-risks likelihood, so a forest that gets the time right but the cause wrong scores badly. oob_log_likelihood(event=k) restricts it to cause k: the cause-specific likelihood (a failure from k scores the cause’s own density, every other row its own survival), which judges the cause’s hazard alone.

  • feature_importances(event=k) shuffles each feature and measures the drop in that restricted score, so a feature that changes only one cause’s risk is important for that cause and near zero for the others; without event it uses the full score.

  • mortality(x, Z, event=k) sums the cause’s incidence over the times (the expected time lost to the cause), and score(x, Z, c, e=e, event=k) is its concordance with the failures from k, a failure from another cause counting as later than all of them (Wolbers et al.).

Random Survival Forest

class surpyval.ml.forest.forest.RandomSurvivalForest(data: SurpyvalData, Z: ArrayLike | NDArray, n_trees: int = 100, max_depth: int | float = inf, min_leaf_samples: int = 5, min_leaf_failures: int = 2, n_features_split: int | float | str = 'sqrt', bootstrap: bool = True, kind: str = 'weibull', selection: str = 'greedy', alpha_split: float = 0.05, random_state: Any = None, feature_names: list[str] | None = None, min_split_gain: float | str = 0.0, n_jobs: int = 1, split: str | None = None, event: Any = None)

Bases: RegressionDataFrameMixin, SerialisableMixin

Random survival forest: an ensemble of survival trees.

n_trees instances of SurvivalTree are fitted, each to an independently bootstrapped sample of the data and each considering a random subset of the features at every split. A prediction evaluates all of them and averages the fitted models their leaves return, which trades the variance of one deep tree for the bias of an average.

Constructed by fit() rather than directly. A fitted forest keeps its training data (data and Z, after dropping rows with a missing covariate) and bootstrap_indices, the rows of data each tree was grown on (with repeats); oob_log_likelihood() and feature_importances() use them to score every row with the trees that did not see it.

A forest fitted from a DataFrame (fit_from_df, or fit with a DataFrame Z) keeps the covariate names as feature_names (None when fitted from an array); its trees’ split descriptions and feature_importances() use them, and its predictions accept a DataFrame Z, read by those names.

Grown with the cause of each failure (e), every tree is a competing-risks tree (see SurvivalTree), grown for every cause of the forest’s data whether or not its bootstrap sample has it: cif() is the average of the trees’ leaf incidences of a cause, total_cif() their sum, sf the average all-cause survival, and the out-of-bag score and importances judge the cause of each failure (oob_log_likelihood()).

feature_importances(n_repeats: int = 5, random_state: Any = None, event: Any = None) → NDArray

Permutation importance of each feature, by out-of-bag log-likelihood.

For each feature, its values are shuffled among each tree’s out-of-bag rows (Breiman, 2001), which breaks its link with the outcome while keeping its distribution, and the out-of-bag log-likelihood (see oob_log_likelihood()) is computed again. A feature’s importance is the drop from the unshuffled value, averaged over n_repeats shuffles: about zero for a feature the forest does not use, positive for one it relies on, and on the scale of the log-likelihood per observation.

Each drop is the mean over the same rows before and after the shuffle, the rows whose out-of-bag log-likelihood is finite in both. A row the ensemble gives zero probability (see oob_log_likelihood()) has a log-likelihood of -inf, and a drop from or to -inf is no number at all: such a row is left out (of every feature’s importance if it is -inf unshuffled, of that shuffle’s drop if it becomes -inf only when shuffled), with one warning giving the counts. Grow more trees, or use kind="exponential", so that every row is scored.

For a forest grown with causes the score is the competing-risks out-of-bag log-likelihood, so a feature’s importance is what it adds to predicting the time and the cause of failure; with event, the score restricted to that cause (see oob_log_likelihood()), so the importance is what the feature adds to predicting that cause’s hazard.

Parameters:

  • n_repeats (int, optional) – The number of shuffles averaged for each feature. Defaults to 5.

  • random_state (None, int or numpy.random.Generator, optional) – Seeds the shuffles. None (the default) draws from NumPy’s global random state, so np.random.seed reproduces it; a seed or Generator gives its own stream.

  • event (optional) – For a forest grown with causes, the cause whose restricted score is used; None (the default) is the full competing-risks score (or, without causes, the plain one).

Returns:

One importance per column of Z, indexed by the feature names (feature_labels: feature_names for a forest fitted from a DataFrame, Z0, Z1, … otherwise).

Return type:

pandas.Series

Raises:

ValueError – If n_repeats is not a positive integer, or on a forest restored with from_dict (see oob_log_likelihood()).

  • selection (str, optional) – How each node chooses its feature: "greedy" (the default) or "ctree" (conditional inference, which also stops a tree where the data show no effect); see SurvivalTree.

"bic" is k log(d) / 2, with d the node’s failures (rows not right censored, counted n times; its units if it has none), as every BIC in SurPyval counts them: the split must lower the Bayesian criterion.   • event (optional) – With e, the cause the splits target: required by split="gray"; None (the default) is every cause.

The fitted forest.

>>> from surpyval.ml import RandomSurvivalForest
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
>>> Z = rng.uniform(0, 1, (200, 2))
>>> x = rng.weibull(2.0, 200) * np.where(Z[:, 0] > 0.5, 5.0, 10.0)

... )

...     x, Z, c=c, n_trees=5, max_depth=1, kind="exponential",
...     random_state=1,
... )
>>> bool(np.array_equal(a.sf(5, Z[:3]), b.sf(5, Z[:3])))
True

>>> t_a = rng.exponential(np.where(Z[:, 0] > 0.5, 2.0, 10.0))
>>> t_b = rng.exponential(10.0, 200)

>>> e = np.where(t_a < t_b, "a", "b").astype(object)
>>> e[xe == 8.0] = None
>>> cr = RandomSurvivalForest.fit(
...     xe, Z, e=e, n_trees=20, max_depth=2, kind="exponential",
...     random_state=0,
... )
>>> cr.causes
['a', 'b']
>>> cr.cif(5.0, [[0.2, 0.5], [0.8, 0.5]], "a").round(2)
array([0.39, 0.78])

classmethod fit_from_df(df: pd.DataFrame, x_col: str | None = None, Z_cols: str | list[str] | None = None, c_col: str | None = None, n_col: str | None = None, tl_col: str | None = None, tr_col: str | None = None, xl_col: str | None = None, xr_col: str | None = None, formula: str | None = None, e_col: str | None = None, *, unit=None, **fit_options: Any) → Any

Fit to data held in the columns of a pandas.DataFrame.

The column names are passed in place of the arrays fit() takes, with the names of every regression fit_from_df; every other fit() option is passed to it unchanged. The columns are handed to fit() as they are, so the result, and the treatment of a missing covariate, is that of fit() on the same arrays. The fitted model keeps the covariate names as feature_names (and the formula): its split descriptions and feature importances are named by them, and it predicts from a DataFrame by them.

Parameters:

  • df (pandas.DataFrame) – The data.

  • x_col (str, optional) – Column of observed times. Required unless xl_col and xr_col are given.

  • Z_cols (str or list of str, optional) – Column(s) of the covariates, in the order fit() reads them (the columns of Z). Exactly one of Z_cols and formula must be given.

  • c_col (str, optional) – Column of censoring flags.

  • n_col (str, optional) – Column of counts.

  • tl_col (str, optional) – Columns of left / right truncation.

  • tr_col (str, optional) – Columns of left / right truncation.

  • xl_col (str, optional) – Columns of the left and right ends of each observation’s interval, in place of x_col (with c_col giving the censoring of each row, as for fit() with xl/xr). Given with x_col, each row is an exact time in x_col or a range in these, the other blank.

  • xr_col (str, optional) – Columns of the left and right ends of each observation’s interval, in place of x_col (with c_col giving the censoring of each row, as for fit() with xl/xr). Given with x_col, each row is an exact time in x_col or a range in these, the other blank.

  • formula (str, optional) – A formulaic formula for the covariates, e.g. "age + C(sex)", as for the regression models’ fit_from_df: categoricals get reference-level columns and the intercept is dropped. Its columns are the features.

  • e_col (str, optional) – Column of the cause of each failure, a blank cell for a right-censored row, passed to fit() as e (a SurvivalTree grown with causes; a model whose fit takes no e refuses it).

  • **fit_options – Every other option of fit().

Returns:

The model fit() returns, with feature_names (and formula) set.

Return type:

model

Raises:

ValueError – If df is not a DataFrame, a name is not one of its columns, or the times or covariates are not given exactly once.

oob_log_likelihood(event: Any = None) → float

The mean out-of-bag log-likelihood per observation.

Each row of the training data is scored by the ensemble of the trees whose bootstrap sample left it out, so by trees that never saw it: its contribution is the log of

  • the density \(f(x)\) if it was observed at \(x\),

  • \(S(x)\) if right censored at \(x\),

  • \(1 - S(x)\) if left censored at \(x\),

  • \(S(x_l) - S(x_r)\) if interval censored in \((x_l, x_r]\),

divided by \(S(t_l) - S(t_r)\) if it is truncated to \((t_l, t_r]\), where \(S\) and \(f\) are the averages of the out-of-bag trees’ leaf survival functions and densities. A censored row’s interval is first cut to its truncation window, as in the fitters’ likelihoods. The result is the count-weighted mean over the rows, so higher is better and it estimates the expected log-likelihood of a new observation; it works for every censoring type and truncation, where the concordance of score() needs orderable event times.

A non-parametric leaf is a step function, which puts no probability at an out-of-bag event time unless the tree saw a tied one, so for this score it is read as a continuous distribution: its survival curve is joined linearly between the points where it drops, from 1 at time 0 (or at the smallest time, if it is negative), and continued past its last drop with the constant hazard it averaged up to there (Brown, Hollander and Korwar’s exponential tail). Its density is then per unit of time, on the same scale as a parametric leaf’s, so forests of different kind can be compared by this score.

A row that is in the bootstrap sample of every tree has no out-of-bag prediction; it is left out of the mean, with one warning giving the count (every row, and a NaN result, with bootstrap=False). A row the ensemble gives zero probability makes the mean -inf, with one warning giving the count of such rows: with few trees a row can land only in leaves that put no density at its time (a steep Weibull leaf grown on bunched failures, or a step leaf); grow more trees, or use kind="exponential", whose leaves give every time a density.

A forest grown with causes (e) is scored by the competing-risks likelihood: a failure from cause \(k\) at \(x\) contributes the cause’s sub-density \(f_k(x)\), the slope of the ensemble’s cumulative incidence \(F_k\) of that cause there (the average of the trees’ leaf sub-densities), so the score judges the cause of each failure as well as its time; a censored row contributes the all-cause \(S(x)\), and delayed entry divides by \(S(t_l)\). An Aalen-Johansen leaf is read continuously as above, each cause’s incidence joined linearly between the all-cause drops. With event, the score is restricted to that cause: the cause-specific likelihood, in which a failure from event contributes the cause’s net density \(h_k(x) S_k(x)\) and every other row (a censoring, or a failure from another cause) its net survival \(S_k(x)\), with delayed entry dividing by \(S_k(t_l)\); it judges the predictions of that cause’s hazard only, and for one parametric tree the causes’ restricted scores add up to the full one. See oob.

Parameters:

event (optional) – For a forest grown with causes, the cause to restrict the score to; None (the default) is the full competing-risks likelihood (or, without causes, the plain one).

Returns:

The mean out-of-bag log-likelihood per observation.

class surpyval.ml.forest.tree.SurvivalTree(data: SurpyvalData, Z: NDArray, max_depth: int | float = inf, min_leaf_samples: int = 5, min_leaf_failures: int = 2, n_features_split: int | float | str = 'sqrt', kind: str = 'weibull', selection: str = 'greedy', alpha_split: float = 0.05, random_state: Any = None, feature_names: list[str] | None = None, min_split_gain: float | str = 0.0, split: str | None = None, event: Any = None, causes: list | None = None)

Bases: RegressionDataFrameMixin, SerialisableMixin

A Survival Tree, for use in RandomSurvivalForest.

The Tree is built on initialisation. Supports the full SurPyval data model: observed, left-, right- and interval-censored observations with optional left and/or right truncation.

The tree’s kind couples the split criterion with the matching leaf model, so every split greedily improves the model the tree predicts with:

  • "weibull" (default): full-likelihood Weibull deviance split (a 2-d.f. likelihood-ratio gain, with power against scale and shape differences) with Weibull MLE leaves. Supports the full data model.

  • "exponential": exponential deviance split (Davis & Anderson, 1989; 1-d.f., splits on rate) with Exponential MLE leaves. Supports the full data model.

  • "non-parametric": for observed / right-censored data (optionally left-truncated), the risk-set log-rank split with Nelson-Aalen leaves. For data with left or interval censoring or right truncation, the Turnbull-score split – the standardised sum of each child’s log-rank scores under the node’s pooled Turnbull estimate (Finkelstein, 1986), which reduces to the log-rank scores on right-censored data; a truncated row’s score is that of its truncation-conditioned likelihood – with Turnbull leaves. Supports the full data model.

selection decides how a node chooses the feature it splits on:

  • "greedy" (default): the best cut of the kind’s criterion over every feature drawn for the split. A feature with many distinct values offers more cuts, so it is favoured even when it carries no information, and a node always splits if some cut is allowed.

  • "ctree": conditional inference (Hothorn, Hornik and Zeileis, 2006). Each feature is tested for association with the scores of the kind’s split statistic (the log-rank scores for "non-parametric"; the working model’s score contributions for "exponential" and "weibull"), by its maximally selected statistic over its cuts, whose p-value allows for the number of cuts. The feature with the smallest p-value is chosen, and the node splits only if that p-value, Bonferroni-adjusted for the number of features tested, is below alpha_split; its cut is then chosen by the kind’s criterion. This removes the preference for features with many values and stops the tree where the data show no effect. See conditional_inference.

With the cause of each failure (e), the tree is a competing-risks tree: it splits by a rule that tells the causes apart (split; see cause_specific_split), each leaf is a competing-risks model of the kind (CompetingRisksLeaf), and cif() gives each cause’s cumulative incidence at given covariates, total_cif() their sum and sf the all-cause survival.

Predictions take one value per covariate the tree was grown on, in its column order (or a DataFrame with its columns); another number raises a ValueError (#657).

Cause-aware splits

Splits of a survival tree that tell the causes of failure apart.

A tree grown with the cause of each failure (SurvivalTree.fit(..., e=...)) chooses its cuts by one of two rules:

  • "cause-specific" (the default): for each cause \(k\), the log-rank statistic \(L_k\) of the cut with cause \(k\)’s failures as the events and every other row (another cause’s failure, or a censoring) censored – the cause-specific log-rank test. The cut chosen maximises \(\sum_k L_k^2\). Under no effect of the cut the cause-specific statistics are asymptotically independent (the causes’ counting-process martingales are orthogonal), so the sum is the \(\chi^2_K\) statistic of the joint test that the cut changes no cause’s hazard. Squaring keeps a cut that raises one cause’s hazard and lowers another’s from cancelling, as a sum of the signed statistics would. With one cause it is \(L^2\), so the cut is the one log_rank_split() chooses. Nominating a cause (event=) uses that cause’s statistic alone.

  • "gray": Gray’s (1988) two-sample statistic for the cumulative incidence of one nominated cause (event=), as gray_test() computes it. This targets the incidence itself rather than the hazard, but a cut costs a whole Gray test (its variance sums over the event times for each candidate cut), so it is far slower than the cause-specific rule on a large node, and it refuses delayed entry, as gray_test does.

Both take observed and right-censored rows, with delayed entry for the cause-specific rule; the tree refuses left- and interval-censored rows and right truncation with causes.

The statistic is that of gray_test() with the two children as the groups. Each allowed cut is scored by its own Gray computation over the node’s event times, so the cost is the number of cuts times the number of event times.
