Weighted Data

Some data come with a number on each row that says how much that row counts: a weight. SurPyval takes it as w, beside the counts n, in the Kaplan-Meier, Nelson-Aalen and Fleming-Harrington estimators, the parametric distributions, the parametric regressions, Cox regression, Aalen’s additive hazards model, the non-parametric competing-risks estimate (CompetingRisks) and the competing-risks regressions (cause-specific Cox and Fine-Gray). This page says what weights are, when to use w rather than n, why a weighted fit’s intervals are computed differently, and what a weighted fit will not do.

What a weight is

A count n says how many identical units a row records: n = 3 is three units, each seen, with the same time and the same censoring. Writing the row out three times gives the same answer.

A weight w says how many units of the population each unit stands for, or how much it should be up- or down-weighted to undo a bias. Nobody saw w units. Three common sources:

  • Sampling weights. You inspected one pump in every 40 at the small sites and one in every 5 at the big site. Each pump inspected at a small site stands for 40 pumps, each at the big site for 5. Without the weights the big site dominates the answer; with them the answer describes the whole fleet. Survey and registry data work the same way.

  • Inverse probability of treatment weights. In observational data the units that got a treatment (an overhaul, a new supplier, a drug) differ from those that did not. Weighting each unit by one over the probability that it got what it got makes the groups comparable, as in a randomised trial. Adjusted Survival Curves works through it, with the checks on such weights.

  • Inverse probability of censoring weights. If units drop out of follow-up for reasons tied to their risk, each unit still followed is weighted by one over its probability of still being followed.

A weight is any positive number (0.37, 12.5); a count is a whole number. A row may have both: n = 3 identical units, each standing for w = 12.5.

When to use w and when n

Use n when the numbers count units you saw. Use w when they say how much each unit stands for, even if they are whole numbers: survey weights of exactly 40 put in n would be read as forty units seen, and the intervals would be more than six times too narrow. SurPyval refuses a decimal n and says to use w, but it cannot tell whole-number weights from counts.

Why the intervals change

The estimate itself is easy: each unit’s contribution is multiplied by its weight (to the numbers at risk and of failures, or to the likelihood).

The uncertainty is not. The classical formulas – Greenwood’s for a Kaplan-Meier curve, the inverse of the information for a fitted Weibull – assume that the information in the data grows with the number of units, and with weights they would take the total of the weights for that number. Multiply every weight by 10 and they would claim ten times the information from the same data. The honest amount of information depends on how uneven the weights are: a few units with huge weights give a noisy answer however large the total. A useful summary is the effective sample size, \((\sum n w)^2 / \sum n w^2\), which equals the number of units when the weights are equal and is smaller the more uneven they are. A weighted fit prints it, and warns when it is below 20.

So every weighted fit uses a robust variance, one that is valid whatever the weights’ scale, and there is no option of the classical one. The intervals are then built from it exactly as before (log(-log) bounds for a survival curve, Wald bounds on each parameter’s scale), so cb, param_cb, quantile_cb and the rest work as usual.

A worked example

Suppose a study samples long-lived units more often than short-lived ones: of a population of lifetimes, a unit above the median is kept with probability 0.8 and one below it with probability 0.2. The sample then overstates how long units last. Each kept unit stands for one over its probability of being kept:

import numpy as np
import surpyval as surv

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
truth = surv.Weibull.from_params([10.0, 1.5])
t = truth.random(2000, random_state=rng)
censor = rng.uniform(0, 30, 2000)
keep_probability = np.where(t > truth.qf(0.5), 0.8, 0.2)
kept = rng.uniform(size=2000) < keep_probability
x = np.minimum(t, censor)[kept]
c = (censor < t)[kept].astype(int)
w = 1 / keep_probability[kept]

print(surv.Weibull.fit(x, c).params)          # biased: too long-lived
model = surv.Weibull.fit(x, c, w=w)
print(model.params)                           # close to (10, 1.5)
print(model)

Bootstrap bounds of a weighted fit

method="bootstrap" on cb, param_cb and quantile_cb (and mrl_cb of a distribution) of a weighted parametric fit or a weighted parametric regression is the case bootstrap: each resample draws the units with replacement, each keeping its own weight, covariates, time, censoring and entry time, and refits the weighted model. The bounds are the percentiles of the refits, corrected for bias as for unweighted data (and, for the regressions, for skewness: the BCa interval). They do not lean on the normal approximation the Wald bounds make, so they are the ones to compare with when the sample is small or the weights are uneven. The parametric bootstrap of unweighted data simulates from the fitted model, which knows nothing of how the units were sampled; resampling the units does. A seed gives the same bounds at any scale of the weights.

print(model.param_cb("beta"))
print(model.param_cb("beta", method="bootstrap", n_boot=200, random_state=1))

[1.44703451 1.66104382]

[1.45060054 1.65587975]

Since nothing is simulated, left- and interval-censored data are resampled as they are. A limited-failure or zero-inflated fit with weights has no bootstrap (without weights it has, with BCa intervals; see Parametric SurPyval Modelling); discrete fits and time-varying covariate fits have none, weighted or not.

Interval censored data: the weighted Turnbull estimate

Turnbull.fit takes w too, for data the Kaplan-Meier family cannot handle: inspection intervals, left censoring and right truncation. The estimate is the one that best explains the population the weights describe: each unit counts as w units when the estimate shares out the chance of failing among the intervals the unit could have failed in. Multiplying every weight by the same number changes nothing, whole-number weights give the same curve as counting each row that many times (n), and on right censored data its Kaplan-Meier option is the weighted Kaplan-Meier.

What it does not have is a formula for its uncertainty. Greenwood’s formula would read the weights as units seen, and the robust (infinitesimal jackknife) variance of the weighted Turnbull estimate is not built. So cb, quantile_cb, the bounds of rmst and sf_diff refuse it, and its bounds come from bootstrap_cb: the units (a row with n = k is k units) are drawn with replacement, each keeping its weight, the estimate is refitted, and the bounds are the percentiles of the refits. Give a seed (random_state) and the same bounds come back, at any scale of the weights. plot draws the curve without bounds.

# Inspected every 2 time units: a failure is known to lie in (2k, 2k + 2]
left = np.where(c == 1, x, np.floor(x / 2) * 2)
right = np.where(c == 1, np.inf, left + 2)
tb = surv.Turnbull.fit(xl=left, xr=right, w=w)
print(tb.sf([4, 8]))
print(tb.bootstrap_cb([4, 8], n_boot=200, random_state=1))

[0.77788935 0.4703155 ]

[[0.74103421 0.8172031 ]
 [0.42845973 0.51864412]]

A probability plot of weighted left or interval censored or truncated data uses these weighted Turnbull positions (heuristic="Turnbull").

In symbols: with \(\alpha_{ij} = 1\) when piece \(j\) lies in row \(i\)’s interval, the self-consistency (EM) step is

\[p_j \leftarrow \frac{1}{\sum_i n_i w_i} \sum_i n_i w_i \frac{\alpha_{ij} p_j}{\sum_k \alpha_{ik} p_k},\]

the unweighted step with \(n_i w_i\) in place of \(n_i\); delayed entry and right truncation add their unseen “ghost” units in the same way, each carrying its row’s \(n_i w_i\). Without truncation the default algorithm is the EM-ICM, as R’s icenReg::ic_np, whose weights give the same estimate (checked to 1e-8). The Fleming-Harrington and Nelson-Aalen options are applied to the weighted expected numbers at risk and of failures, with Fleming-Harrington’s tied failures at their mean weight as in the weighted FlemingHarrington. The coverage of the 95% bootstrap bounds under informative sampling was 0.946 and 0.930 (Monte Carlo error 0.01) at two times in 500 simulated samples (scripts/studies/weighted_turnbull/coverage.py).

Comparing weighted groups

logrank and pairwise_logrank take w. The numerator compares the weighted observed and expected events in each group; the variance is the design-based one, the sum of the units’ squared weighted score residuals, so the test is R’s robust score test, coxph(Surv(x, d) ~ group, weights = w, robust = TRUE, ties = "breslow")$rscore. Every weighting, strata, tl and t0 take weights, and the result does not depend on their scale. A weighted test answers the population question the weights describe; an unweighted test of weighted data answers a question about the sample, and a test that weighted the counts without this variance would be too confident.

group = (rng.uniform(size=kept.sum()) < 0.5).astype(int)
print(surv.logrank(x, group, c=c, w=w))

SurpyvalData holds the weights too (SurpyvalData(x, c, n, w=w), SurpyvalData.from_dataframe(df, ..., w_col="weight")), and fit_from_df takes w_col (Cox’s too: a row dropped for a missing covariate drops its weight with it).

A Cox model with time-varying covariates takes weights as well, CoxPH.fit_tvc(i, xl, xr, c, Z, w=w) (and fit_tvc_from_df(..., w_col=)), one weight per interval row: give each of a subject’s rows the subject’s weight. Its robust covariance clusters the rows by subject, as R’s coxph(Surv(start, stop, event) ~ z, weights = w, robust = TRUE, id = id), so splitting a subject’s follow-up into episodes on which nothing changes gives the same fit and the same standard errors.

Competing-risks regressions

Both competing-risks regressions take w: CompetingRisksProportionalHazards.fit(x, Z, e, w=w) (model="Cox" or "Fine-Gray"), FineGray.fit(x, Z, e, event=..., w=w) and their fit_from_df(..., w_col=).

  • Cause-specific Cox (model="Cox") is one weighted Cox model per cause, the other causes censored, exactly as CoxPH.fit(..., w=w): R’s coxph(Surv(x, e == k) ~ z, weights = w, robust = TRUE) for each cause k, with R’s tie convention and the robust (Lin-Wei) standard errors.

  • Fine-Gray keeps a unit that failed from another cause in the risk set of the cause of interest, down-weighted by how likely it is still to be followed (G(t-) / G(x-), G the probability of not yet being censored). With weights, each unit counts w times that. G is estimated from the weighted censoring times, so a whole-number weight gives exactly the fit of the row repeated that many times. The standard errors are robust, each unit’s contributions over its time at risk summed (clustered by unit).

R’s survival::finegray(..., weights = w) multiplies the weights into its censoring weights the same way but estimates G without the weights; on the reference data the two differ in the third decimal of the coefficients. cmprsk::crr takes no weights. The weighted Fine-Gray standard errors are Fine and Gray’s sandwich, the default without weights too, with each unit’s influence multiplied by its weight: they include the part that comes from estimating G (from the weighted Kaplan-Meier), so a fit with w = 1 is the fit without w, standard errors included. Those of coxph on finegray’s data hold G fixed and leave that part out; on the reference data the two differ by under 1%. A weighted fit has no model-based covariance: covariance(robust=False) is refused.

The information criteria, the diagnostics (diagnose(), the proportional subdistribution hazards test) and the penalised fit refuse weighted fits; summary() gives robust Wald tests and intervals, and the printout the effective sample size.

from surpyval.univariate.competing_risks import FineGray

# Two causes of failure, and the group of the log-rank example above
kind = rng.uniform(size=x.size) < 0.6
cause = np.where(c == 1, None, np.where(kind, "wear", "shock"))
fg = FineGray.fit(x, group, cause, event="wear", w=w)
print(fg)

In formulas: the weighted Fine-Gray estimate solves

\[\sum_i n_i w_i \delta_i \left(Z_i - \bar Z(x_i)\right) = 0, \qquad \bar Z(t) = \frac{\sum_j n_j w_j W_j(t) e^{\beta' Z_j} Z_j} {\sum_j n_j w_j W_j(t) e^{\beta' Z_j}},\]

with W_j(t) 1 while unit j is under observation, G(t-)/G(x_j-) after it failed from another cause and 0 otherwise, and G the Kaplan-Meier of the censoring with each unit counted n w. The covariance is A^-1 (sum_i n_i w_i^2 L_i L_i') A^-1, A the weighted information and L_i unit i’s score residual over its whole time at risk, as for Cox.

Distributional and Royston-Parmar regression with weights

Distributional regression (WeibullDistributional.fit(..., w=w), any Distributional(...) fitter) and the Royston-Parmar spline regression (RoystonParmarPH.fit(..., w=w), RoystonParmarPO, RoystonParmarRegression(...)) take weights as the other parametric regressions do: the estimates maximise the weighted log-likelihood, every row’s term counted n * w times, and the covariance is the robust (sandwich) one. fit_from_df takes w_col; a row dropped for a missing covariate drops its weight with it. The Wald bounds (cb, param_cb, quantile_cb and a distributional model’s parameter_cb) use the robust covariance, method="bootstrap" is the case bootstrap, and the likelihood-ratio bounds, the likelihood_ratio_test() and the information criteria refuse.

A Royston-Parmar fit places its knots on the event times counted by n, not weighted, so the spline’s shape is the same whatever the weights and their scale, and w = 1 gives the unweighted fit exactly. A weight of 2 is therefore not quite a count of 2 here: a count moves the quantiles the knots sit at and a weight does not. Pass knots to fix them; with the same knots, whole-number weights give the fit of the rows counted that many times. R’s flexsurvspline(..., weights = w) places its default knots at weighted quantiles found by an approximate expansion of the weights (each rounded to a multiple of a hundredth of a unit), so pass the knots to it too when comparing. A left truncation at 0 (the usual “no late entry”) is read as none.

load = rng.uniform(0, 1, kept.sum())  # no effect: coefficients near 0
model = surv.WeibullDistributional.fit(
    x, load, c=c, w=w, covariates={"alpha": [0], "beta": [0]}
)
print(model.params.round(3), model.standard_errors().round(3))

[ 9.593  1.592 -0.1   -0.051] [0.511 0.116 0.096 0.126]

Checked against R: flexsurvreg(..., anc = list(shape = ~ z1), weights = w) and flexsurvspline(..., weights = w) on both scales for the estimates (to 1e-5) and the log-likelihood, and a sandwich computed by hand from numDeriv’s per-row scores and Hessian for the robust standard errors (to 1e-4 relative); flexsurv’s own standard errors with weights are model based.

Competing risks: weighted cumulative incidence

CompetingRisks.fit(x, e, c, n, w=w) (and fit_from_df(..., w_col=)) gives the Aalen-Johansen cumulative incidence of each cause in the population the weights describe. Each unit counts n * w in the numbers at risk and of failures from each cause, so whole-number weights give exactly the estimate of the same rows counted that many times, and w = 1 gives the unweighted estimate. The bounds of cb (on the cumulative incidence, and on sf, ff and Hf) use the robust, infinitesimal-jackknife variance, the one R’s survfit(Surv(x, factor(e)) ~ 1, weights = w) reports as std.err, so they do not change when every weight is multiplied by the same number. With w = 1 they are the robust bounds, close to but not the same as those without w (which use Aalen’s variance). The fit warns if the effective sample size is below 20, and its printout shows it.

In symbols, with \(\hat S\) the weighted all-cause Kaplan-Meier survival, \(h_{jk} = d_{jk} / r_j\) cause \(k\)’s weighted hazard increment at \(t_j\) and \(V_i\) unit \(i\)’s influence on \(\log \hat S\) (the formula below), unit \(i\)’s influence on cause \(k\)’s cumulative incidence is

\[U_{ik}(t) = \sum_{t_j \le t} \hat S(t_{j-1}) \left[ \frac{dN_{ik}(t_j) - Y_i(t_j)\, h_{jk}}{r_j} + h_{jk}\, V_i(t_{j-1}) \right],\]

and the variance is \(\sum_i n_i w_i^2 U_{ik}(t)^2\). The estimate takes no delayed entry, weighted or not; Gray’s test refuses weights (see below).

Penalised fits with weights

CoxPH.fit_penalised, CoxPH.fit_tvc_penalised and the parametric families’ fit_penalised (WeibullPH, WeibullAFT, WeibullPO and the others) take w. A penalised fit divides the log-likelihood by the number of units before adding the penalty; with weights that number is the weighted one, \(N_w = \sum n w\), and the covariates are standardised with the weighted mean and standard deviation (divisor \(N_w\)). Both scale with the weights, so the fit depends only on their proportions, and glmnet’s Cox fit with weights is the same objective (checked to 1e-6). Penalised estimates have no standard errors, weighted or not, and the information criteria stay refused. The details are in Regression Modelling with SurPyval (Penalised regression). The other models’ penalised fits (competing risks, recurrent events, Royston-Parmar, distributional) refuse w.

What a weighted fit will not do

Some bounds and methods are wrong with weights, and refuse with a message saying what to use instead:

  • Likelihood-ratio bounds (method="lr"): the likelihood-ratio statistic of a weighted likelihood is not chi-squared. Use the Wald bounds (the default) or the case bootstrap (method="bootstrap").

  • AIC, BIC, AICc and fit_best: a weighted fit maximises a pseudo-likelihood, which does not compare models.

  • likelihood_ratio_test() of a regression or Cox model: the ratio of weighted pseudo-likelihoods is not chi-squared (it grows with the scale of the weights). Use the Wald tests of summary(), which are on the robust covariance.

  • life_table of weighted data or a weighted estimate: use KaplanMeier.fit(data), or the arrays without the weights for the table of the units themselves.

  • Bayesian fits: fit_bayes, update, prior.from_data, log_likelihood_fn, BayesianParametric.from_draws and the PyMC helpers (log_likelihood, censored_likelihood, to_surpyval) refuse weighted data, since a posterior from the weighted likelihood would treat the weights as units seen. Fit by maximum likelihood with the weights instead.

  • Simultaneous bands and bootstrap_cb of a Kaplan-Meier, Nelson-Aalen or Fleming-Harrington estimate: use cb. A weighted Turnbull estimate is the other way round: cb refuses and bootstrap_cb gives its bounds (above).

  • Rank-based plotting positions (Blom, median ranks, …): a weighted model’s probability plot uses the weighted Kaplan-Meier, Nelson-Aalen, Fleming-Harrington or Turnbull positions.

  • Methods other than maximum likelihood (how="MPP", "MOM", "MSE", "MPS").

  • The Cox model’s residuals and diagnose(), strata and exact ties. These may come later.

  • Gray’s test (gray_test) of weighted data: R’s cmprsk has no weighted version to check one against. Use logrank(..., w=w) on the cause-specific hazards, or compare the groups’ weighted cumulative incidences by their bounds.

  • Aalen’s constant_effects_test(): its null distribution is simulated for an unweighted fit, and a weighted version has not been built or checked. Judge a constant effect from the robust bands of plot() or cumulative_coefficients_cb() (a straight line through 0 inside the band), or test it on the data without w.

  • The fitters that do not take w: the parametric competing-risks models, recurrent events, degradation, mixtures and the frailty models, among others, which refuse weighted data (the Bayesian fits and the life table are above).

  • Reshaping weighted data with censor_at, select and the other record-based methods: index the rows, data[rows], which keeps the weights, or build a new SurpyvalData from the arrays.

Two cautions:

  • Estimated weights. When the weights are themselves estimated (inverse probability of treatment weights from a logistic regression), the robust variance ignores that estimation and is usually a little conservative: intervals slightly too wide. This is what R and lifelines do too.

  • Complex survey designs. With w alone, units are independent draws with unequal weights. Strata, clusters of units sampled together and finite-population corrections are out of scope; for a Cox model or a parametric regression, robust_covariance(cluster) (and summary(robust=True, cluster=...)) gives the variance for units sampled in clusters, and R’s survey package handles full designs.

The formulas

Kaplan-Meier, Nelson-Aalen and Fleming-Harrington use weighted numbers at risk and of deaths, \(r_k = \sum_i n_i w_i Y_i(t_k)\) and \(d_k = \sum_i n_i w_i dN_i(t_k)\). Their variance is the infinitesimal jackknife (R’s survfit(..., weights = w, robust = TRUE)): with \(U_i(t)\) the derivative of \(\log \hat S(t)\) in unit \(i\)’s weight,

\[\widehat{Var}\,\log \hat S(t) = \sum_i n_i w_i^2 U_i(t)^2, \qquad U_i^{KM}(t) = -\sum_{t_k \le t} \frac{dN_i(t_k) - Y_i(t_k)\, d_k / r_k}{r_k - d_k}\]

(with \(r_k\) in place of \(r_k - d_k\) for Nelson-Aalen). Fleming-Harrington’s increment with \(m_k\) tied deaths takes each at the deaths’ mean weight, \(\sum_{l < m_k} a_k / (r_k - l a_k)\), \(a_k = d_k / m_k\), as R’s ctype = 2 does. The restricted mean’s variance is the same jackknife of the area under the curve.

A parametric or regression fit maximises the pseudo-likelihood \(\sum_i n_i w_i \ell_i(\theta)\). With \(H\) the Hessian of its negative and \(s_i\) the score of one unit of row \(i\), the robust covariance is the sandwich (Huber, 1967; Binder, 1983)

\[V = H^{-1} \Big( \sum_i n_i w_i^2 s_i s_i^\top \Big) H^{-1},\]

as R’s survreg(..., weights = w, robust = TRUE) computes it, and every Wald bound follows from it by the delta method. covariance() holds it, and so a regression’s robust_covariance() and summary(robust=True) are the same. A parametric model’s hess_inv is \(H^{-1}\) itself, the inverse Hessian of the weighted log-likelihood with the weights as given (R’s naive.var): it is not the variance of the estimate, and nothing in SurPyval uses it for one, so read covariance() or standard_errors() for a variance; with cluster the meat sums each cluster’s \(n_i w_i s_i\) first, as survreg with cluster() does.

A saved weighted model (to_dict, to_json) keeps each matrix under its own name: "hess_inv" is \(H^{-1}\) and "covariance" is \(V\). It is stamped schema 4, which SurPyval 0.26 refuses with a request to upgrade (0.26 saved \(V\) as "hess_inv", and would read the smaller \(H^{-1}\) as the variance). A weighted model saved by 0.26 still loads, with its robust bounds and no hess_inv, which it did not save. Weighted data (SurpyvalData with w) is schema 3.

The Cox model maximises the weighted partial likelihood, with Efron’s tie correction taking the \(m\) tied deaths at their mean weight (R’s coxph convention), and its covariance is the Lin-Wei sandwich \(\sum_i n_i w_i^2 D_i^\top D_i\), \(D_i\) the dfbeta of unit \(i\) (R’s coxph(..., weights = w, robust = TRUE)); within clusters the weighted dfbetas are summed first. R’s survey::svycoxph gives the same coefficients and standard errors larger by \(\sqrt{n / (n - 1)}\), a finite-sample factor SurPyval, as R’s survival, does not apply.

Aalen’s additive hazards model with weights

In plain words: at each event time Aalen’s model fits a straight-line (least-squares) regression of “who failed now” on the covariates of everyone still at risk. With weights it is a weighted least-squares fit, each unit counting as much as its weight says, and the running sum of the fitted slopes is the cumulative effect \(B(t)\). Its uncertainty is measured by how much each unit moves \(B(t)\), unit by unit, so it does not depend on how the weights are scaled.

At each event time, with \(X\) the rows at risk (a leading 1 for the baseline), \(W\) the diagonal of \(n_i w_i\) over them, \(P\) the ridge penalty of penalizer (0 by default) and \(dN\) the events,

\[dB(t) = (X^\top W X + P)^{-1} X^\top W\, dN(t),\]

and the robust variance of \(B(t)\) sums each unit’s squared influence, its martingale residuals mapped as the increments are:

\[\psi_i(t) = \sum_{s \le t} (X^\top W X + P)^{-1} X_i w_i \bigl(dN_i(s) - Y_i(s) X_i^\top dB(s)\bigr), \qquad \widehat{Var}\, B(t) = \sum_i n_i \psi_i(t) \psi_i(t)^\top.\]

This is R’s timereg::aalen(..., weights = w, robust = 1) (robvar.cum), checked to about \(10^{-15}\). Every bound of the model (cumulative_coefficients_cb, summary and cb on sf, ff and Hf) takes it. With a penalty, \(P\) is penalizer times each covariate’s weighted sum of squares about its weighted mean, over \(\sum_i n_i - 1\), so that it grows with the weights as \(X^\top W X\) does and the fit does not depend on their scale; the price is that whole-number weights then differ from counts (without a penalty they are the same, to the last digit).

The robust formula: HC0, with counts as frequency weights

Every robust (sandwich) standard error in SurPyval, with weights or from robust_covariance() and summary(robust=True) without them, is the plain sandwich above, known as HC0: no small-sample correction is applied. R’s survival (survreg and coxph with robust = TRUE), R’s sandwich package by default and lifelines give the same. R’s survey package multiplies each standard error by \(\sqrt{n / (n - 1)}\), \(n\) the number of units (or of clusters with cluster). The difference is small unless the sample is: about 0.5% at \(n = 100\) (\(\sqrt{100/99} = 1.005\)) and about 5% at \(n = 10\) (\(\sqrt{10/9} = 1.054\)). To match survey, multiply SurPyval’s standard errors by that factor.

The counts n are frequency weights: a row with n = 3 is three units, in the sandwich as in the fit. Without cluster each of the three is a unit (a group) of its own; with cluster the three are members of the row’s cluster. Either way the result is that of the data written out one row per unit, so counting does not change a robust standard error.

Weights and counts with tied failure times (Cox, Efron)

In a weighted Cox model with tied failure times, Efron’s method gives a different answer for one subject with weight 3 than for three identical copies of that subject (or one row with n = 3). The copies are three deaths tied at one time, and Efron’s correction shrinks the risk set as each tied death is processed; a single weighted death is one death, and there is nothing to shrink. R’s coxph does the same (SurPyval follows it).

A worked example: two subjects, A and B, at equal risk; A dies, B is still at risk. Each death adds a term \(\log(\text{its risk} / \text{the risk set's total})\) to the partial log-likelihood, here with every risk 1.

  • A with weight 3 (one row, w = 3): one death of weight 3 against a risk set of weight \(3 + 1 = 4\), so the death term is \(-3 \log 4 = -4.16\).

  • Three tied copies of A (three rows, or n = 3): three deaths, the risk set 4, then 3, then 2 as Efron processes them, so the term is \(-\log 4 - \log 3 - \log 2 = -3.18\).

  • With Breslow’s ties (tie_method="breslow") both are \(-3 \log 4\): Breslow does not shrink the risk set within a tie.

So use the counts n for repeated identical units (several pumps that failed at the same inspection) and the weights w for survey and importance weights, where one unit stands for others that were not seen. The test surpyval/tests/univariate/regression/test_weighted_cox_ties.py checks these numbers against SurPyval’s fits and R’s coxph.

Weights and counts in the log-rank test

The log-rank test does not have the tie problem of the Cox model above. Its weighted observed-minus-expected numerator treats a tied death of weight 3 and three tied copies the same, for every weighting (log-rank, Fleming-Harrington, Gehan, Tarone-Ware, Peto-Peto): there is no shrinking risk set. What differs is how sure the test says it is.

  • Counts n give exactly the answer of writing each row out n times, statistic, degrees of freedom and p-value alike (checked to rounding, 1e-10, with no ties, heavy ties and late entry, pairwise_logrank too). The classical (hypergeometric) variance treats them as that many units seen, which is right for counts.

  • Weights w give the same numerator but a larger variance, so a smaller statistic and a larger p-value than the copies. The variance is the robust one, which counts the units, not the weights: ten copies of a unit are ten independent pieces of evidence, but one unit with weight 10 is still one. Roughly, the statistic is the copies’ times the effective sample size over the total weight, \((\sum w)^2 / \sum w^2 \,/\, \sum w\), so it is lower the more uneven the weights (about a third of the copies’ statistic for weights spread evenly over 1 to 4). Multiplying the weights by a constant changes nothing, as it should.

Use n for repeated identical units and w for sampling or inverse probability weights, where a unit stands for others that were not seen: treating those as copies would claim evidence nobody collected. Two smaller differences: a test with all weights equal to 1 is the robust test, which differs a little from the classical test without w (more with tied times, as the classical variance has a tie correction the robust one has not), and Gehan’s, Tarone-Ware’s and Peto-Peto’s weights use the numbers at risk scaled to a mean weight of 1 per unit, so they too do not match the copies. Gray’s test for competing risks takes counts n only, and they equal copies.

Translating from lifelines and R

  • lifelines KaplanMeierFitter().fit(T, E, weights=w) is KaplanMeier.fit(T, 1 - E, w=w); CoxPHFitter().fit(df, "T", "E", weights_col="w", robust=True) is CoxPH.fit_from_df(df, "T", Z_cols, c_col=..., w_col="w"), with the flag column holding 1 - E.

  • R survfit(Surv(T, E) ~ 1, weights = w, robust = TRUE) is KaplanMeier.fit(T, 1 - E, w=w); survreg(Surv(T, E) ~ z, weights = w, robust = TRUE) is WeibullAFT.fit(T, z, c=1 - E, w=w) (on SurPyval’s parameterisation); coxph(Surv(T, E) ~ z, weights = w, robust = TRUE) is CoxPH.fit(T, z, c=1 - E, w=w); per cause, coxph(Surv(T, cause == k) ~ z, weights = w, robust = TRUE) is CompetingRisksProportionalHazards.fit(T, z, e, w=w); timereg::aalen(Surv(T, E) ~ z, weights = w, robust = 1) is AalenAdditive.fit(T, z, c=1 - E, w=w) (pass weights = df$w: aalen looks for a bare w outside the data).

The reference tests (surpyval/tests/reference/test_weights.py) compare these with R survival and survey and with lifelines, and the calibration study (surpyval/tests/calibration/test_coverage_weights.py) checks the intervals’ coverage under informative sampling, at any scale of the weights, and with estimated treatment weights.
