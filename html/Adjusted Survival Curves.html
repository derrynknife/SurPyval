Adjusted Survival Curves

Two groups are compared: two suppliers, two designs, treated and untreated patients. Nobody assigned the groups at random, so they differ in other ways as well: one supplier’s parts went mostly to the heavy-duty sites, the treated patients were older. The plain Kaplan-Meier curve of each group then shows two things at once, the effect of the group and the effect of everything else that differs between the groups, and cannot tell them apart.

An adjusted survival curve answers a sharper question:

What would the survival curve be if every unit in the sample had been in this group?

Each group’s adjusted curve is about the same set of units, with the same mix of duty, age and so on. A difference between two adjusted curves is then a difference between the groups, and not between the units that happened to be in them. From the curves come the two summaries people ask for: the difference in the chance of surviving to a given time, and the difference in the restricted mean survival time (the average time lived within a horizon), each with an interval.

This page shows the two standard ways of getting adjusted curves, what to check, and when the two disagree. The functions are documented in Comparison Tests and Validation Metrics.

Two ways to adjust

Weighting. Give each unit a weight of one over the probability of being in its own group, given its covariates (a propensity weight). A light-duty part from the supplier that rarely got light duty counts for several parts; a heavy-duty part from the same supplier counts for about one. In the weighted sample each group has the whole sample’s mix of covariates, and the weighted Kaplan-Meier curve of each group is its adjusted curve. The Kaplan-Meier estimator takes the weights as w (see Weighted Data); sf_diff and rmst_diff compare two weighted curves with robust variances; and weight_diagnostics checks the weights. SurPyval does not fit the propensity model: the weights are yours.

Direct adjustment (also called standardisation, or g-computation). Fit a regression model for the lifetime with the group and the other covariates in it. Then, for each group, predict every unit’s survival curve as if it were in that group, leaving its other covariates as they are, and average the predictions over the sample:

\[\bar S_g(t) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat S(t \mid g, Z_i).\]

direct_adjustment does this for a fitted parametric regression (WeibullAFT, LogNormalAFT, WeibullPH and the rest) or CoxPH.

Both need the same thing from you: the covariates that drive both the group a unit ended up in and how long it lasts (the confounders) must be measured and used. Neither can adjust for something that was not recorded.

An engineering example: two suppliers, different duty

A plant buys a seal from suppliers A and B. Supplier B’s seals went mostly to the light-duty pumps, supplier A’s mostly to the heavy-duty ones. The data below are simulated so that the truth is known: heavy duty halves the life, and supplier B’s seals last about 16% longer on the same duty. Units still running at 1,500 hours are censored.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import surpyval as sp

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n = 600
heavy = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)
is_b = rng.binomial(1, np.where(heavy == 1, 0.25, 0.75))
life = 1000 * np.exp(-0.7 * heavy + 0.15 * is_b) * rng.weibull(2.0, n)
df = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "hours": np.minimum(life, 1500),
        "c": (life > 1500).astype(int),
        "supplier": np.where(is_b == 1, "B", "A"),
        "heavy": heavy,
    }
)
pd.crosstab(df["supplier"], df["heavy"])
heavy 0 1
supplier
A 82 221
B 218 79

The plain comparison, a Kaplan-Meier curve per supplier, says that 34 percentage points more of supplier B’s seals are still running at 800 hours:

a, b = df[df["supplier"] == "A"], df[df["supplier"] == "B"]
km_a = sp.KaplanMeier.fit(a["hours"], c=a["c"])
km_b = sp.KaplanMeier.fit(b["hours"], c=b["c"])
naive = sp.sf_diff(km_b, km_a, 800, transform="linear")
print(round(naive["difference"], 3))

0.337

Most of that is duty, not the supplier. On the same duty mix the true difference at 800 hours is 8 points.

Direct adjustment

Fit a regression with the supplier and the duty, and hand it to direct_adjustment with the name of the group. A model fitted with a formula needs the data frame as Z, so that the supplier can be set in it and the formula’s columns rebuilt:

model = sp.WeibullAFT.fit_from_df(
    df, x_col="hours", c_col="c", formula="C(supplier) + heavy"
)
adjusted = sp.direct_adjustment(model, group="supplier", Z=df)
adjusted

Directly adjusted survival curves
  model     : ParametricRegressionModel
  group     : 'supplier', levels ['A', 'B']
  population: 600 units (600 rows), covariates sampled

res = adjusted.sf_diff(800, "B", "A")
print(
    "difference at 800 h: {:.3f} ({:.3f} to {:.3f})".format(
        res["difference"], res["lower"], res["upper"]
    )
)

difference at 800 h: 0.128 (0.068 to 0.187)

The adjusted difference is 13 points, with an interval that holds the true 8 and lies well below the plain comparison’s 34. The restricted mean says the same in hours: on the plant’s duty mix, a seal from B runs for more of the first 1,500 hours than one from A.

rm = adjusted.rmst_diff(1500, "B", "A")
print(
    "extra hours within 1,500: {:.0f} ({:.0f} to {:.0f})".format(
        rm["difference"], rm["lower"], rm["upper"]
    )
)

extra hours within 1,500: 129 (72 to 187)

ax = adjusted.plot()

adjusted.sf(x, level), ff and cb give one group’s adjusted curve and its confidence bounds, and rmst(tau, level) its restricted mean.

By default the curves are averaged over the units the model was fitted to (with their counts and weights). Pass Z to average over another population, for example next year’s planned duty mix, and w to weight its rows.

Weighting

For weighting, each seal needs the probability that a seal on its duty came from its supplier. With one yes/no covariate that is just a proportion within each duty class; with several covariates it would come from a logistic regression fitted elsewhere.

share_b = df.groupby("heavy")["supplier"].transform(
    lambda s: (s == "B").mean()
)
df["w"] = np.where(df["supplier"] == "B", 1 / share_b, 1 / (1 - share_b))

Check the weights before using them:

check = sp.weight_diagnostics(df["w"], df["supplier"], df[["heavy"]])
check["groups"].round(3)

Three things to read:

  • Effective sample size (ess): the number of equally weighted units that would carry the same information. Here each supplier keeps nearly 80% of its units. A group left with a small fraction has uneven weights, and wide intervals to match.

  • Extreme weights (max_share, n_extreme): the largest weight’s share of its group’s total, and the number of weights more than ten times the group’s mean. One unit carrying a tenth of its group means the adjusted curve is close to that unit’s own history.

  • Balance: the standardised mean difference of each covariate between the groups, before and after weighting. Before, duty differs between the suppliers by a full standard deviation; after, by nothing. Below 0.1 after weighting is the usual rule.

flags lists, in plain sentences, anything outside those rules; here it is empty. The weighted comparison:

a, b = df[df["supplier"] == "A"], df[df["supplier"] == "B"]
kw_a = sp.KaplanMeier.fit(a["hours"], c=a["c"], w=a["w"])
kw_b = sp.KaplanMeier.fit(b["hours"], c=b["c"], w=b["w"])
weighted = sp.sf_diff(kw_b, kw_a, 800, transform="linear")
print(
    "difference at 800 h: {:.3f} ({:.3f} to {:.3f})".format(
        weighted["difference"], weighted["lower"], weighted["upper"]
    )
)

difference at 800 h: 0.108 (0.021 to 0.195)

The two routes agree: 11 points by weighting, 13 by direct adjustment, and both intervals hold the truth. The weighted interval is wider. That is the usual trade: weighting assumes nothing about the shape of the lifetime distribution and pays for it in precision. sp.rmst_diff(kw_b, kw_a, 1500) is the weighted counterpart of the restricted-mean difference.

When the two disagree

Each route leans on a different model, and each fails in its own way.

Direct adjustment

Weighting

What must be right

The model for the lifetime given the group and the covariates

The model for the group given the covariates (the propensity)

Typical failure

A missing interaction or curvature; proportional hazards assumed where they do not hold; predictions for covariate values a group never had (extrapolation, which the model does without complaint)

A wrong propensity model, seen as imbalance after weighting; groups that barely overlap, seen as extreme weights and a small effective sample size

Precision

Higher: the model lends strength

Lower, and much lower with extreme weights

How to check

The model’s own diagnostics (model.diagnose(), residuals, an interaction term, a different family)

weight_diagnostics

When the two answers agree, as above, each supports the other. When they do not, something specific is wrong, and the checks say where to look:

  • Balance is poor after weighting: the propensity model is wrong. Fix the weights before reading anything into the difference.

  • The weights are extreme: the groups hardly overlap on some covariate. There are units in one group with no counterpart in the other. Weighting shows it (through the diagnostics); direct adjustment hides it, by extrapolating the regression into the gap. Neither answer is trustworthy there. Restrict the comparison to where the groups overlap and say so.

  • Balance is good and the weights are mild, yet the answers differ: look at the lifetime model. Add the interaction of the group with the covariates, try another family, and compare the model’s curve for each group with the weighted Kaplan-Meier curve.

A medical example: lung cancer survival by sex

In the North Central Cancer Treatment Group lung cancer data (lung in R’s survival), women live longer than men. Women in the study were also slightly younger and in slightly better condition (ECOG performance score). How much of the difference is left once age and condition are held equal?

from surpyval.datasets import load_lung

lung = load_lung().dropna(subset=["ph.ecog"])
Z = lung[["sex", "age", "ph.ecog"]].to_numpy(dtype=float)
cox = sp.CoxPH.fit(lung["time"].to_numpy(), Z, c=1 - lung["status"])
by_sex = sp.direct_adjustment(cox, group=0)
by_sex.levels

[1.0, 2.0]

The group is column 0 of Z (sex, 1 for men and 2 for women). The adjusted chance of living a year, and the difference:

res = by_sex.sf_diff(365, 2, 1)
print("men {:.3f}, women {:.3f}".format(res["sf_b"], res["sf_a"]))
print(
    "difference: {:.3f} ({:.3f} to {:.3f}), p = {:.4f}".format(
        res["difference"], res["lower"], res["upper"], res["p_value"]
    )
)

men 0.337, women 0.525
difference: 0.187 (0.079 to 0.296), p = 0.0007

If every patient were a woman, 52% would be alive at one year; if every patient were a man, 34%: with age and condition held equal, the gap remains. Over the first 700 days:

rm = by_sex.rmst_diff(700, 2, 1)
print(
    "extra days within 700: {:.0f} ({:.0f} to {:.0f})".format(
        rm["difference"], rm["lower"], rm["upper"]
    )
)

extra days within 700: 99 (42 to 157)

ax = by_sex.plot()

A Cox model’s baseline ends at the last observed time, so its restricted mean refuses a horizon beyond it; a parametric regression extrapolates and takes any horizon.

This is an adjusted association. Sex was not assigned, and the adjustment covers age and ECOG score only; whatever else differs between the men and women in the study is still in the difference.

How the uncertainty is computed

An adjusted curve is uncertain for two reasons.

  1. The model was estimated. For a parametric regression the standard error comes from the delta method: the gradient \(g\) of the averaged quantity in the model’s parameters and their covariance \(\Sigma\) give \(g^\top \Sigma\, g\). For a Cox model it is the variance of the baseline hazard plus the coefficients’ part, the same two parts as the bounds of its own predicted curve (CoxPH.cb), carried through the average.

  2. The units are a sample. The average is over the units that happened to be studied. Another sample would have other covariates, so the average of even a perfectly known model would move. This adds the spread of the units’ own predictions, \(\sum_i (v_i - \bar v)^2 / N^2\), where \(v_i\) is unit \(i\)’s predicted survival (or difference, or restricted mean).

covariates="sampled", the default, includes both. Use covariates="fixed" when the rows are the population of interest (a reference duty mix, or exactly this fleet): only the first part is then counted.

The two curves being compared come from one model and one set of units, so they are not independent. sf_diff and rmst_diff take the standard error of the difference itself, not the sum of two separate variances. Intervals for a curve are formed on the logit scale, so they stay between 0 and 1; intervals for a difference are the difference plus or minus a normal quantile times its standard error. robust=True uses the model’s robust (sandwich) covariance in the first part.

The curves are checked against R: a Cox model’s adjusted curves equal the row means of survival::survfit with newdata, and a Weibull model’s curves, differences, restricted means and delta-method standard errors match survival::survreg worked by hand. The Cox curves also match riskRegression::ate (the G-formula behind adjustedCurves’ direct method). Its standard errors can differ from the ones here, by 4% to 13% on the lung data, and the reason is the estimator, not the averaging. Both count the sampling of the covariates the same way (that part agrees exactly). For the model’s own uncertainty, ate uses the empirical (sandwich) influence function of the Cox fit, and SurPyval uses the model-based variance that survfit reports for a single curve. The two agree when the proportional hazards model holds, and drift apart when it fits less well. On lung the same gap shows for a single patient: riskRegression’s standard error of one curve is 10% below survfit’s for one sex and 8% above it for the other.

For the weighted route, the variance is the weighted Kaplan-Meier’s robust one (see Weighted Data). It treats the weights as known. Weights estimated from the same data usually make the true variance a little smaller, so those intervals are, if anything, conservative.

What is not built

  • Fitting the propensity model. Bring the weights; any logistic regression will produce them.

  • Bounds for other model families. direct_adjustment averages the predictions of any regression model with sf(x, Z), but the variance is built for the parametric regression families and CoxPH. For another family, cb, sf_diff, rmst and rmst_diff refuse and say so; refit the model to resampled rows for a bootstrap.

  • Stratified Cox models are refused: put the stratifying variable in the model as a covariate, or use a parametric regression.

  • Doubly robust estimators (which combine the two routes) and adjusted cumulative incidence under competing risks.
