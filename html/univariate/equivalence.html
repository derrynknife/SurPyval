Equivalent Time Scales

One life on two clocks, combined into one age \(T = x_1 + k\,x_2\) (equivalent hours: hours plus \(k\) hours per start), with \(k \ge 0\) estimated by maximum likelihood together with the distribution on \(T\). The data are univariate: x, c, n and t on the main clock, and the second clock’s readings at the same moments as x2 (an array or a SecondaryClock). The likelihood is that of the first clock, with the clock-rate factor \(1 + k\rho_i\) on each failure. The guide, with a worked example, is Equivalent Time Scales.

Pre-built instances: WeibullEquivalence, LogNormalEquivalence, LogLogisticEquivalence, GammaEquivalence and ExponentialEquivalence.

Equivalent time scales: one life measured on two clocks.

Many units age on more than one clock at once. A gas turbine runs for hours and is started and stopped; a pump runs for hours and is cycled; a vehicle is driven for kilometres and for engine hours. Each start (cycle, landing, …) wears the unit as some number of hours of running would, so the age that predicts failure is a combination of the two,

\[T = h + k\,s ,\]

h the first clock (hours, say), s the second (starts) and \(k \ge 0\) what one unit of the second clock is worth in units of the first: “one start is worth \(k\) hours”. This is the equivalent operating hours of gas-turbine practice, and the ideal time scale of the statistics literature (Kordonsky and Gertsbakh, 1993; Duchesne and Lawless, 2000). Equivalence(Weibull) estimates \(k\) from the data, together with the lifetime distribution’s parameters on \(T\), by maximum likelihood.

The likelihood

Each unit is observed at one point \((h_i, s_i)\): at its failure, or still running. Between installation and that point it is assumed to have used its two clocks at a constant ratio, \(\rho_i = s_i / h_i\) starts per hour, so its combined clock runs at \(1 + k \rho_i\) per hour. That makes the model an accelerated-failure-time model on the hours scale, with acceleration factor \(1 + k\rho_i\): the unit’s life in hours has survival \(S(h(1 + k\rho_i); \theta)\), and so

  • a failure contributes the density in hours, \(f(T_i; \theta)\,(1 + k\rho_i)\), \(T_i = h_i + k s_i\);

  • a unit still running contributes \(S(T_i; \theta)\); one that had failed before it was inspected (left-censored), \(F(T_i; \theta)\);

  • a unit that failed between two inspections, at \((h_{a,i}, s_{a,i})\) and \((h_{b,i}, s_{b,i})\) (interval censored), \(S(h_{a,i} + k s_{a,i}; \theta) - S(h_{b,i} + k s_{b,i}; \theta)\): its combined clock passed the failure age between the two, whatever the path between them, so no rate is assumed;

  • a unit that entered observation late, at \((h_{l,i}, s_{l,i})\), is divided by \(S(h_{l,i} + k s_{l,i}; \theta)\).

The data are univariate: one row per unit on the main clock x (with c, n and tl as for any univariate fit), and the subordinate clock’s readings at the same moments, x2 (a SecondaryClock, or an array). Censoring is the unit’s, so the second clock has none.

The factor \(1 + k\rho_i\) is the Jacobian of the change of clock. Leaving it out – fitting the distribution to the \(T_i\) as if they were ordinary failure times – gives a likelihood whose maximum in \(k\) is badly biased. A larger \(k\) stretches every unit’s \(T\), and a density on a stretched clock is lower by the stretch, so without the factor the likelihood falls with \(k\) for a reason that has nothing to do with the data, and the estimate is pulled towards 0 however large the sample. The factor is exactly that stretch for each failure, so it cannot be dropped.

A unit that failed with no hours on its first clock (\(h_i = 0\), \(s_i > 0\)) used only the second: its combined clock is \(T = k s\), and its density is taken on the starts scale, \(f(k s_i; \theta)\,k\). Such a failure is impossible at \(k = 0\), so it bounds \(k\) away from 0.

\(k\) is identifiable only when units differ in how they use the two clocks: if every unit has the same ratio \(\rho\), then \(T = h(1 + k\rho)\) for every unit, and \(1 + k\rho\) is absorbed by the distribution’s scale. The fit refuses such data.

The minimum-CV shortcut of Kordonsky and Gertsbakh (choose \(k\) to make the coefficient of variation of the failures’ \(T\) smallest) is not used: it ignores censoring and truncation and has no likelihood, so it gives no intervals or tests.

References

Duchesne, T. and Lawless, J. (2000). Alternative time scales and failure time models. Lifetime Data Analysis 6, 157-179.

Kordonsky, K. B. and Gertsbakh, I. B. (1993). Choice of the best time scale for system reliability analysis. European Journal of Operational Research 65, 235-246.

Self, S. G. and Liang, K.-Y. (1987). Asymptotic properties of maximum likelihood estimators and likelihood ratio tests under nonstandard conditions. Journal of the American Statistical Association 82, 605-610.

surpyval.univariate.equivalence.Equivalence(dist: Any) → EquivalenceFitter

An equivalent-time-scale fitter for a lifetime distribution: the combined clock \(T = x_1 + k\,x_2\) (equivalent hours: hours plus \(k\) hours per start), \(k \ge 0\) estimated by maximum likelihood together with the distribution on \(T\).

dist is one of Weibull, LogNormal, LogLogistic, Gamma and Exponential; WeibullEquivalence and the others are prebuilt.

Examples

>>> import numpy as np
>>> from surpyval import Equivalence, LogNormal
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
>>> rate = rng.uniform(0.0, 0.4, 400)
>>> T = np.exp(rng.normal(7, 0.5, 400))
>>> hours = T / (1 + 5 * rate)
>>> model = Equivalence(LogNormal).fit(hours, rate * hours)
>>> model.parameter_names
['mu', 'sigma', 'k']

fit(x: ArrayLike | None = None, x2: ArrayLike | SecondaryClock | None = None, c: ArrayLike | None = None, n: ArrayLike | None = None, tl: ArrayLike | None = None, clocks: tuple[str, str] | None = None, init: ArrayLike | None = None, *, t: ArrayLike | None = None, tr: ArrayLike | None = None, unit=None) → EquivalenceModel

Fit the combined clock x + k x2 and the distribution on it by maximum likelihood.

The data are univariate: one row per unit (or n alike), on the main clock, as any univariate fit takes them (x, c, n, t), plus x2, the second clock’s reading at the same moments.

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – Each unit’s age on the main clock (hours, say): at its failure, or when last seen running; for an interval row (c = 2), its [lower, upper] window (an N x 2 array, equal columns elsewhere). k is measured in this clock’s units.

  • x2 (array_like or SecondaryClock) – The second clock’s reading (starts, cycles, landings) at each row’s x: an array (one per row, or N x 2 windows as x), or a SecondaryClock with its entry readings tl and its name. It has no censoring of its own.

  • c (array_like, optional) – Censoring, of the unit on the main clock: 0 failed at that age (the default), 1 still running, -1 failed before it was seen, 2 failed between two inspections (an interval row). By default 2 where x’s window has width, else 0.

  • n (array_like, optional) – The count of units in each row.

  • tl (array_like or float, optional) – Late entry: each unit’s reading on the main clock when observation began. The second clock’s goes in SecondaryClock(..., tl=...). (The 0.28 form, a pair per unit of shape (N, 2), is deprecated.)

  • clocks (tuple of two str, optional) – Names of the two clocks, used when the model describes k (default ("x1", "x2"), the second the SecondaryClock’s name where it has one; fit_from_df uses the columns’ names).

  • init (array_like, optional) – A start for the search: the distribution’s parameters, then k. The default start is searched as well, and the better answer kept.

  • t (array_like, optional) – The truncation window [tl, tr] of each row on the main clock (in place of tl and tr). Only late entry is taken; a finite upper bound is refused.

  • tr (array_like, optional) – Right truncation: refused where finite (it is not supported).

Return type:

EquivalenceModel

Examples

>>> import numpy as np
>>> from surpyval import SecondaryClock, WeibullEquivalence
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
>>> rate = rng.choice([0.02, 0.1, 0.5], 300)   # starts per hour
>>> T = 2000 * rng.weibull(2.0, 300)          # equivalent hours
>>> hours = T / (1 + 10 * rate)               # one start = 10 h
>>> starts = rate * hours
>>> model = WeibullEquivalence.fit(hours, starts)
>>> round(model.k, 1)
9.8

The same, the second clock named:

>>> same = WeibullEquivalence.fit(
...     hours, SecondaryClock(starts, name="starts"),
...     clocks=("hours", "starts"))
>>> same.clocks, bool(np.allclose(same.params, model.params))
(('hours', 'starts'), True)

class surpyval.univariate.equivalence.SecondaryClock(values: ArrayLike, tl: ArrayLike | None = None, name: str | None = None)

Bases: object

The second clock of a two-clock fit (Equivalence()): a subordinate clock (starts, cycles, landings) read at the same moments as the main clock x.

The data stay univariate: one event per unit, on the main clock, with its censoring c, counts n and late entry tl. This object adds the second clock’s readings at those same moments. A bare array given as x2 means SecondaryClock(array).

Parameters:

  • values (array_like) – The second clock’s reading at each row’s x: one per row, or, like x, an N x 2 array of each row’s window for interval rows (c = 2), with equal columns where the row is not an interval.

  • tl (array_like or float, optional) – Its reading when each unit entered observation (late entry), aligned with the main clock’s tl; one number for every row, or one per row.

  • name (str, optional) – The clock’s name (“starts”, “landings”), used in messages and as the model’s name for the second clock (model.clocks[1]).

  • here (There is no censoring)

  • whole

  • alone. (so c belongs to the main clock)

Examples

>>> import numpy as np
>>> from surpyval import SecondaryClock, WeibullEquivalence

>>> rate = rng.choice([0.02, 0.1, 0.5], 300)   # starts per hour

class surpyval.univariate.equivalence.EquivalenceModel

Bases: InformationCriteriaMixin, SerialisableMixin

A fitted equivalent time scale (Equivalence()): the combined clock \(T = x_1 + k\,x_2\) and a lifetime distribution on it.

params are the distribution’s parameters on \(T\), then \(k\), what one unit of the second clock is worth on the first (parameter_names names them). sf, ff, df, hf, Hf, qf, mean and cb are the distribution’s on the combined scale; equivalent() gives a unit’s \(T\); the *_at_usage methods give the life of a unit that uses the second clock at a constant rate per unit of the first (starts per hour), in the first clock’s units, with bounds that include \(k\)’s uncertainty.

>>> T = 2000 * rng.weibull(2.0, 300)
>>> hours = T / (1 + 10 * rate)
>>> model = WeibullEquivalence.fit(hours, rate * hours,
...                                    clocks=("hours", "starts"))
>>> model.parameter_names
['alpha', 'beta', 'k']
>>> model.param_cb("k").round(1)
array([ 8. , 12.1])
>>> float(model.qf_at_usage(0.1, rate=0.1).round(0))  # B10 in hours

Notes

The correction \((2k^2 + 2k)/(N - k - 1)\) uses the same sample size \(N\) as bic(): the number of observed failures (exact, left- or interval-censored, weighted by their counts), or the number of units when there is none. Right-censored units do not add to it. The correction only exists for \(N > k + 1\); otherwise the corrected criterion is undefined and nan is returned (the formula gave inf at \(N = k + 1\), and a value below aic() for smaller \(N\), which would have won a comparison).

Bayesian Information Criterion for Censored Survival Models.

Notes

The sample size \(d\) in the penalty \(k \ln d\) is the number of observed failures: every unit whose failure was observed, exactly or within a left- or interval-censored window, weighted by its count; right-censored units add nothing. Should that be zero, the number of units is used instead, so the criterion is always finite. Every model in SurPyval that reports a BIC counts \(d\) this way (see “Information criteria” in the Parametric Estimation guide).

k_lr_test() → dict

The likelihood-ratio test that the second clock does not matter (k = 0): the statistic, twice the gain in log-likelihood from fitting k, and its p-value. k = 0 is on the edge of the parameter space, so the statistic is referred to an even mixture of 0 and a chi-square with 1 degree of freedom (Self and Liang, 1987): the p-value is half the chi-square’s. Where a unit failed with no age on the first clock, k = 0 is impossible and the p-value is 0.

>>> test = WeibullEquivalence.fit(hours, rate * hours).k_lr_test()
>>> test["p_value"] < 1e-10
True

class surpyval.univariate.equivalence.LifeAtUsage(model: Any, rate: Any)

Bases: object

The lifetime distribution of a unit used at one constant rate: a fitted equivalent-scale model (Equivalence()) with the rate bound, as a plain life model on the first clock.

model.at_usage(rate) returns one. rate is the second clock’s units per unit of the first, in the model’s own units: starts per hour for a model fitted to (hours, starts). A unit used at that rate ages \(a = 1 + k \cdot rate\) units of the combined clock per unit of the first, so its life on the first clock is the combined life divided by \(a\). Every function takes ages (or probabilities) on the first clock only, as a univariate model’s do, and answers on the first clock: sf(x) is model.sf_at_usage(x, rate), and so for ff, hf, qf, mean, cb (cb_at_usage) and quantile_cb (quantile_cb_at_usage); df(x) is the density per unit of the first clock, \(a f(a x)\), and Hf(x) the cumulative hazard \(H(a x)\). The second clock’s age at a first clock age x is rate * x.

random(size) is qf(U) for uniform U, as Parametric.random is, so the same random_state gives common random numbers across models (and the first clock’s ages that model.random(size, rate) draws). The bounds (cb, quantile_cb, rmst) carry the uncertainty of \(k\) and of the distribution together; a model built from given parameters has none, and they refuse, saying so.

It is a view, not a copy: it holds the model and the rate, and every call goes to the model, so it has no to_dict of its own; save the model (model.to_dict()) and the rate.
