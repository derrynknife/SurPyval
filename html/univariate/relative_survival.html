Relative Survival

Net survival and excess hazards beyond a population’s own rates: the population’s hazards in a RateTable, net survival by Pohar Perme’s estimator or Ederer II (RelativeSurvival) and excess hazard regression (ExcessHazard). The guide, with worked examples, is Relative Survival.

class surpyval.RateTable(rates: Any, ages: ArrayLike, years: ArrayLike | None = None)

Bases: SerialisableMixin

Population hazard rates by attained age, and optionally by calendar year and stratum: the expected hazards of a relative survival analysis (surpyval.RelativeSurvival, surpyval.ExcessHazard).

Each band runs from its edge up to the next: an age band from ages[k] to ages[k + 1], the last open above, and the first taken to cover attained ages below it too; the same for years. A unit’s attained age and calendar year advance with its follow-up time, so its expected cumulative hazard is the integral of the rates along that diagonal path through the table (see the module).

Parameters:

  • rates (array_like or dict) – The hazard rates, per unit of time, at least 0: shape (len(ages),), or (len(ages), len(years)) with years. With strata, a dict of such arrays keyed by stratum label ({"female": ..., "male": ...}).

  • ages (array_like) – The lower edge of each age band, strictly increasing.

  • years (array_like, optional) – The lower edge of each calendar-year band, strictly increasing. Without it the rates depend on age alone.

Notes

Ages, years and the follow-up times given to the fits must be in the same unit, and the rates per that unit: per person-year with times in years. A life table’s annual probability of death \(q\) is the rate \(-\log(1 - q)\) per year.

Examples

A table by age and sex, the rates rising 9% a year of age:

>>> import numpy as np
>>> import surpyval as sp
>>> ages = np.arange(0, 101)
>>> table = sp.RateTable(
...     {"F": 5e-5 * np.exp(0.09 * ages), "M": 8e-5 * np.exp(0.09 * ages)},
...     ages=ages,
... )
>>> table
RateTable: 101 age band(s) (0 to 100+), 2 strata ('F', 'M')
>>> float(table.sf(10, age=70, stratum="F").round(4))  # expected survival
0.6557

class surpyval.RelativeSurvival

Bases: SerialisableMixin

Net survival from relative survival data: the survival of the units studied once the hazard of their background population is taken away, estimated by Pohar Perme’s estimator (the default) or Ederer II (see the module surpyval.univariate.relative.net_survival for both).

The expected hazards come from a surpyval.RateTable of the population’s rates by attained age (and calendar year and stratum): each unit’s age, year and stratum at time 0 place it in the table, and its age and the calendar advance with its follow-up.

sf is the net survival \(S_E(t) = \exp(-\Lambda_E(t))\), ff its complement and Hf the cumulative excess hazard \(\Lambda_E\). The observed part steps at the event times and the expected part changes continuously; between two follow-up times the expected part is taken as linear in time (exact where the rates of the units at risk do not change between them). Before time 0 net survival is 1, and after the last follow-up time it holds its last value.

Net survival is not a probability of a set of units: where fewer units fail than the population rates expect it rises, and it can exceed 1. It is reported as it is.

Examples

Simulated patients aged 50 to 80, with population rates rising 9% a year of age and an excess hazard of 0.1 a year:

>>> import numpy as np
>>> import surpyval as sp
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
>>> ages = np.arange(0, 121)
>>> table = sp.RateTable(5e-5 * np.exp(0.09 * ages), ages=ages)
>>> age = rng.uniform(50, 80, 2000)
>>> excess = rng.exponential(10, 2000)
>>> u = rng.uniform(size=2000)
>>> # the population time to death: invert the expected survival
>>> grid = np.linspace(0, 60, 601)
>>> background = np.array([np.interp(-np.log(v), table.Hf(grid, a), grid)
...                        for v, a in zip(u, age)])
>>> time = np.minimum(np.minimum(excess, background), 10.0)
>>> c = (time == 10.0).astype(int)
>>> model = sp.RelativeSurvival.fit(time, c, rate_table=table, age=age)
>>> model.sf([1, 5]).round(3)  # the truth is exp(-0.1 t): 0.905, 0.607
array([0.896, 0.591])
Hf(x: ArrayLike) → NDArray

The cumulative excess hazard \(\hat\Lambda_E\) at x: the observed cumulative hazard less the expected one (see the class). It falls where fewer units fail than the population rates expect.

Examples

cb(x: ArrayLike, on: str = 'sf', alpha_ci: float = 0.05, bound: str = 'two-sided', bound_type: str = 'log') → NDArray

Pointwise confidence bounds on net survival (or on ff or Hf) at x.

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – The times.

  • on ({"sf", "ff", "Hf"}, optional) – The function bounded ("R" and "F" are "sf" and "ff"). Defaults to "sf".

  • alpha_ci (float, optional) – The significance level: 0.05 (the default) gives a 95% two-sided interval.

  • bound ({"two-sided", "lower", "upper"}, optional) – Defaults to "two-sided" ([lower, upper] on the last axis). A one-sided bound puts all of alpha_ci on one side.

  • bound_type ({"log", "normal"}, optional) – "log" (the default, as R’s relsurv): a normal interval for the cumulative excess hazard, \(\hat\Lambda_E \pm z \hat\sigma\), so \(\hat S_E e^{\pm z \hat\sigma}\) on net survival. "normal": \(\hat S_E \pm z \hat S_E \hat\sigma\) on net survival itself. Neither is cut at 1: net survival can exceed it. (The log(-log) scale of the Kaplan-Meier bounds is not offered: the cumulative excess hazard can be 0 or below.) The bounds on ff are one minus those on sf, and those on Hf minus their logarithm, so a "normal" lower bound on survival below 0 gives an upper bound on Hf of NaN.

Returns:

The bounds: the query’s shape, with a last axis of [lower, upper] when two-sided.

Return type:

numpy.ndarray

Examples

>>> import surpyval as sp
>>> table = sp.RateTable([0.01, 0.03], ages=[0, 70])
>>> model = sp.RelativeSurvival.fit(
...     [2, 3, 5, 6, 8], c=[0, 0, 1, 0, 1], rate_table=table,
...     age=[60, 64, 66, 70, 75])
>>> model.cb(3).round(3)
array([0.37 , 1.258])

class surpyval.ExcessHazard

Bases: InformationCriteriaMixin, SerialisableMixin

Excess hazard regression (Estève et al., 1990): the hazard of each unit is its population’s hazard, from a surpyval.RateTable, plus an excess hazard \(\lambda_0(t) e^{\beta' Z}\) that the covariates act on proportionally, \(\lambda_0\) constant within intervals of follow-up time. See the module surpyval.univariate.relative.excess_hazard.

params are the baseline excess hazard rates, one per interval (rate_0, …; the intervals are intervals), then the coefficients, named by their columns (parameter_names). sf, ff, hf, Hf, df and qf are the net (excess-only) functions of a unit with covariates Z: sf(x, Z) is its net survival. observed_sf() is a unit’s all-cause survival.

Examples

Simulated patients aged 50 to 80 whose treatment halves the excess hazard, with population rates rising 9% a year of age:

>>> import numpy as np
>>> import surpyval as sp
>>> rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
>>> ages = np.arange(0, 121)
>>> table = sp.RateTable(5e-5 * np.exp(0.09 * ages), ages=ages)
>>> age = rng.uniform(50, 80, 3000)
>>> treated = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, 3000)
>>> excess = rng.exponential(1 / (0.15 * np.exp(-0.7 * treated)))
>>> grid = np.linspace(0, 60, 601)
>>> u = rng.uniform(size=3000)
>>> background = np.array([np.interp(-np.log(v), table.Hf(grid, a), grid)
...                        for v, a in zip(u, age)])
>>> time = np.minimum(np.minimum(excess, background), 8.0)
>>> model = sp.ExcessHazard.fit(time, treated, c=(time == 8.0),
...                             rate_table=table, age=age)
>>> model.parameter_names
['rate_0', 'coef_0']
>>> model.params.round(3)  # the truth is 0.15 and -0.7
array([ 0.146, -0.716])

Notes

The correction \((2k^2 + 2k)/(N - k - 1)\) uses the same sample size \(N\) as bic(): the number of observed failures (exact, left- or interval-censored, weighted by their counts), or the number of units when there is none. Right-censored units do not add to it. The correction only exists for \(N > k + 1\); otherwise the corrected criterion is undefined and nan is returned (the formula gave inf at \(N = k + 1\), and a value below aic() for smaller \(N\), which would have won a comparison).

References

Bayesian Information Criterion for Censored Survival Models.

Notes

The sample size \(d\) in the penalty \(k \ln d\) is the number of observed failures: every unit whose failure was observed, exactly or within a left- or interval-censored window, weighted by its count; right-censored units add nothing. Should that be zero, the number of units is used instead, so the criterion is always finite. Every model in SurPyval that reports a BIC counts \(d\) this way (see “Information criteria” in the Parametric Estimation guide).

Parameters:

  • x (array_like) – Each unit’s follow-up time from time 0, in the rate table’s unit.

  • Z (array_like) – The covariates, one row per row of x (a DataFrame’s columns name the coefficients).

  • c (array_like, optional) – 0 where the follow-up ended in the event (of any cause), 1 where it was right censored. Defaults to all 0.

  • n (array_like, optional) – Counts of identical units. Defaults to 1 per row.

  • rate_table (RateTable) – The population’s hazards (required).

  • age (array_like) – Each unit’s age at time 0, in the table’s unit (required).

  • year (array_like, optional) – Each unit’s calendar time at time 0, where the table is by year.

  • stratum (array_like, optional) – Each unit’s stratum of the table, where it has strata.

  • breaks (int or array_like, optional) – The intervals of follow-up time the baseline excess hazard is constant on. None (the default, as R’s relsurv): one interval, a constant excess hazard.   • init (array_like, optional) – A start: the baseline rates, then the coefficients. The fit is also started from its own default start, and the better kept.

  • ExcessHazard

  • A row with a missing age, year, stratum or covariate is dropped,

  • with one warning; a missing time is refused. A covariate column

  • that is constant, or a combination of the others, is aliased (its)

  • coefficient is nan, with one warning.
