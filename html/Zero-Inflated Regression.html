Zero-Inflated Regression

Some units fail the moment they are used. They are dead on arrival:

  • a power supply that does not start when it is commissioned;

  • a part found broken when its box is opened;

  • a seedling that never germinates, a device rejected at its first check, a transplant that fails on the table.

In the data these are failures at time zero. An ordinary lifetime distribution cannot place a share of its failures at exactly 0, so a fit either refuses them or bends its whole shape to accommodate them, and gets the life of every other unit wrong.

A zero-inflated model treats the population as two groups. A unit is dead on arrival with some probability, \(f_0\); a unit that works has a time to failure with an ordinary lifetime distribution. A zero-inflated regression lets covariates act on both parts, and so answers two different questions at once:

  • Which units arrive dead? Which suppliers, routes or batches have a higher chance of failing at time 0.

  • How long do the rest last? Among the units that work, what makes failure come sooner.

A covariate can matter for one and not the other. Rough shipping may break more units in transit without shortening the life of those that survive it; a cheaper supplier may make units that both arrive dead more often and wear out sooner. An ordinary regression, with one effect per covariate, cannot tell these apart.

Without covariates this is the zero-inflated model that the distributions already fit (Weibull.fit(x, c, zi=True), see Parametric SurPyval Modelling). ZeroInflated is that model with covariates. It is the mirror image of the mixture-cure regression, whose extra group never fails rather than failing at once. The API reference is Zero-Inflated Regression.

An example: power supplies from two suppliers

A fleet of 1,200 power supplies came from two suppliers (supplier_b is 1 for the second) and travelled different distances to their sites (distance, in thousands of kilometres). Some failed at commissioning (recorded as 0 days); the rest have been in service for 500 to 1,500 days. The data are simulated, so the truth is known: the chance of arriving dead rises with distance and is higher for supplier B, and supplier B’s working units also fail sooner. Distance does nothing to the life of a unit that arrived working.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import surpyval as sp

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 1200
supplier_b = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)
distance = rng.uniform(0, 2, n)
p_doa = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-3.0 + 0.8 * supplier_b + 0.9 * distance)))
doa = rng.random(n) < p_doa
life = 2000 * np.exp(-0.3 * supplier_b) * rng.weibull(1.5, n)
in_service = rng.uniform(500, 1500, n)
failed = ~doa & (life < in_service)
df = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "days": np.where(doa, 0.0, np.where(failed, life, in_service)),
        "c": np.where(doa | failed, 0, 1),
        "supplier_b": supplier_b,
        "distance": distance,
    }
)
print("failed at commissioning:", int(np.sum(df["days"] == 0)))

failed at commissioning: 193

A failure at commissioning is days = 0 with c = 0. Fit the model. WeibullZeroInflated is ZeroInflated(Weibull): a Weibull life for the units that work and a logistic regression for the chance of arriving dead.

model = sp.WeibullZeroInflated.fit_from_df(
    df, x_col="days", c_col="c", Z_cols=["supplier_b", "distance"]
)

How to read it:

  • alpha and beta are the Weibull scale and shape of a working unit’s life at the reference row (supplier A, no distance), and f0 is the probability there of arriving dead: about 5%. It is the same name as the univariate fit’s zero-inflation share.

  • f0_supplier_b and f0_distance are the coefficients of the dead-on-arrival chance, on the logit scale, so exp of one is an odds ratio. A supplier B unit has exp(0.80) = 2.2 times the odds of arriving dead, and each thousand kilometres multiplies the odds by about the same again.

  • alpha_supplier_b and alpha_distance are the coefficients of the life of the units that work, with the sign of SurPyval’s AFT models: positive means shorter. A working supplier B unit has exp(-0.26) = 0.77 times the life of a supplier A unit. Distance shows no effect on the life of a unit that arrived working (alpha_distance is near 0, as simulated): it only breaks units on the way.

Every coefficient has the same sign convention: positive is worse, a higher chance of arriving dead or an earlier failure.

The two parts for a given unit:

rows = pd.DataFrame({"supplier_b": [0, 1], "distance": [1.0, 1.0]})
print("dead on arrival:", model.doa_probability(rows).round(3))
print(
    "median life of a unit that works:",
    model.qf(0.5, rows, susceptible=True).round(0),
)

dead on arrival: [0.101 0.201]

sf, ff, hf and the rest are the population’s: the survival of a unit about to be commissioned, which drops to 1 - f0 at time 0. With susceptible=True they are those of a unit that worked at commissioning.

b = rows.iloc[[1]]

The difference is the spares question. To cover a site for a year, plan for the units that will arrive dead and for the working ones that will fail in service; once a unit is running, only the second applies.

Choosing what acts on what

By default every covariate acts on f0 and on the latency’s life parameter (the scale of a Weibull or log-logistic, mu of a log-normal, the rate of a gamma or exponential). covariates chooses, by parameter; with a data frame, formulas gives each part its own formula:

chosen = sp.WeibullZeroInflated.fit_from_df(
    df,
    x_col="days",
    c_col="c",
    formulas={"f0": "supplier_b + distance", "alpha": "supplier_b"},
)
print(chosen.parameter_names)
print("AIC, distance on both parts:", round(model.aic(), 1))
print("AIC, distance on f0 only   :", round(chosen.aic(), 1))

['alpha', 'beta', 'f0', 'alpha_supplier_b', 'f0_supplier_b', 'f0_distance']
AIC, distance on both parts: 7613.9
AIC, distance on f0 only   : 7612.1

Dropping distance from the life lowers the AIC: the data agree that it does not change how long a working unit lasts. A covariate on the shape is possible too (covariates={"beta": [...]}), as in a distributional regression, of which this model is a member: the fits, checks, bounds (cb, param_cb and parameter_cb with Wald, likelihood-ratio and bootstrap methods), weights, fit_penalised, to_dict and from_dict are that family’s. ZeroInflated(Weibull, incidence="probit") (or "cloglog") changes the link of f0.

When a failure might have been on arrival

Sometimes the data do not say whether a unit was dead on arrival. A site that is only visited at day 30 finds a failed unit, but cannot tell whether it failed at commissioning or on day 12. That unit’s time is recorded as an interval from 0 to 30 (or as left censored at 30: “failed by day 30”). Such a row is evidence for both parts at once, and the model uses it that way: it contributes \(f_0 + (1 - f_0) F_u(30)\), the chance of arriving dead or failing by day 30.

visited = rng.random(n) < 0.3
early = visited & (df["days"] <= 30) & (df["c"] == 0)
inspected = df.assign(
    lo=np.where(early, 0.0, df["days"]),
    hi=np.where(early, 30.0, df["days"]),
    c=np.where(early, 2, df["c"]),
)
joint = sp.WeibullZeroInflated.fit_from_df(
    inspected,
    xl_col="lo",
    xr_col="hi",
    c_col="c",
    Z_cols=["supplier_b", "distance"],
)
print(int(early.sum()), "units found failed at the day-30 visit")
print("route:", joint.fit_route)
joint.summary().round(3)

How the model is fitted

When every row says whether its unit was dead on arrival (a failure at 0 was; a failure or a censoring after 0 was not), the likelihood splits into two pieces with no parameter in common:

  • a binary regression, dead on arrival or not, over every unit; and

  • the latency’s regression, over the units that worked, with all their censoring and truncation.

Each is fitted on its own, and together they are exactly the joint maximum, with a covariance that has no terms between the two parts. This is the two-stage route, the usual one, and it is fast and reliable: neither piece can pull the other off course.

When some rows do not say (left censoring, an interval that starts at 0, or right truncation without late entry), those rows tie the two parts together, and both are searched together from the zero-inflated fit without covariates. This is the joint route.

fit chooses the route (method="auto"); method="joint" forces the joint search, and method="two-stage" refuses data that do not allow it. model.fit_route says which was used. A fit with fixed parameters or an init is searched jointly.

Late entry. A unit that entered observation at an age above 0 (left truncation, tl > 0) was known to be working then, so it says nothing about f0: it is left out of the binary regression and enters only the latency’s, divided by its survival to the entry age. A left truncation at 0 is no truncation.

The model

A unit with covariates \(z\) is dead on arrival with probability \(f_0(z)\), and a unit that works has a life with distribution function \(F_u(t \mid z)\). The population’s distribution function is

\[F(t \mid z) = f_0(z) + \bigl(1 - f_0(z)\bigr)\, F_u(t \mid z),\]

which jumps to \(f_0(z)\) at time 0.

Dead on arrival. \(g(f_0(z)) = g(f_0) + x_{f_0}(z)' b_{f_0}\), with \(g\) the logit (the default; logistic regression), the probit or the complementary log-log link, chosen by ZeroInflated(latency, incidence=...). f0 is \(f_0\) at the reference row.

Latency. A Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic, gamma or exponential distribution whose parameters depend on the covariates through their own links, as in a distributional regression: a scale as \(\alpha(z) = \alpha\, e^{-x' b}\) (the AFT model), a location as \(\mu(z) = \mu - x' b\), a rate as \(\lambda(z) = \lambda\, e^{x' b}\).

Likelihood. Each kind of observation contributes what the population model says of it:

Observation

Contribution

Failed at 0 (dead on arrival)

\(f_0\)

Failed at \(t > 0\)

\((1 - f_0)\, f_u(t)\)

Still working at \(t\) (right censored)

\((1 - f_0)\, S_u(t)\)

Failed by \(t\) (left censored, or an interval from 0 to \(t\))

\(f_0 + (1 - f_0)\, F_u(t)\)

Failed between \(l > 0\) and \(r\)

\((1 - f_0)\, (F_u(r) - F_u(l))\)

Entered observation at age \(a > 0\) (left truncated)

divided by \((1 - f_0)\, S_u(a)\)

Counts n and weights w multiply each row’s term. The covariance is the inverse of the observed information (the robust one for a weighted fit), and the fit reports whether it reached a verified maximum (model.maximum). With no covariates on either part the fit is the distribution’s own zero-inflated fit, latency.fit(x, c, zi=True), to the last digit of the likelihood.

An interval starting at 0 includes the units dead on arrival, because an inspection that finds a unit failed cannot tell when, and the unit may never have worked; the univariate fits (fit(zi=True)) read it the same way. Record a failure known to be after 0 with a lower bound above 0. This is the likelihood of Moulton and Halsey (1995) and of Braekers and Grouwels (2016), whose rows “failed by \(t\)” include the mass at 0.

Refusals. If no unit failed at 0 and every row says its unit worked, f0 is estimated as 0 and the model is the latency’s regression alone: the fit refuses and says to fit that instead. If every unit that could have been dead on arrival was, there is no life left to fit, and the fit refuses too. Where the joint route’s f0 runs towards 0, the fit warns “No finite maximum”, naming f0.

What is not built

  • A semi-parametric (Cox) latency: the latency here is parametric.

  • Zero inflation and a cure fraction together (zi=True with lfp=True).

  • Latencies on the whole line (normal, Gumbel, logistic): a time to failure is positive.

  • Covariate paths (fit_tvc), as for every distributional regression.
