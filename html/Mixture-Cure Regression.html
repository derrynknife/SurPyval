Mixture-Cure Regression

Most survival models assume that every unit fails eventually. Often that is not true:

  • only the parts with a manufacturing defect fail of it; the sound ones never do, however long they run;

  • some patients are cured, and never have the relapse the study follows;

  • some customers never cancel, some borrowers never default.

The mark of such data is a survival curve that levels off above zero: after a while the failures stop, with many units still under observation.

A mixture-cure model treats the population as two groups. A unit is susceptible (it will fail) with some probability, and a susceptible unit has a time to failure with an ordinary lifetime distribution. A mixture-cure regression lets covariates act on both parts, and so answers two different questions at once:

  • Who fails? Which lots, sites or patients have a higher chance of ever failing. This is the incidence.

  • When? Among those that do fail, what makes failure come sooner. This is the latency.

A covariate can matter for one and not the other. A bad production lot may contain more defective parts without the defective parts failing any faster; a treatment may cure more patients without delaying relapse in those it does not cure. An ordinary regression, with one effect per covariate, cannot tell these apart.

Without covariates this is the limited-failure-population model that the distributions already fit (Weibull.fit(x, c, lfp=True), see Parametric SurPyval Modelling). MixtureCure is that model with covariates. The API reference is Mixture-Cure Regression.

A warranty example: a defect that depends on the lot

A controller fails early when a solder joint is defective. Two things were recorded for each of 1,500 units: whether it came from a production lot made after a process change (late_lot), and the thermal load at its site (load, standardised). Units have been in service for between 300 and 500 days. The data are simulated, so the truth is known: the defect is more common in the late lots and at high load, and the late lots’ defective units also fail sooner.

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import surpyval as sp

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
n = 1500
late_lot = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)
load = rng.normal(size=n)
p_defect = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(-0.5 + 1.0 * late_lot + 0.5 * load)))
defective = rng.random(n) < p_defect
life = 100 * np.exp(-0.4 * late_lot) * rng.weibull(2.0, n)
in_service = rng.uniform(300, 500, n)
failed = defective & (life < in_service)
df = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "days": np.where(failed, life, in_service),
        "c": np.where(failed, 0, 1),
        "late_lot": late_lot,
        "load": load,
    }
)

The Kaplan-Meier curve shows the plateau: failures stop after about 250 days with roughly half the units still running.

km = sp.KaplanMeier.fit(df["days"], c=df["c"])
ax = km.plot()

Fit the model. WeibullMixtureCure is MixtureCure(Weibull): a Weibull life for the defective units and a logistic regression for the chance of being defective.

model = sp.WeibullMixtureCure.fit_from_df(
    df, x_col="days", c_col="c", Z_cols=["late_lot", "load"]
)
model

How to read it:

  • alpha and beta are the Weibull scale and shape of a defective unit’s life at the reference row (an early lot at average load), and lfp_p is the probability there of being defective: 0.39. It is the same name as the univariate fit’s limited-failure proportion.

  • lfp_p_late_lot and lfp_p_load are the incidence’s coefficients, on the logit scale, so exp of one is an odds ratio. A late lot has exp(1.1) = 2.9 times the odds of containing the defect.

  • alpha_late_lot and alpha_load are the latency’s coefficients, with the sign of SurPyval’s AFT models: positive means shorter. A late lot’s defective units have exp(-0.42) = 0.65 times the life of an early lot’s. Load shows no effect on when a defective unit fails (alpha_load is near 0, as simulated): it only makes the defect more likely.

Every coefficient has the same sign convention: positive is worse, a higher chance of failing or an earlier failure.

The two parts for a given unit:

rows = pd.DataFrame({"late_lot": [0, 1], "load": [0.0, 0.0]})
print("defective :", model.susceptible_probability(rows).round(3))
print("sound     :", model.cure_probability(rows).round(3))
print(
    "median life of a defective unit:",
    model.qf(0.5, rows, susceptible=True).round(1),
)

sf, ff, hf and the rest are the population’s: the survival of a unit whose status is not known, which levels off at the cure probability. With susceptible=True they are the latency’s, the functions of a unit that does fail.

Is a unit that is still running sound?

The field question is about the units still in service. A new unit from a late lot is sound with probability cure_probability. One that has already run 150 days without failing is much more likely to be sound, because most of the defective ones would have failed by then:

ages = [0, 50, 100, 150, 250]
model.cure_given_survival(ages, late).round(3)

array([0.354, 0.489, 0.825, 0.985, 1.   ])

This is the number to plan a recall or a spares buy with: of the late-lot units still running at a given age, the share that will never fail.

Choosing what acts on what

By default every covariate acts on the incidence and on the latency’s life parameter (the scale of a Weibull or log-logistic, mu of a log-normal, the rate of a gamma or exponential). covariates chooses, by parameter; with a data frame, formulas gives each part its own formula:

chosen = sp.WeibullMixtureCure.fit_from_df(
    df,
    x_col="days",
    c_col="c",
    formulas={"lfp_p": "late_lot + load", "alpha": "late_lot"},
)
chosen.parameter_names

['alpha', 'beta', 'lfp_p', 'alpha_late_lot', 'lfp_p_late_lot', 'lfp_p_load']

print("AIC, load on both parts  :", round(model.aic(), 1))
print("AIC, load on incidence   :", round(chosen.aic(), 1))

AIC, load on both parts  : 9624.5
AIC, load on incidence   : 9622.6

Dropping the load from the latency lowers the AIC: the data agree that it does not change when a defective unit fails. A covariate on the shape is possible too (covariates={"beta": [...]}), as in a distributional regression, of which this model is a member: the fits, checks, bounds (cb, param_cb and parameter_cb with Wald, likelihood-ratio and bootstrap methods), weights, to_dict and from_dict are that family’s.

One bound behaves differently. A quantile, the age by which a fraction p of the units have failed, is infinite when p is at or above the chance of ever failing. So the likelihood-ratio bound on a quantile (quantile_cb(p, Z, method="lr")) has an infinite upper end whenever the data cannot rule out that so few units ever fail: whenever the likelihood-ratio lower bound on lfp_p at that row is at or below p. It is an honest answer, saying the data do not bound that quantile from above.

A medical example: who relapses, and when

In a trial, some patients given a treatment never relapse. Two questions are asked of the treatment: does it cure more patients, and does it delay relapse in those it does not cure? The data here are simulated with a treatment that does the first and not the second, and a log-normal time to relapse (in months), followed for five to eight years.

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
n = 800
treated = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)
relapses = rng.random(n) < np.where(treated == 1, 0.45, 0.70)
months = np.exp(rng.normal(2.3, 0.7, n))
follow = rng.uniform(60, 96, n)
seen = relapses & (months < follow)
trial = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "months": np.where(seen, months, follow),
        "c": np.where(seen, 0, 1),
        "treated": treated,
    }
)
cure = sp.LogNormalMixtureCure.fit_from_df(
    trial, x_col="months", c_col="c", Z_cols=["treated"]
)
cure.summary().round(3)

About 30% of the untreated and over half of the treated are cured, an odds ratio of relapse well below 1, while the coefficient on the time to relapse (mu_treated) is not distinguishable from 0. A Cox or AFT model of the same data would report one diluted “treatment effect” and break its own assumptions doing it (the hazards of the two arms are not proportional when one arm has more cured patients).

Was follow-up long enough?

A cure fraction can only be seen if the units were followed past the point where the susceptible ones have failed. If follow-up stops while failures are still arriving, the data cannot tell “30% never fail” from “30% have not failed yet”, and the fitted cure probability is a guess that depends on the latency distribution chosen. No model can fix that.

Two checks:

  • Look at the Kaplan-Meier curve. It should be flat at the end, with a good number of units still at risk on the flat part.

  • model.follow_up() is Maller and Zhou’s check. It compares the length of the plateau (from the last failure to the end of follow-up) with how the failures were spaced before it, and returns a p-value: small means the failures had died away before follow-up ended.

model.follow_up()

{'last_failure': 257.2522708859546,

 'n': 1500.0,

When the data have no plateau at all, the likelihood has no maximum inside the model: the susceptible probability runs to 1. The fit then warns “No finite maximum”, naming lfp_p, and says to fit the latency alone; model.maximum is "no finite maximum". Take the warning at its word.

The model

A unit with covariates \(z\) is susceptible with probability \(\pi(z)\), and a susceptible unit’s life has survival function \(S_u(t \mid z)\). The population’s survival is

\[S(t \mid z) = 1 - \pi(z) + \pi(z)\, S_u(t \mid z),\]

which falls to the cure probability \(1 - \pi(z)\), not to 0.

Incidence. \(g(\pi(z)) = g(\pi) + x_\pi(z)' b_\pi\), with \(g\) the logit (the default; logistic regression), the probit or the complementary log-log link, chosen by MixtureCure(latency, incidence=...). lfp_p is \(\pi\) at the reference row.

Latency. A Weibull, log-normal, log-logistic, gamma or exponential distribution whose parameters depend on the covariates through their own links, as in a distributional regression: a scale as \(\alpha(z) = \alpha\, e^{-x' b}\) (the AFT model), a location as \(\mu(z) = \mu - x' b\), a rate as \(\lambda(z) = \lambda\, e^{x' b}\). For a Weibull latency, a coefficient \(b\) on the scale is a log hazard ratio of \(\beta\, b\) among the susceptible, \(\beta\) the shape.

Likelihood. Both parts are fitted together by maximum likelihood. Each kind of observation contributes what the population model says of it:

Observation

Contribution

Failed at \(t\)

\(\pi\, f_u(t)\)

Still running at \(t\) (right censored)

\(1 - \pi + \pi\, S_u(t)\)

Failed before \(t\) (left censored)

\(\pi\, F_u(t)\)

Failed between \(l\) and \(r\) (interval censored)

\(\pi\, (F_u(r) - F_u(l))\)

Entered observation at age \(a\) (left truncated)

divided by \(1 - \pi + \pi\, S_u(a)\)

A unit still running is either cured or a susceptible unit that has not failed yet; the likelihood adds the two. Counts n and weights w multiply each row’s term. The covariance is the inverse of the observed information (the robust one for a weighted fit), and the fit reports whether it reached a verified maximum (model.maximum).

With no covariates on either part the fit is the distribution’s own limited-failure fit, latency.fit(x, c, lfp=True), to the last digit of the likelihood. The fits are checked against R’s flexsurvcure.

The chance that a survivor is cured follows from Bayes’ rule:

\[P(\text{cured} \mid T > t, z) = \frac{1 - \pi(z)}{1 - \pi(z) + \pi(z)\, S_u(t \mid z)} .\]

What is not built

  • A semi-parametric (Cox) latency, as in R’s smcure: the latency here is parametric. A flexible parametric choice (compare the latencies by AIC) covers most uses.

  • A dead-on-arrival fraction (zero inflation) in the same model: that is its own model, Zero-Inflated Regression.

  • The incidence estimated by a general classifier (a gradient-boosted or random-forest model in place of the link).

  • Latencies on the whole line (normal, Gumbel, logistic): a time to failure is positive.

  • Covariate paths (fit_tvc), as for every distributional regression.
