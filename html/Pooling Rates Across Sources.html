Pooling Rates Across Sources

Often the evidence about how often something happens comes from several places, each with its own count: infections at several hospitals with the patient-days at each, failures of one kind of pump at several plants with their running hours, adverse events in several trials with the doses given, failures to start out of the starts attempted at several sites. surv.pooled_rate combines them.

In plain words

The sources really differ. A hospital in one city and a hospital in another do not share one true infection rate, and neither do two plants running the same pump in different service. Adding every count and every exposure into one pot assumes they do. When they don’t, the one-pot answer is dominated by the biggest source and reports a precision nobody has.

So ask a different question. Not “what is the one rate?”, but “across sources like these, how are the rates spread?” The sources are treated as a sample from a population of sources, each with its own rate. The method estimates that population from the counts, and every source still counts for as much as its exposure says: a source with ten events in a million hours speaks louder than one with one event in a hundred thousand, and a source with no events in a long exposure is real evidence of a low rate.

It gives two different ranges, and says which is which.

  • The average across sources: how well the middle of the spread is known. This narrows as sources are added.

  • A new source like these: where the rate of one more source, about which nothing else is known, probably lies. This is the population’s own spread (plus the uncertainty in it), so it stays wide however many sources there are if they really differ. It is the one to use as a starting belief for a new site, hospital or study.

Each source gets two estimates. Its own (its events over its exposure) and a shrunk one, pulled towards the others: a source with little exposure is pulled a long way, one with a lot hardly at all. The shrunk estimates are usually better guesses of each source’s true rate than the raw ones, especially for small sources.

Do the sources differ at all? A heterogeneity test (Pearson’s chi-square that every source has one rate) and \(I^2\), the share of the variation that is more than chance, say how much the sources disagree.

At least four sources. With two or three sources, how different a fourth would be cannot be estimated: the range for a new source comes out either absurdly wide or wrongly narrow. Below min_sources (default 4) the function gives the sources side by side, each with its own interval, the one-pot estimate and the heterogeneity test, and no population, with a warning saying so. With one source it gives that source’s own (Jeffreys) interval.

Rates and probabilities

kind="rate" (the default) is for events in a time at risk: patient-days, operating hours, person-years, kilometres. kind="probability" is for events out of a number of trials or demands: failures to start out of starts, adverse events out of doses, defects out of items inspected. The two are never converted into each other.

An example

Six sources of failures to start, out of the starts (demands) attempted. The first is a large modern data set; the others are older and smaller. These inputs are illustrative.

import surpyval as surv

failures = [227, 137, 25, 3, 42, 6]
demands = [410593, 49000, 2100, 260, 3700, 2400]
pooled = surv.pooled_rate(
    failures, demands, kind="probability", alpha_ci=0.1,
    labels=["A", "B", "C", "D", "E", "F"],
)
print(pooled)

Pooled probability from 6 sources (Bayes, 90% intervals)
  One pot (assumes one probability for all): 0.0009401 (0.0008686 to 0.001016)
  Average across sources: 0.006681 (0.003372 to 0.01667)
  A new source like these: median 0.00458, mean 0.00809 (0.0001693 to 0.026)
  Heterogeneity: chi-square 978.4 on 5 df, p = 2.84e-209, I2 = 99%

Read the three answers in turn:

  • One pot: about 0.00094 per demand, with a range of 1.2 times from end to end. It is dominated by source A’s huge exposure, and no other source’s own range comes near it.

  • The average across sources: about 0.0067, known to within about 5 times.

  • A new source like these: median about 0.0046, but anywhere from about 0.00017 to 0.026, a range of about 150 times. That is the method saying, correctly, that these sources disagree by a lot. A range this wide is a cue to split the sources into groups that are more alike (here, the modern source and the older ones) and pool each group.

Updating with a new source’s own data

The pooled population is a starting belief for a new source; its own counts then update it (the “second stage”). update gives the new source’s estimate from its own events and exposure:

for own_failures in (0, 1, 4):
    print(own_failures, "in 400:", pooled.update(own_failures, 400))

0 in 400: {'mean': 0.0016261703880023775, 'median': 0.0010492503746074083, 'lower': 1.5002928594896567e-05, 'upper': 0.005205415113740026}

1 in 400: {'mean': 0.0035975324019048207, 'median': 0.003037873826112358, 'lower': 0.0006081915898039653, 'upper': 0.008498203498305837}
4 in 400: {'mean': 0.009076172831618951, 'median': 0.008420728855243458, 'lower': 0.0035633470197417704, 'upper': 0.01682536738966712}

With a wide population the new source’s own data quickly take over; with a narrow one they barely move it. Compare the update with the source’s own data alone before relying on it: a narrow population from the wrong kind of source can hold the answer far from what the new source’s data say.

A rate example

Infections and patient-days at eight hospitals (illustrative), one with no infections:

infections = [12, 4, 0, 9, 21, 3, 7, 15]
patient_days = [8000, 5200, 1900, 4100, 9800, 3500, 6100, 7300]
hosp = surv.pooled_rate(infections, patient_days, alpha_ci=0.1)
print(hosp)
print(hosp.sources[["own", "shrunk", "shrunk_lower", "shrunk_upper"]].round(5))

Bayes or maximum likelihood

method="Bayes" (the default) puts a weak prior on the population’s two parameters and averages every interval over their posterior, computed on a grid (exact up to the grid, and deterministic). method="MLE" fits them by marginal maximum likelihood (the “empirical Bayes” estimate), with a profile-likelihood interval for the average and a parametric bootstrap for the others.

With few sources the difference matters. The MLE treats the population’s spread as known once estimated, and with four to ten sources that spread is itself very uncertain. In a simulation with six sources, the nominal 90% range for a new source covered the new source’s rate 88% of the time under "Bayes" and 72% under "MLE"; the average’s interval covered the true mean 91% and 85% of the time. Use "MLE" for many sources, or to compare with an empirical-Bayes analysis.

The maths

Rates. Source \(i\) has \(x_i\) events in exposure \(T_i\) with its own rate \(\lambda_i\):

\[x_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_i T_i), \qquad \lambda_i \sim \text{Gamma}(a, b),\]

shape \(a\), rate \(b\), mean \(a / b\). With \(\lambda_i\) integrated out each \(x_i\) is negative binomial,

\[P(x_i \mid a, b) = \frac{\Gamma(x_i + a)}{\Gamma(a)\, x_i!} \left(\frac{b}{b + T_i}\right)^{a} \left(\frac{T_i}{b + T_i}\right)^{x_i},\]

and the likelihood of \((a, b)\) is the product over the sources.

Probabilities. \(x_i \sim \text{Binomial}(n_i, p_i)\) and \(p_i \sim \text{Beta}(a, b)\), so \(x_i\) is beta-binomial, \(P(x_i \mid a, b) = \binom{n_i}{x_i} B(x_i + a, n_i - x_i + b) / B(a, b)\).

Estimating the population. Under "Bayes" the prior is flat on the log of the mean rate \(a / b\) (the logit of the mean probability) and \(p(a) \propto (1 + a)^{-2}\) on the shape, a proper, weak prior that does not depend on the unit of time. The posterior of the mean and the shape is computed on a grid of 161 by 161 points, refined once around where it has weight. Under "MLE" the same likelihood is maximised; the likelihood can be largest with no spread at all (\(a \to \infty\), every source one rate), and the MLE then says so (an infinite shape).

The results.

  • The population mean’s interval: the quantiles of its posterior ("Bayes"); the profile-likelihood interval ("MLE").

  • A new source: its rate is a draw from the population, \(\text{Gamma}(a, b)\), averaged over the posterior of \((a, b)\) ("Bayes") or over the bootstrap refits ("MLE"). This is the predictive distribution; the population “averaged over the uncertainty” is not exactly a gamma, so population is the gamma (or beta) with the same median and upper quantile, which keeps the upper side honest.

  • A source’s shrunk estimate: given the population, its rate has the conjugate posterior \(\text{Gamma}(a + x_i, b + T_i)\) (\(\text{Beta}(a + x_i, b + n_i - x_i)\)), again averaged over \((a, b)\).

  • A source’s own interval: Jeffreys, \(\text{Gamma}(x_i + 1/2, T_i)\) or \(\text{Beta}(x_i + 1/2, n_i - x_i + 1/2)\), which is defined with no events.

  • Heterogeneity: Pearson’s \(Q = \sum_i (x_i - T_i \hat r)^2 / (T_i \hat r)\) with \(\hat r = \sum x_i / \sum T_i\), on \(k - 1\) degrees of freedom (the 2 by \(k\) table’s chi-square for probabilities), and \(I^2 = \max(0, (Q - (k - 1)) / Q)\).

This is the “two-stage” or hierarchical Bayes treatment of generic data in probabilistic risk assessment (NUREG/CR-6823, chapter 8), and the empirical-Bayes analysis of event rates of Gaver and O’Muircheartaigh (1987). Averaging over the posterior of \((a, b)\) does directly what the Kass-Steffey correction to the empirical-Bayes intervals approximates.

Checks. The tests reproduce the BUGS “Pumps” example (ten pumps’ failures and operating times, with that example’s own prior), whose published posterior means of \(a\) and \(b\) are about 0.70 and 0.93, and its per-pump posterior means to 0.2% of an independent numerical integration; the MLE agrees with SciPy’s negative binomial and beta-binomial likelihoods maximised directly; and a simulation study checks the coverage of the intervals.
