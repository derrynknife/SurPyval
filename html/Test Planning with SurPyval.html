Test Planning with SurPyval

Before a reliability test is run, four questions decide what it costs and what it can show:

  • Will it demonstrate the target? A demonstration test shows, at a confidence level, that a design meets a reliability or MTBF target.

  • Can it decide sooner? A sequential test stops as soon as the evidence is clear.

  • How precise will the estimate be? A life test run to estimate a B-life or a mean needs enough units and time for the precision wanted; an accelerated test also needs the right stresses.

  • Will a comparison detect the difference? Comparing a new design with an old one needs enough events to detect the improvement.

surpyval.test_planning answers each from planning values: the target, and assumed values of what the test will measure. Every function here plans a test; the fitters elsewhere in SurPyval analyse it once it is run. As everywhere in SurPyval, a confidence level is given as alpha_ci, the significance level: for a demonstration test it is the consumer’s risk, the chance that a design no better than the target passes, 0.05 for 95% confidence. The other party’s risk, the supplier’s or producer’s (that a good design fails), is producer_risk; the section on procurement below works through both.

Demonstration tests

A pass/fail (attribute) test runs n units for a mission each. With no failures allowed, 59 units demonstrate 95% reliability at 95% confidence, the success run of surpyval.success_run(); allowing one failure takes 93:

from surpyval.test_planning import (
    demonstrated_reliability,
    demonstration_pass_probability,
    demonstration_plan,
    demonstration_sample_size,
)

print(demonstration_sample_size(0.95), demonstration_sample_size(0.95, 1))
print(round(demonstrated_reliability(59), 4))

59 93
0.9505

A test has a second risk, the producer’s: that a good design fails. The 22-unit zero-failure test that demonstrates 90% reliability at 90% confidence passes a design of 95% reliability only about a third of the time. demonstration_plan() finds the smallest test that keeps both risks: here 128 units, allowing 8 failures.

print(round(demonstration_pass_probability(0.95, 22), 2))
plan = demonstration_plan(0.9, 0.95, alpha_ci=0.1, producer_risk=0.2)

When the lifetime’s Weibull shape is known, testing each unit for longer than a mission trades units for test time (the extended success run, or Weibayes): for a wear-out shape of 2, two missions per unit need a quarter of the units, 15 instead of 59.

demonstration_sample_size(0.95, test_multiple=2.0, shape=2.0)

15

With the units fixed, demonstration_test_time() gives how long each must run, in hours rather than missions: ten units, a believed shape of 2, to show 90% reliability over 1000 hours at 90% confidence, each run about 1478 hours with no failure (the Weibayes closed form, \(t_m (\ln \alpha / (n \ln R))^{1/\beta}\)). Given n and shape, demonstration_plan() searches the test length that keeps both risks, and mission_time reports it in hours: ten units are too few to keep a 20% producer’s risk at any length, which the refusal says with the units that are enough; twenty run about 2820 hours each, allowing 8 failures.

Sequential tests

A sequential test watches the failures as they come: it accepts the design once enough test time has passed for the failures seen, rejects it if they come too soon, and otherwise keeps testing. With the same risks it usually stops well before the fixed-length test. Truncated at the fixed-length test, as MIL-HDBK-781 requires, it never runs longer.

To show an MTBF of 100 hours, a 200-hour design to pass, both risks 10%, the fixed-length test runs 2,013 hours; the sequential test about 1,080 on average for the good design and 910 for one at the target:

from surpyval.test_planning import sequential_mtbf_test

test = sequential_mtbf_test(100.0, 200.0, alpha_ci=0.1, producer_risk=0.1)
print(round(test.expected_test_time(200.0)), round(test.expected_test_time(100.0)))
test

The test’s decide method says what to do at any point, and its risks are computed exactly: Wald’s boundaries and the truncation move them a little from the 10% asked for, here to about 13% and 10%. The pass/fail analogue is sequential_demonstration_test().

Supplier’s and consumer’s risk in procurement

A reliability requirement in a contract is usually checked by an acceptance test, and every acceptance test carries two risks, one for each party:

  • the consumer’s risk (the buyer’s): that a design no better than the requirement passes the test and is accepted;

  • the supplier’s risk, or producer’s risk: that a design which is good fails the test and is rejected.

A contract that states only a reliability “at 90% confidence” fixes the first and leaves the second to chance. The examples below show how large it can be, how to agree a test that bounds both, and how to check a test clause that is already written.

Notation. MIL-HDBK-781 and most procurement standards call the producer’s (supplier’s) risk \(\alpha\) and the consumer’s risk \(\beta\). SurPyval writes every confidence level as alpha_ci, and for a demonstration test that is the consumer’s risk; the supplier’s is producer_risk. So a handbook plan with \(\alpha = 10\%\) and \(\beta = 20\%\) is producer_risk=0.1, alpha_ci=0.2.

The supplier’s risk hidden in a zero-failure test

The familiar 59-unit, zero-failure test demonstrates 95% reliability at 95% confidence: a design at exactly 95% passes it only 5% of the time. But the supplier’s side is far worse than most contracts realise. A design of 99% reliability, five times fewer failures than the requirement allows, fails the test 45% of the time; to pass it nine times in ten, a design needs about 99.8%. The test’s operating characteristic, its chance of passing against the design’s true reliability, shows both risks at once:

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

reliability = np.linspace(0.9, 0.999, 200)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(reliability, demonstration_pass_probability(reliability, 59),
        label="59 units, no failures allowed")
ax.plot(reliability, demonstration_pass_probability(reliability, 128, 8),
        label="128 units, 8 failures allowed")
ax.axvline(0.95, color="grey", lw=0.8, ls="--")
ax.set_xlabel("True reliability of the design")
ax.set_ylabel("Chance of passing the test")
ax.legend()
plt.show()

for r in (0.97, 0.98, 0.99, 0.995):
    print(f"{r}: supplier's risk {1 - demonstration_pass_probability(r, 59):.3f}")

0.97: supplier's risk 0.834
0.98: supplier's risk 0.696
0.99: supplier's risk 0.447
0.995: supplier's risk 0.256

The steeper the curve, the better the test separates good designs from bad ones; allowing a few failures with more units is what makes it steep.

Agreeing a plan that bounds both risks

A fair acceptance test is agreed from four numbers, two from each party: the buyer’s requirement and consumer’s risk, and the supplier’s expected (“good”) reliability and supplier’s risk. demonstration_plan() gives the smallest test that keeps both. The closer the good reliability is to the requirement, the larger the test: for a requirement of 95% and both risks 10%, a supplier confident of 99% needs 105 units allowing 2 failures, but one expecting only 97% needs 628 units allowing 24.

for good in (0.99, 0.98, 0.97):
    plan = demonstration_plan(0.95, good, alpha_ci=0.1, producer_risk=0.1)
    print(f"good design {good}: {plan.n} units, "
          f"accept with up to {plan.failures} failures "
          f"(consumer's risk {plan.consumer_risk:.3f}, "
          f"supplier's risk {plan.producer_risk:.3f})")

good design 0.99: 105 units, accept with up to 2 failures (consumer's risk 0.099, supplier's risk 0.089)
good design 0.98: 258 units, accept with up to 8 failures (consumer's risk 0.099, supplier's risk 0.077)
good design 0.97: 628 units, accept with up to 24 failures (consumer's risk 0.100, supplier's risk 0.096)

An MTBF acceptance test (MIL-HDBK-781)

For equipment with a constant failure rate the requirement is an MTBF, and the test runs a total number of unit-hours, failed units repaired. The handbook calls the requirement the lower test MTBF (\(\theta_1\)), the supplier’s design goal the upper test MTBF (\(\theta_0\)), and their ratio the discrimination ratio \(d = \theta_0 / \theta_1\). For a requirement of 500 hours, the test shrinks quickly as the ratio grows or the risks are relaxed: with both risks 20% and a ratio of 3 it is about 2,140 unit hours, accepting with up to 2 failures (the handbook’s plan XVII-D, 4.3 times the requirement).

for risk in (0.1, 0.2):
    for ratio in (1.5, 2.0, 3.0):
        plan = mtbf_demonstration_plan(
            500.0, 500.0 * ratio, alpha_ci=risk, producer_risk=risk)
        print(f"risks {risk:.0%}, d = {ratio}: {plan.test_time:,.0f} unit hours, "
              f"accept with up to {plan.failures} failures")

risks 10%, d = 1.5: 24,695 unit hours, accept with up to 40 failures
risks 10%, d = 2.0: 10,064 unit hours, accept with up to 14 failures
risks 10%, d = 3.0: 4,637 unit hours, accept with up to 5 failures
risks 20%, d = 1.5: 10,720 unit hours, accept with up to 17 failures
risks 20%, d = 2.0: 4,538 unit hours, accept with up to 6 failures
risks 20%, d = 3.0: 2,140 unit hours, accept with up to 2 failures

A sequential test with the same risks (sequential_mtbf_test()) usually finishes much sooner: for the requirement of 500 hours, a goal of 1000 and both risks 10%, about 5,400 unit hours on average for a design at the goal, against 10,064 for the fixed-length test. Its exact risks as run are a little different from the 10% asked for (about 13% consumer’s and 10% supplier’s), because of the truncation; a contract that must not exceed a stated consumer’s risk should say which kind of test is meant.

test = sequential_mtbf_test(500.0, 1000.0, alpha_ci=0.1, producer_risk=0.1)
print(round(test.expected_test_time(1000.0)), round(test.max_time))
print(round(test.consumer_risk, 3), round(test.producer_risk, 3))

5409 10064
0.128 0.103

Checking a test clause that is already written

Contracts often state a test without its risks: “the equipment shall be tested for 3,000 hours and accepted with no more than 2 failures; required MTBF 500 hours.” mtbf_pass_probability() gives both risks. Equipment at the required MTBF passes 6% of the time, so the buyer is well protected; but equipment twice as good, at 1000 hours, fails 58% of the time. The clause puts almost all of the risk on the supplier, who would want either a longer test allowing more failures, or a design goal well above 1000 hours.

from surpyval.test_planning import mtbf_pass_probability

for mtbf in (500.0, 750.0, 1000.0, 1500.0):
    print(f"true MTBF {mtbf:,.0f} h: passes {mtbf_pass_probability(mtbf, 3000.0, 2):.1%}")

true MTBF 500 h: passes 6.2%
true MTBF 750 h: passes 23.8%
true MTBF 1,000 h: passes 42.3%
true MTBF 1,500 h: passes 67.7%

Planning a life test for precision

A life test run to estimate a B-life or a mean needs enough failures for the precision wanted. The precision is the factor R of the confidence interval [estimate / R, estimate * R]; “within 20%” is R = 1.2. It depends on planning values of the distribution, above all its shape, which earlier tests or engineering knowledge give.

Thirty units of a wear-out part (Weibull, scale 1000 hours, shape 2) on a 500-hour test, about 7 failures, estimate its B10 life of 325 hours to within a factor of 1.62 at 95% confidence:

import surpyval
from surpyval.test_planning import (
    life_test_length,
    life_test_precision,
    life_test_sample_size,
)

plan = life_test_precision(surpyval.Weibull, (1000.0, 2.0), 30,
                           censor_time=500.0)
plan

Life Test Plan

Expected failures: 6.63598
Estimating       : B10 = 324.593

The same functions answer the question the other way round: how many units, or how long a test, for a precision. To estimate the B10 life within 30% at 90% confidence takes 72 units on a 600-hour test; forty units need a test of about 330 hours to estimate it within a factor of 1.5.

print(life_test_sample_size(surpyval.Weibull, (1000.0, 2.0), 1.3,
                            censor_time=600.0, alpha_ci=0.1).n)
print(round(life_test_length(surpyval.Weibull, (1000.0, 2.0), 40, 1.5,
                             alpha_ci=0.1).censor_time))

72
329

The precision is a large-sample approximation, from the test’s expected Fisher information. simulate checks it: it simulates the test, fits the distribution to each simulated data set, and reports the precision the estimates actually had, and how often they fell within the planned factor.

plan.simulate(200, random_state=1)

Accelerated life tests

An accelerated test runs units at raised stress to get failures in time, and estimates the life at the use stress through a life-stress model. Where to put the stresses matters as much as how many units: the units at the lowest stress, closest to use, carry the extrapolation, but fail least. accelerated_test_plan() chooses the lowest stress and the share of units at it, with the highest stress fixed.

For a voltage endurance test (power law, life \(10^6 V^{-2}\) hours, Weibull shape 2), 40 units for 1000 hours, the highest stress 60 V, to estimate the B10 life at 10 V: the optimum plan tests about 78% of the units near 25 V and the rest at 60 V. A compromise plan of three equally spaced levels (Meeker and Hahn) puts 20% of the units in the middle, giving up a little precision for protection against a life-stress relation that bends:

from surpyval import life_models
from surpyval.test_planning import accelerated_test_plan

voltage = dict(use_stress=10.0, high_stress=60.0, n=40, censor_time=1000.0)
optimum = accelerated_test_plan(
    surpyval.Weibull, life_models.Power, {"a": 1e6, "n": -2.0}, 2.0,
    **voltage)
compromise = accelerated_test_plan(
    surpyval.Weibull, life_models.Power, {"a": 1e6, "n": -2.0}, 2.0,
    levels=3, **voltage)
print(optimum)
print(compromise)

Each plan reports what the acceleration buys: the acceleration factor at each stress (an hour at 60 V ages a unit as much as 36 hours at 10 V), and how long the same 40 units would have to run at the use stress to estimate the B10 life as precisely, about 2480 hours against the 1000 of the accelerated test.

accelerated_test_precision() gives the precision of a test you propose, and the plans’ simulate method checks the approximation by fitting surpyval.AcceleratedLife to simulated tests.

Comparing two groups

To show that a new design fails less often than the old, a log-rank test needs enough events: detecting a halving of the hazard with 80% power at the 5% level takes 66, with equal groups (Schoenfeld’s formula). The units follow from the chance each has of failing during the study: if 25% of the new units and 35% of the old would fail, detecting a 30% lower hazard takes 412 units in each group.

from surpyval.test_planning import logrank_design

print(logrank_design(0.5, power=0.8).events)
print(logrank_design(0.7, power=0.8, event_probability=(0.25, 0.35)).n)
plan = logrank_design(0.7, n=(200, 200), event_probability=(0.25, 0.35))
print(plan.solved_for, round(plan.power, 2))

66
(412, 412)
power 0.5

Freedman’s formula, as lifelines’ sample_size_necessary_under_cph uses, is the option method="freedman".

A difference stated in mean lives becomes a hazard ratio only through the shape. For exponential lives, a mean life 30% longer is a hazard ratio of 1 / 1.3; for Weibull lives with a common shape \(\beta\), it is 1.3 ** -beta. Where the shape is known, mean_ratio_design() plans the exact F test of the two means themselves (Epstein and Sobel; Lawless, section 3.5): 458 failures for exponential lives, against the log-rank test’s 457, and 116 for a shape of 2.

from surpyval.test_planning import mean_ratio_design

print(mean_ratio_design(1.3, power=0.8).events,
      logrank_design(1 / 1.3, power=0.8).events)
print(mean_ratio_design(1.3, shape=2.0, power=0.8).events)

458 457
116

Every design below works the same way: give all of its quantities (the power, the size and the effect) but one, and the one left None is solved for. The answer, a PowerCalculation, says what was solved for (solved_for) and holds it (value) beside the power, the events and the units. Giving none, or more than one, is refused, naming them.

To show that a change to a repairable system lowered its rate of failures, the comparison is of the failures before and after the change, over the operating time of each: rate_ratio_design() gives the power, the smallest change detectable or the operating time needed, whichever is left None, for the test of surpyval.recurrent.intervention_test(). They are worked through in Intervention Analysis with SurPyval.

When the plain log-rank calculation is wrong

Schoenfeld’s events answer one question: a test that the hazard ratio of two groups is 1, when it is the same at every age. Four common plans ask something else.

A covariate that is measured, or correlated with others. To detect the effect of a measurement (a temperature, a dose, a load) in a Cox model, the information is the covariate’s variance, and a covariate partly explained by the others in the model carries less: its coefficient’s variance is inflated by \(1 / (1 - R^2)\), \(R^2\) its squared multiple correlation with them (Hsieh and Lavori 2000). A hazard ratio of 1.5 per standard deviation takes 48 events alone, 69 if the other covariates explain 30% of its variance. For a covariate of two levels give proportion instead of sd; with r_squared=0 that is Schoenfeld’s formula.

from surpyval.test_planning import cox_design

alone = cox_design(1.5, power=0.8, sd=1.0)
adjusted = cox_design(1.5, power=0.8, sd=1.0, r_squared=0.3)
print(alone.events, adjusted.events)
print(cox_design(1.5, power=0.8, event_probability=0.4, sd=1.0, r_squared=0.3).n)
smallest = cox_design(power=0.8, n=200, event_probability=0.4, sd=1.0,
               r_squared=0.3)
print(smallest.solved_for, round(smallest.value, 2))

48 69
171
hazard_ratio 1.45

Showing that a change is no worse, or as good. A cheaper part, a shorter procedure: the aim is to show the new group’s hazard is at most a margin above the old’s (non-inferiority), or within margins on both sides (equivalence), not that it differs. The null hypothesis is then the margin, not a ratio of 1: non-inferiority is shown when the upper limit of the 1 - alpha_ci confidence interval of the hazard ratio is below the margin, and equivalence when the whole interval is inside the margins (two one-sided tests; the classical 5% tests compare a 90% interval, alpha_ci=0.1). Showing a raise of at most 30% when the two are in fact equally good takes 457 events, which is Schoenfeld’s count for a hazard ratio of 1.3 at one side of a 95% interval.

from surpyval.test_planning import equivalence_design, noninferiority_design

print(noninferiority_design(1.3, power=0.8).events)
print(noninferiority_design(1.3, power=0.8, event_probability=0.4).n)
print(equivalence_design(1.25, power=0.8, alpha_ci=0.1).events)

457
(571, 571)
688

A difference in restricted mean survival time. When the hazards are not expected to be proportional (an effect that starts late, curves that cross), the hazard ratio is not one number, and a log-rank plan can be far off. The restricted mean survival time (RMST) to a horizon \(\tau\), the area under the survival curve up to \(\tau\), needs no such assumption, and its difference reads in time: “over five years, the new units run on average 0.8 years longer before failing”. rmst_design() takes the survival curve expected in each group (any SurPyval distribution, fitted or with given parameters, or a function of time, such as a piecewise exponential), the time over which units enter, the follow-up after the last enters and the dropout rate, and computes the variance of each group’s Kaplan-Meier RMST from them (not by simulation):

\[\sigma_j^2 = \int_0^\tau \frac{\left(\int_t^\tau S_j(u)\,du\right)^2} {S_j(t)^2\, G(t)}\, dF_j(t),\]

with \(G\) the chance of still being observed at \(t\). The test planned is surpyval.rmst_diff()’s.

import numpy as np
from surpyval.test_planning import rmst_design

def piecewise(t):
    # hazard 0.15 for two years, 0.25 after
    t = np.asarray(t, dtype=float)
    return np.exp(-np.where(t < 2, 0.15 * t, 0.3 + 0.25 * (t - 2)))

curves = (surpyval.Weibull.from_params([8.0, 1.3]), piecewise)
study = dict(accrual_time=2.0, follow_up=4.0, dropout_rate=0.05)
n = rmst_design(curves, 5.0, power=0.8, **study).n
print(n, round(rmst_design(curves, 5.0, n=n, **study).power, 3))

(84, 84) 0.8

Some units never fail. When a fraction of the units is cured, immune or free of the defect, each group’s survival levels off at its cured fraction, \(S(t) = \pi + (1 - \pi) S_u(t)\), and the hazard ratio drifts towards 1 as the uncured fail. Schoenfeld’s count for one hazard ratio is then wrong, by an amount that depends on the follow-up. cure_logrank_design() uses the mean and variance of the log-rank statistic under the two mixture-cure curves (Schoenfeld 1981), over the study’s entry, follow-up and dropout. A difference in the cured fraction, in the latency (latency_hazard_ratio or a pair of latency distributions), or both, may be given:

from surpyval.test_planning import cure_logrank_design

latency = surpyval.Weibull.from_params([2.0, 1.2])
study = dict(accrual_time=1.0, follow_up=3.0)
plan = cure_logrank_design((0.45, 0.30), latency, power=0.8, **study)
print(plan.n, plan.events)

(247, 247) 265

A difference only in the cured fraction shows late, when the uncured have failed: with half a year of follow-up instead of three, the same plan needs several times the units.

Group-sequential designs

A comparison that runs for years is usually looked at along the way: if the difference is already clear it can stop early, and if it is clearly not coming it can stop for futility. Each extra look is another chance of a false finding, so a group-sequential design holds each look to a stricter level, chosen so that the chance of a false finding over all the looks is still the level asked for. The price is a few more events at most (the inflation factor); the gain is fewer on average, because the study often stops early.

To detect a 30% lower hazard with 90% power at the one-sided 2.5% level, one analysis needs 331 events. Looking after a third, two thirds and all of the events, with O’Brien-Fleming-type boundaries (very strict early, close to the single analysis’s level at the end) and a non-binding futility boundary, needs at most 353, but about 272 on average if the difference is there and 207 if there is none:

from surpyval.test_planning import logrank_group_sequential

design = logrank_group_sequential(
    0.7, 3, 0.9, alternative="one-sided", alpha_ci=0.025,
    futility="hwang-shih-decani", futility_parameter=-2,
)

At each look the log-rank statistic (signed so that large values favour the new design) is compared with the boundaries: at or above the efficacy boundary the study stops with the difference shown; at or below the futility boundary it may stop without it. The “HR at bound” column is the observed hazard ratio that is on the efficacy boundary.

The events rarely arrive exactly as planned. With a spending function (the default) the boundaries are re-computed for the events the looks actually came at, and the level is still kept. Here the second look came at 230 events with a log-rank statistic of 2.6, past its boundary, so the study stops; the estimate of the hazard ratio allows for having stopped early (stopping when the difference looks large makes the plain estimate too optimistic):

actual = design.update([120, 230, 349])
print(actual.decide(2, 2.6))
print(round(actual.p_value(2, 2.6), 4))
estimate = actual.hazard_ratio_estimate(2, 2.6)
print({k: round(v, 3) for k, v in estimate.items() if k != "repeated"})

The p-value and the median-unbiased estimate with its confidence interval use the stage-wise ordering (an earlier stop is a stronger result); the repeated confidence interval (estimate["repeated"]) holds at any look, whether or not the study stops there.

The boundaries can be O’Brien-Fleming’s, Pocock’s, Wang and Tsiatis’ or Haybittle and Peto’s classical shapes, or the Lan-DeMets spending functions (O’Brien-Fleming-type, Pocock-type, Hwang-Shih-DeCani, Kim-DeMets); the futility boundary spends the type II error with one of the spending functions, binding or not. group_sequential_design() gives the same designs for any normal test statistic (a difference of means or proportions, a Cox coefficient), on the scale of the information, and boundary_crossing() the chances of crossing any boundaries. They are computed by recursive numerical integration (Jennison and Turnbull 2000, chapter 19), not simulated, and agree with R’s gsDesign and rpact to about 1e-6.

Which sequential test? Wald’s sequential tests above (sequential_mtbf_test(), sequential_demonstration_test()) look after every failure or unit and decide between a target and a good value of one MTBF or reliability: they suit a demonstration test watched continuously. A group-sequential design looks a few planned times at a normal test statistic: it suits a comparison of groups analysed in batches, such as a log-rank test at interim analyses.
