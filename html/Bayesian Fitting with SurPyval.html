Bayesian Fitting with SurPyval

With plenty of failures, the data say what a life distribution is. With three failures they say much less, but the engineer, the clinician or the manufacturer often knows more than the data show: “wear-out, a Weibull shape of about 2”, “a B10 of around 5,000 hours”, “it is the same design as last year’s fleet”. A Bayesian fit puts that knowledge, the prior, together with the data, by Bayes’ rule, and gives back a model that is used like any other SurPyval model.

Weibull.fit_bayes(x, c, priors=...) takes the data exactly as Weibull.fit does (any censoring, truncation, counts and intervals) and returns a BayesianParametric:

  • its sf, ff, df, hf, Hf, qf, mean and random describe the life of the next unit, with the uncertainty about the parameters averaged in (the posterior predictive);

  • cri, quantile_cri, mean_cri and param_cri say how sure we are: credible intervals, read as “there is a 95% chance the B10 lies between these two values, given the prior and the data”.

A credible interval is not a confidence interval. A confidence interval is a statement about the method (95% of such intervals contain the truth); a credible interval is a statement about this parameter, given what was believed and what was seen. A Bayesian model’s cb refuses and points to cri.

Few failures and a belief about the shape

Forty pump seals: three have failed, at 2,100, 3,400 and 4,050 hours, and 37 are still running. The seals wear out, and from the manufacturer’s data and experience the Weibull shape is “about 2, somewhere between 1.5 and 3”.

import numpy as np
import surpyval as sp

x = np.r_[2100, 3400, 4050, np.linspace(1500, 6000, 37)]
c = np.r_[0, 0, 0, np.ones(37)]

mle = sp.Weibull.fit(x, c)
print(mle.param_cb("beta").round(2))

[0.99 6.5 ]

With three failures, the maximum-likelihood fit’s 95% confidence interval on the shape runs from about 1 to 6.5: the data alone can hardly tell early failures from wear-out.

Say what is believed, and fit:

belief = {"beta": sp.prior.around(2, between=(1.5, 3))}
model = sp.Weibull.fit_bayes(x, c, priors=belief, unit="hours")
model

The summary reads from the top: the data, the priors as they were built (“about 2, 90% between 1.5 and 3” is a LogNormal whose 5% and 95% points are 1.5 and 3), the posterior’s medians and 95% credible intervals, the B10 life and the mean life with theirs, and what the prior did: it narrowed the shape’s interval by about 70% against the maximum-likelihood interval, while the scale, which was given no prior, came from the data alone. The last line says how it was computed (below).

The model answers the usual questions. The chance that a new seal survives 5,000 hours, with the 95% credible interval on that reliability:

print(model.sf(5000).round(3), model.cri(5000).round(3))

0.887 [0.744 0.975]

The B10 life comes in two forms, and they answer different questions:

print(model.qf(0.1).round(0))               # 10% of new seals fail by
print(model.quantile_cri(0.1, point=True).round(0))  # our best B10
print(model.quantile_cri(0.1).round(0))    # its 95% credible interval

4698.0
4919.0
[ 3015. 10423.]

qf(0.1) is the age by which 10% of new seals will have failed, all things considered: the uncertainty in the parameters is averaged in, which spreads the distribution and pulls its early quantiles in. It is what a spares forecast or a reliability block diagram needs. quantile_cri is about the population’s B10 itself: its posterior median, the single best guess, and the interval it lies in. With few failures the two differ (here about 4,700 against 4,900 hours).

model.plot() draws the data on Weibull paper with the predictive curve, its credible band and, dashed, the maximum-likelihood fit, so what the prior did is visible at a glance; model.plot_posterior() draws each parameter’s prior and posterior, and their joint contours.

ax = model.plot()

fig = model.plot_posterior()

A belief about a B-life

People seldom think in shapes and scales. “Our B10 is around 5,000 hours; we’d be surprised if it were under 3,000 or over 8,000. And it wears out.” prior.on_quantities takes the belief as said, one statement for each free parameter:

belief = sp.prior.on_quantities(
    sp.Weibull,
    B10=sp.prior.around(5000, between=(3000, 8000)),
    beta=sp.prior.between(1.2, 4),
)
sp.prior.check(sp.Weibull, belief)

What the prior says before any data (Weibull)

  B10              4,899   2,999 - 7,999

prior.check shows what the prior says before any data, in the terms people use. Here its B10 is between 3,000 and 8,000 hours with 90% probability, as said, and so the prior is exactly the belief: SurPyval carries the density of the B10 and the shape to the Weibull’s parameters by the change of variables, with nothing approximated. Run it on any prior: a “vague” prior often says something nobody believes (a LogNormal with a spread of 10 on alpha puts B10 anywhere from a nanosecond to geological time).

sp.Weibull.fit_bayes(x, c, priors=belief, unit="hours")

The same works with the reliability at a time (R_at={2000: sp.prior.between(0.9, 0.99)}), with the mean or median, or with any quantile (quantile={0.5: ...}).

Last year’s fleet as the prior for a new design

Last year’s fleet of 60 units gave a good Weibull fit. This year’s units are a new design, so far with two failures among 20:

last_year = sp.Weibull.fit(sp.Weibull.random(60, 9000, 1.8,
                                             random_state=2025))
x_new = np.r_[3900, 6100, np.linspace(1000, 5000, 18)]
c_new = np.r_[0, 0, np.ones(18)]
print(sp.Weibull.fit(x_new, c_new).param_cb("beta").round(1))

[ 3.6 19.6]

Two failures say little: the maximum-likelihood shape’s interval runs from about 3.6 to 20. The new design is similar, but not the same, so the old fleet is counted as half as much evidence:

prior = sp.prior.from_model(last_year, weight=0.5)
new = sp.Weibull.fit_bayes(x_new, c_new, priors=prior)
new

Weibull: Bayesian fit, 20 items (2 failed, 18 running)
Prior:      from an earlier Weibull fit (alpha 10,620, beta 1.995), weight 0.5
Posterior       median   95% credible interval
  alpha         10,720   8,986 - 12,800
  beta           2.098   1.632 - 2.692
B10 life         3,662   2,502 - 5,077
Mean life        9,511   7,979 - 11,360
Compared with the maximum-likelihood fit: alpha 5,919 (95% CI 4,957-7,068); beta 8.357 (95% CI 3.569-19.57);
the prior moved alpha outside the maximum-likelihood interval, the prior moved beta outside the maximum-likelihood interval.
Computed exactly on two offset 69 x 76 grids (integration error < 0.09%).

from_model takes the old fit’s estimates and their covariance (how sure they were and how they move together), so a fit from 60 failures counts for a lot and one from five for little; weight discounts it (0.5 counts it as half the evidence) and widen loosens it further; and param="beta" takes only one parameter’s prior from it. The weight is a judgement: try two and compare. Where the old data are at hand, prior.from_data(x_old, c_old, weight=0.5) uses their likelihood itself (a power prior), which is exact, and better with few old failures. A model known only by its numbers (a handbook, a data sheet) is prior.from_params(sp.Weibull, [9000, 1.8], within=0.2): each within about 20%.

Is the new design’s B10 longer than the old one’s?

new.compare_to(last_year, on="qf", p=0.10)

There is a 60.1% chance that this model's 10% quantile (B10 life) is greater than the other's.
Assumption: the other model's estimate taken as normal on the log scale with its delta-method standard error (its large-sample posterior under a flat prior), independent of this posterior.

The answer is a probability, with the assumption it rests on.

As data arrive

update adds data to a Bayesian fit. It keeps the original prior and all the data, so updating in steps gives exactly the posterior of fitting everything at once:

first = sp.Weibull.fit_bayes(x[:20], c[:20], priors={
    "beta": sp.prior.around(2, between=(1.5, 3))})
both = first.update(x[20:], c[20:])
print(np.allclose(both.params, model.params))

True

Units that were running at the last update and are still running (or have since failed) are the same units: pass the current records with replace=True, so that no unit counts twice.

Comparing fits: WAIC and leave-one-out

Which belief predicts the seals better: wear-out (the shape about 2), random failures (an Exponential), or early failures (a shape below 1)? AIC compares maximum-likelihood fits and ignores the priors. For Bayesian fits the question is how well each predicts a seal it has not seen, averaged over its posterior: the expected log predictive density (elpd), estimated by leaving out each unit in turn (loo()) or by WAIC (waic()). Higher is better; ic is -2 elpd, on AIC’s scale.

random = sp.Exponential.fit_bayes(
    x, c, priors={"failure_rate": sp.prior.between(1e-6, 1e-3)})
early = sp.Weibull.fit_bayes(
    x, c, priors={"beta": sp.prior.around(0.8, between=(0.5, 1.2))})
comparison = sp.bayes.compare(
    {"wear-out": model, "random": random, "early": early})
comparison

Comparison by LOO
                elpd       se       p  elpd_diff  se_diff
wear-out      -35.54    18.23    1.28       0.00      nan
random        -36.44    18.69    0.99      -0.90     0.73
early         -37.09    19.13    1.31      -1.54     1.11

Wear-out comes first, but by less than one and a half standard errors of the difference from random failures: three failures cannot tell the two apart. Read se_diff, not se: each fit’s own standard error is large (the three failures and 37 survivors predict very differently), but the fits are scored on the same seals, and the paired difference cancels most of it.

Every row counts through its own likelihood term (a survivor’s probability of surviving, an interval’s probability, a truncated row’s term over the probability of its window), and a row with count n is n units. Each fit is scored from its one posterior, without refitting, by \(p(y_i \mid y_{-i}) = 1 / E[1 / p(y_i \mid \theta)]\):

  • a conjugate fit in closed form, exactly;

  • a grid or Laplace fit by the grid’s quadrature. edge is each row’s share of its leave-one-out posterior in the grid’s outermost cells, and loo() warns above 0.001: without that row the data say much less, and the grid, laid for the full posterior, may not hold it;

  • draws from another sampler (from_draws) by Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (Vehtari, Gelman and Gabry, 2017; Vehtari, Simpson, Gelman, Yao and Gabry, 2024), as R’s loo and ArviZ do, taking the draws as independent. pareto_k is each row’s diagnostic, and loo() warns, counting the rows, above \(\min(1 - 1/\log_{10} S, 0.7)\) for \(S\) draws.

WAIC is \(\mathrm{lppd} - p_\mathrm{WAIC}\), with \(p_\mathrm{WAIC}\) the posterior variance of each row’s log-likelihood; it warns when a row’s term is above 0.4, where it is unreliable and loo() is the better estimate. The standard errors are \(\sqrt{N}\) times the standard deviation of the units’ values (with divisor \(N - 1\), as R’s loo).

Priors: the helpers, and their dangers

A prior on a parameter is any SurPyval distribution (sp.Gamma.from_params([2, 1]), sp.Beta.from_params([...]) for a proportion) or one of the helpers:

  • prior.around(m, between=(a, b), mass=0.9): “about m, almost surely between a and b”; a LogNormal for a positive parameter, a logit-normal for a proportion, a Normal on the real line;

  • prior.between(a, b): “somewhere between a and b”;

  • prior.uniform(a, b): “anything from a to b equally”, which is not “no opinion”: it rules out everything else, and it is not uniform on any other scale;

  • prior.flat(): no opinion, flat on the log scale for a positive parameter. It is the default for a parameter given no prior, and it is allowed only where the data determine the parameter (old data given as a prior with prior.from_data or as_prior() count with the new): with no failures, a flat prior on the scale is refused, with what to do instead, and so is a fit in which every parameter is flat where the maximum-likelihood fit has no verified maximum;

  • prior.fixed(v): the value is known, as fixed= in fit.

A prior that puts mass where the parameter cannot be (a Normal on a shape, which must be positive) is refused. Where the data contradict a prior, the fit warns, with the prior’s interval and the posterior median, and takes neither side: check both.

The formulas

Bayes’ rule: the posterior density of the parameters \(\theta\) given the data \(D\) is

\[p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{p(\theta) \, L(\theta; D)}{\int p(\theta) \, L(\theta; D) \, d\theta},\]

where \(p(\theta)\) is the prior and \(L\) the likelihood: the same one fit maximises, a product of \(f(x)\) for each failure, \(S(x)\) for each unit still running, \(F(x)\) for each left censored unit, \(F(r) - F(l)\) for each interval, each divided by \(S(t_l) - S(t_r)\) for a truncated one. The denominator is the evidence (model.log_evidence, for Bayes factors, where every prior is proper).

The predictive survival function averages the parameters out,

\[S_{\text{pred}}(t) = \int S(t \mid \theta) \, p(\theta \mid D) \, d\theta,\]

and is a proper distribution: \(F = 1 - S\), \(f = -S'\), \(h = f / S\), and qf inverts ff. The credible interval on \(S(t)\) is the 2.5% and 97.5% points of \(S(t \mid \theta)\) over the posterior.

How it is computed. For the textbook cases the posterior is in closed form: an Exponential with a Gamma(\(a, b\)) prior on its rate and data that are exact or right censored (with any late entry) has the Gamma(\(a + r, b + T\)) posterior, \(r\) the failures and \(T\) the total time on test, and a Lomax predictive; a Normal’s mean with its spread known, and a Bernoulli with a Beta prior, are the others. Otherwise, for one to three free parameters, SurPyval integrates on a grid: on the parameters’ unconstrained scale (logs of positive parameters, logits of proportions), along the posterior’s own axes at its peak, widened until the density at every edge is below \(10^{-8}\) of the peak, and laid twice, offset by half a cell. Half the difference between the two grids’ summaries is the integration error the summary reports; it must be below 0.1% of the 95% interval’s width (0.5% with three free parameters), or the grid is refined, and past its budget the fit refuses with the numbers. No random numbers are involved: the same data and prior give the same answer on every machine. method="laplace" is a normal approximation at the peak instead, labelled approximate.

Very small and very large probabilities. A chance of failure of one in a million million is as much a number as one of a half, and it is where a safety case or a rare adverse event lives. The predictive ff is therefore the posterior average of each parameter value’s own failure function, \(F_{\text{pred}}(t) = \sum_i w_i F(t \mid \theta_i)\), and not \(1 - S_{\text{pred}}(t)\): a double next to 1 holds about sixteen digits, so the subtraction would leave eight digits of a probability of \(10^{-8}\) and none of one of \(10^{-16}\). sf is the same average of the survival functions, so it keeps its digits far past the data (whichever of the two is larger is one minus the other, so they always sum to 1); Hf is \(-\log(1 - F)\) (as log1p) while less than half have failed and \(-\log S\) after, and stays finite where sf itself underflows. The credible intervals from cri are built the same way, and qf solves on the log of ff up to the median and on the log of sf above it, so model.qf(1e-12) and model.qf(1 - 1e-12) are both found (a limited failure population’s quantile is infinite above the proportion that ever fails).

Many points: a table. A grid of two or three free parameters has tens of thousands of points, and each value of the predictive is a sum over all of them, too slow for a calculation that asks for tens of thousands of values (an optimal replacement age, an integral). Where the posterior has more than 2,000 points, the first predictive value builds a table: the predictive cumulative hazard \(H(t)\) is summed exactly at a few hundred times and read between them by a cubic in \(\log H\) against \(\log t\) (against \(t\) for a distribution on the whole line), with the exact slope at each time, so sf, ff, Hf, hf and df come from one curve and stay a proper distribution. The times are placed adaptively: each stretch is checked against the full sum at two points and split until sf and ff (relative to itself, so a small failure probability keeps its digits) are within \(3 \times 10^{-7}\) and hf within ten times that. A stretch that cannot be brought within that (near the posterior points’ own offsets, say), and the far tails (where the points have a failure or survival probability below \(10^{-10}\)), use the full sum, so nothing is extrapolated: sf, ff and Hf are within \(10^{-6}\) of the full sum, relative, and hf and df within \(10^{-5}\). Building it takes under a second for 37,500 points; after it a value takes microseconds. Set model.predictive_table = False for the full sum everywhere.

Random lives. model.random(size, random_state) takes one uniform number per life. With a closed-form predictive the life is its quantile at that number. Otherwise the number picks a parameter value by where it falls among the cumulative weights \(w_i\), and its place within that value’s share, rescaled to (0, 1), is the probability at which that value’s own quantile function gives the life. The draws follow the distribution that sf, ff and qf describe, and the same seed gives the same lives.

The posterior itself is model.posterior: draws(k, random_state), to_dataframe(), density(points) (exact for the grid), and marginal(name); model.draw_models(k) gives ordinary models, one per draw. from_draws() builds a Bayesian model from another sampler’s draws, and surpyval.bayes.log_likelihood_fn() gives SurPyval’s likelihood, at many parameter points at once, to any sampler.

References: Gelman et al., Bayesian Data Analysis (3rd ed., 2013); Hamada, Wilson, Reese and Martz, Bayesian Reliability (2008); Meeker, Escobar and Pascual, Statistical Methods for Reliability Data (2nd ed., 2021), chapter 16; Ibrahim and Chen, “Power prior distributions for regression models” (2000); Talts et al., “Validating Bayesian inference algorithms with simulation-based calibration” (2018).
